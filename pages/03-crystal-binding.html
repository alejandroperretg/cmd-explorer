Crystal binding

What holds a crystal together: van der Waals forces in noble-gas solids, Coulomb attraction in ionic crystals, shared electron pairs in covalent crystals, delocalised electrons in metals and hydrogen bridges. Each mechanism gives a binding curve U(R) whose minimum fixes the lattice spacing and the cohesive energy. Its curvature returns as the elastic constants (1.5) and the phonon spring constants (1.6).

Overview

A crystal is stable because its energy is lower than that of the same atoms far apart. The difference is the cohesive energy. Topic 1.4 covers the five ways solids achieve this: van der Waals forces between closed-shell atoms (1.4.1), Coulomb attraction between ions (1.4.2), shared electron pairs (1.4.3), delocalised electrons (1.4.4) and hydrogen bridges (1.4.5). The two quantitative models are the Lennard-Jones crystal and the Madelung sum. All five sheet problems use them.

Key results to leave with:

  • Lennard-Jones fcc crystal: $R_0=1.09\,\sigma$ and $U_0=-8.61\,N\varepsilon$; bcc/fcc ≈ 0.957.
  • Madelung constant $\alpha=\sum_j(\pm)R/r_j$, with $\alpha=2\ln2$ for the 1D chain and 1.7476 for NaCl.
  • With repulsion $A/R^n$: $U(R_0)=-\frac{\alpha q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R_0}\big(1-\frac1n\big)$ per ion pair.
  • Cohesive energy is measured from neutral atoms, so ionisation energies and electron affinities enter.

Concepts

1.4.1Molecular crystals: noble gases

Where you meet this. Argon fills double-glazed windows and shields welds, and it is a gas at room temperature for a reason you can now compute: its whole binding is 0.080 eV per atom, about three times $k_BT$ at 300 K. Solid argon only exists below 84 K.

What this covers. What cohesive energy is and what a binding curve U(R) tells you. Then why closed-shell atoms such as Ar attract ($-1/R^6$) and repel (Pauli), the Lennard-Jones potential, lattice sums and the equilibrium crystal, and quantum corrections. Not in the notes (p17 is blank), so taught from Kittel ch. 3.

Why it matters. Sheets 3.3 (Ar bcc vs fcc) and 3.4 (solid H₂) are Lennard-Jones calculations, and the exam-style exercise below is too. The same pair potential is the standard model for elasticity (1.5) and phonons (1.6).

1.4.1 · Cohesive energy (all bond types)

Cohesive energy and the binding curve

The cohesive energy is the energy needed to take a crystal at $T=0$ apart into separated, neutral atoms at rest:

Cohesive energy (positive for a bound crystal)
$$\begin{aligned}E_{\text{coh}}&=E(\text{free neutral atoms})\\&\quad-E(\text{crystal}).\end{aligned}$$ (1)

For ionic crystals Kittel also uses the lattice energy, which is measured from separated ions. The two differ by the energy needed to make the ions from neutral atoms (§1.4.2). If the energy comes from pair interactions $\phi(r)$, each pair must be counted once:

Pair-sum energy of N identical atomsSheet 3.3
$$\begin{aligned}U&=\tfrac12\sum_i\sum_{j\neq i}\phi(r_{ij})\\&=\tfrac N2\sum_{j}{}'\,\phi(r_{j}).\end{aligned}$$ (2)

The primed sum runs over all atoms $j$ except the reference atom. The ½ corrects for counting every pair twice. As a function of the nearest-neighbour distance $R$, $U(R)$ tells you four things at once:

  • where its minimum sits: the equilibrium spacing $R_0$, and hence the lattice constant;
  • its depth: $-E_{\text{coh}}$;
  • its curvature at $R_0$: the bulk modulus and the spring constants (1.5, 1.6);
  • its asymmetry: thermal expansion, because the curve is steeper on the compressed side.

Mixing up cohesive and lattice energy. For NaCl the lattice energy (from Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions) is 7.9 eV. The cohesive energy (from Na and Cl atoms) is only 6.4 eV. Sheet 4.2 asks for the cohesive energy.

NaCl: lattice energy 7.9 eV, Na ionisation energy 5.1 eV, Cl electron affinity 3.6 eV. Cohesive energy per NaCl unit?

Start from the ions and bring the electron back: Cl⁻ → Cl costs 3.6 eV, and Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na releases 5.1 eV. So $E_{\text{coh}}=7.9-5.1+3.6=6.4$ eV (Kittel Fig. 8).

Cohesive energy is measured from neutral atoms, lattice energy from ions. One curve U(R) gives the lattice constant (position of its minimum), the binding (depth) and the stiffness (curvature).

1.4.1 · Van der Waals and Pauli

Noble gases: attraction and repulsion

1.4.1 Cohesive energy

Attraction. A noble-gas atom has no permanent dipole, but its electron cloud fluctuates. Kittel models each atom as a charge on a spring (force constant $C$, frequency $\omega_0=\sqrt{C/m}$). The two oscillators couple through their dipole–dipole energy, $-2e^2x_1x_2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3)$. This splits the two normal-mode frequencies, and to second order their summed zero-point energy $\frac12\hbar(\omega_s+\omega_a)$ drops:

Van der Waals–London attraction (Kittel eq. 9, SI)
$$\begin{aligned}\Delta U&=-\frac{\hbar\omega_0}{8}\left(\frac{2e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0CR^3}\right)^2\\&=-\frac{A}{R^6}.\end{aligned}$$ (3)

The effect is quantum: $\Delta U\to0$ as $\hbar\to0$. It also needs no overlap of the two charge clouds. Repulsion. When the clouds do overlap, the Pauli principle pushes electrons into higher states, so the energy rises steeply. The repulsion is modelled empirically as $B/R^{12}$. Together they give

Lennard-Jones pair potentialSheet 3.3Sheet 3.4
$$\phi(r)=4\varepsilon\left[\Big(\frac\sigma r\Big)^{12}-\Big(\frac\sigma r\Big)^{6}\right].$$ (4)

$\phi(\sigma)=0$. The minimum lies at $r=2^{1/6}\sigma=1.122\,\sigma$ with depth $-\varepsilon$. For Ar (Kittel Table 4), $\varepsilon=167\times10^{-16}$ erg $=1.67\times10^{-21}$ J $=10.4$ meV (1 erg = 10⁻⁷ J, 1 eV = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ J) and $\sigma=3.40$ Å. Both are fitted to the gas phase, not to the solid.

Thinking van der Waals binding needs permanent dipoles. Noble-gas atoms have none. The attraction comes from correlated fluctuations, i.e. an induced dipole in the field of a fluctuating one.

A dipole field falls as 1/R³. Why does the attraction fall as 1/R⁶ rather than 1/R³?

The mean dipole is zero, so the first-order dipole–dipole energy averages away. The surviving term is second order in the coupling, $\propto(1/R^3)^2=1/R^6$, as in (3).

Noble gases bind through fluctuating-dipole attraction ($-1/R^6$) balanced by Pauli repulsion ($+1/R^{12}$): the Lennard-Jones potential (4), with well depth ε at $1.12\sigma$.

For Ar the prediction is $R_0=3.71$ Å (observed 3.76 Å) and 89.7 meV per atom (observed 80 meV, Kittel Table 4).

Keeping only the 12 nearest neighbours. That sets $A_{12}=A_6=12$ and gives $U_0=-6N\varepsilon$, which is 30% too weak, because the $r^{-6}$ tail of the further shells is not small ($A_6=14.45$, not 12). Use the full lattice sums.

Without computing: which is more stable in the Lennard-Jones model, fcc or bcc, and by roughly how much?

fcc, because it has more close neighbours (12 vs 8). From (6), $\frac{U_{\text{bcc}}}{U_{\text{fcc}}}=\frac{12.2533^2/9.11418}{14.45392^2/12.13188}=0.957$, so fcc binds about 4% more strongly. That is sheet 3.3.

Lattice sums turn the pair potential into a crystal energy (5). Minimising it gives $R_0=1.09\sigma$ and $U_0=-8.61N\varepsilon$ for fcc, the same for every element. fcc beats bcc by a factor 1/0.957.

1.4.1 · Zero-point motion

Quantum corrections for light atoms

1.4.1 Lattice sums

Sheet 3.4

Equation (6) treats the atoms as classical particles at rest. A real atom confined to a cage of size $L$ by its neighbours has zero-point kinetic energy. Kittel's estimate takes half a de Broglie wavelength across the cage, $\lambda=2L$:

Zero-point kinetic energy of a confined atom (order of magnitude)
$$\begin{aligned}E_{\text{zp}}&\sim\frac{p^2}{2M}=\frac{h^2}{2M\lambda^2}\\&=\frac{h^2}{8ML^2}\;\propto\;\frac1M.\end{aligned}$$ (7)

This energy is gained back by expanding the lattice, so light atoms bind more weakly and sit further apart. Kittel gives the reduction from (6) as 28, 10, 6 and 4 % for Ne, Ar, Kr and Xe. The observed $R_0/\sigma$ is 1.14, 1.11, 1.10 and 1.09, against the classical 1.09. Even the isotope matters: ²⁰Ne has a larger lattice constant (4.4644 Å) than ²²Ne (4.4559 Å). For H₂ ($M=2$ u) the classical value is 2.59 kJ/mol but the observed value is 0.751 kJ/mol, which is sheet 3.4's closing note.

Expecting gas-phase Lennard-Jones parameters to predict a light-molecule solid. They describe the potential correctly. What (6) leaves out is the zero-point motion, and for H₂ and He that motion dominates.

Why is the quantum correction 28% for Ne but only 4% for Xe?

By (7), $E_{\text{zp}}\propto1/M$. Xe is about 6.5 times heavier than Ne, and it also has a deeper well (ε is 6.4 times larger), so the relative correction is much smaller.

Zero-point kinetic energy ∝ 1/M weakens binding and expands the lattice. It is small for Xe and Kr, about 10% for Ar, and huge for H₂, which is why sheet 3.4's two numbers disagree with experiment.

You can now compute the cohesive energy and lattice spacing of any Lennard-Jones solid from ε and σ, compare fcc with bcc, and say why light atoms miss. That is Sheet 3.3, Sheet 3.4 and the krypton exercise.

Where you meet this. Table salt melts at 801 °C because pulling one Na⁺Cl⁻ pair out of the crystal costs 7.9 eV, about 760 kJ/mol. Magnesium oxide, with doubly charged ions, melts near 2850 °C and lines steel furnaces. The z² in the Madelung energy is the reason, and it is exactly what sheet 4.2 tests.

1.4.2 · Madelung energy

Ionic crystals: the Madelung energy

1.4.1 Cohesive energy; NaCl and CsCl structures on topic 01

The ions have closed shells (Na⁺ like Ne, Cl⁻ like Ar) and are nearly spherical, so each acts like a point charge $\pm q$. Coulomb energy is the main part of the binding; van der Waals adds only 1–2%. For a reference ion, the Coulomb energy with all the others is $-\alpha q^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R)$, with

Madelung constant (Kittel eqs. 21 and 25)Sheet 4.1Sheet 4.2
$$\begin{aligned}\alpha&=\sum_j{}'\,\frac{(\pm)}{p_j}\\&=\sum_j{}'\,(\pm)\,\frac{R}{r_j}.\end{aligned}$$ (8)

The sign is (+) for ions of opposite charge to the reference ion and (−) for ions of like charge. $R$ is the nearest-neighbour distance. For the 1D chain of alternating ions, a reference ion has two ions at each distance $nR$, of alternating sign:

1D chain (Kittel ch. 3, Fig. 9)Sheet 4.1 (i)
$$\begin{aligned}\alpha&=2\Big(1-\frac12+\frac13-\frac14+\dots\Big)\\&=2\ln2=1.386,\end{aligned}$$ (9)

using $\ln(1+x)=x-x^2/2+x^3/3-\dots$ at $x=1$. In 3D, Kittel quotes:

Convergence. The terms fall only as $1/r$ while the number of ions in a shell grows as $r^2$. The sum converges only because + and − nearly cancel, and the result depends on the order of summation. Adding whole spherical shells gives values that swing between about −6 and +6. Adding charge-neutral cubes, with ions on a face weighted ½, on an edge ¼ and on a corner ⅛ (Evjen's method), converges fast. Model 2 shows both.

Tutor board 07 differs from Kittel: it writes $\alpha=\sum r_{NN}/(2|\mathbf R|)$, with the same $q_1q_2$ for every ion. That is wrong in two ways.
  • The sign must alternate with the charge of ion $j$. Without it the sum diverges.
  • The ½ belongs to the double counting of the total energy, eq. (10) below, not to α. With the ½, sheet 4.1 would give ln 2 instead of 2 ln 2.
The board's own table quotes Kittel's values, which follow from (8). The board also calls ½Σφ the "cohesive energy". For an ionic crystal that sum is the lattice energy (relative to ions), not the cohesive energy that sheet 4.2 asks for (relative to atoms).

Writing α with a ½ or without the alternating sign (tutor board 07). Use (8): no ½, (+) for opposite charges. Then the 1D chain gives 2 ln 2 = 1.386 and NaCl gives 1.7476.

Why must α be positive for a stable ionic crystal?

The Coulomb energy per ion is $-\alpha q^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0R$. Only if α > 0 do the attractions to unlike neighbours outweigh the repulsions from like ones, so that bringing the ions together lowers the energy.

In the 1D chain, which terms of the sum come from like charges?

The even terms $-\frac12,-\frac14,\dots$. The ions at $2R, 4R,\dots$ carry the same charge as the reference ion, so they repel.

The Coulomb energy per ion pair is $-\alpha q^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0R$, with α from (8): alternating signs and no ½. α = 2 ln 2 for the 1D chain and 1.7476 for NaCl. The sum converges only conditionally, so it must be added in neutral blocks.

1.4.2 · Equilibrium and energy cycle

Repulsion, equilibrium and the energy cycle

1.4.2 Madelung energy

Closed-shell ions repel at contact, as noble-gas atoms do. Kittel uses an exponential repulsion $\lambda e^{-r/\rho}$ between nearest neighbours. Sheet 4.1 uses a power law $A/R^n$. For $2N$ ions (N ion pairs), with $z$ nearest neighbours each:

Total energy of an ionic crystal (Kittel eq. 20, SI)Sheet 4.1 (ii)
$$\begin{aligned}\frac{U_{\text{tot}}}{N}&=z\lambda e^{-R/\rho}-\frac{\alpha q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R}\\ \text{or}\quad\frac{U_{\text{tot}}}{N}&=\frac{zA}{R^n}-\frac{\alpha q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R}.\end{aligned}$$ (10)

The prefactor is $N$, not $2N$, because each pair is counted once. Setting $dU/dR=0$ at $R_0$ for the power law gives $zA/R_0^n=\alpha q^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 nR_0)$. Substituting back eliminates $A$:

Energy at equilibrium, per ion pairSheet 4.1 (ii)
$$U(R_0)=-\frac{\alpha q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R_0}\Big(1-\frac1n\Big).$$ (11)

With Kittel's exponential repulsion the bracket is $(1-\rho/R_0)$ instead (Kittel eq. 24). Repulsion removes only a fraction $1/n$ (or $\rho/R_0\approx0.1$) of the Madelung energy, so neglecting it, as sheet 4.2 does, costs about 10%. A useful number: $e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0=1.440$ eV·nm. For NaCl ($R_0=0.282$ nm, ρ = 0.321 Å; Kittel Table 7), (11) gives $1.7476\times5.106\,\text{eV}\times(1-0.114)=7.91$ eV, against a measured lattice energy of 7.92 eV (182.6 kcal/mol).

To get the cohesive energy, close the cycle back to neutral atoms. For charge $z$ ions $M^{z+}X^{z-}$:

Cohesive energy from neutral atoms (Born–Haber cycle)Sheet 4.2
$$\begin{aligned}E_{\text{coh}}&=E_{\text{latt}}-\textstyle\sum_k I_k\\&\quad+\textstyle\sum_k A_k,\\ E_{\text{latt}}&=\frac{\alpha z^2e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R_0}\\&\quad\times\big(1-1/n\big).\end{aligned}$$ (12)

$E_{\text{latt}}$ is the lattice energy (from ions), and both sums run over $k=1,\dots,z$. $I_k$ are the successive ionisation energies of the metal. $A_k$ are the successive electron affinities, i.e. the energy released when the $k$-th electron is added. The Madelung term grows as $z^2$, which can outweigh the extra ionisation cost (Model 3).

The sign of the second electron affinity. For O, $A_2=-9.0$ eV: adding the second electron costs 9.0 eV, so in (12) it subtracts 9.0 eV. Only the crystal's Madelung field makes O²⁻ stable.

Why does (10) have the prefactor N and not 2N for a crystal of 2N ions?

Summing the energy of every ion with all the others counts each pair twice. Multiplying the per-ion energy by $2N$ and halving gives $N$.

n = 9: what fraction of the Madelung energy survives at equilibrium?

$1-1/9=8/9\approx89\%$. Repulsion takes the other 11%.

At equilibrium the repulsion takes back only 1/n of the Madelung energy, giving (11). The cohesive energy then subtracts ionisation energies and adds electron affinities (12). Mind that the second affinity of O is negative.

You can now compute a Madelung constant by summing in neutral blocks, eliminate the repulsion to get the energy at equilibrium, and run a Born–Haber cycle with the right signs. That is Sheet 4.1 and Sheet 4.2.

1.4.3 · Electron-pair bond

Covalent crystals

1.4.1 Cohesive energy; the diamond structure on topic 01

A covalent bond is a pair of electrons, one from each atom, with antiparallel spins, concentrated between the two nuclei. The spin matters through the Pauli principle, not through magnetic forces. In H₂ the spin singlet (antiparallel) puts charge between the protons and binds, while the triplet (parallel) keeps it out and repels (Kittel Fig. 12). This spin-dependent Coulomb energy is the exchange interaction. In the simplest picture, two equal orbitals with energy $\varepsilon_0$ and coupling $\gamma$ mix into

Bonding and antibonding levels (two-orbital model, overlap neglected)
$$\begin{aligned}\varepsilon_\pm&=\varepsilon_0\mp|\gamma|,\\ \psi_\pm&=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\phi_A\pm\phi_B).\end{aligned}$$ (13)

Two electrons with opposite spins fill the bonding level and gain $2|\gamma|$. A third and fourth electron would have to enter the antibonding level, cancelling the gain. That is why filled shells do not bond covalently: Cl₂ (one hole in 3p) has a 2 Å bond, while Ar atoms (full 3p) stay 3.76 Å apart.

C, Si and Ge each lack four electrons of a full shell. Carbon is promoted from $2s^22p^2$ to $2s\,2p^3$ (cost ≈ 4 eV, more than regained) and forms four $sp^3$ bonds at the tetrahedral angle 109.5°. The result is the diamond structure, with only 4 neighbours. Its packing fraction is low:

Packing fraction, diamond vs close-packed
$$\begin{aligned}f_{\text{diamond}}&=\frac{\pi\sqrt3}{16}=0.34,\\ f_{\text{fcc}}&=\frac{\pi}{3\sqrt2}=0.74.\end{aligned}$$ (14)

Bonds can also be partly ionic. Phillips' fractional ionic character (Kittel Table 8) runs from Si 0.00 through GaAs 0.31 and ZnS 0.62 to NaCl 0.94.

Saying covalent bonding is driven by a magnetic spin–spin force. It is electrostatic. The spins matter only because the Pauli principle changes where the charge can sit.

Diamond fills only 34% of space. Why doesn't it collapse into a close-packed structure?

The bonds are directional: the $sp^3$ orbitals point to four tetrahedral neighbours. Packing 12 neighbours closer would break these strong bonds, which costs more than it gains.

Covalent bonds are spin-paired electron pairs filling a bonding level. They are strong and directional, and they produce open structures such as diamond (packing 0.34). Ionic and covalent bonding are two ends of a continuous range.

You can now explain why covalent bonds need antiparallel spins, why they make open structures such as diamond (packing 0.34), and where they sit between ionic and covalent. That sets up the semiconductors of topic 12.

1.4.4Metallic crystals

Where you meet this. A copper wire bends where a salt crystal would shatter. The electron "glue" of a metal has no preferred direction, so atomic planes can slide past each other without breaking bonds. The same glue is weak in the alkali metals: sodium melts at 98 °C, while NaCl holds until 801 °C.

What this covers. Why delocalised valence electrons bind a metal, with the uncertainty-principle estimate of sheet 4.3. The notes have only "metals → easy model: free e⁻ gas" (p18).

Why it matters. Sheet 4.3 asks for this estimate. It is also the first step towards the free-electron gas (topic 07, exam 2022 Q2).

1.4.4 · Kinetic-energy lowering

Metallic crystals

1.4.1 Cohesive energy

In a metal the valence electrons leave their atoms and spread over the crystal as conduction electrons, usually one or two per atom. According to Kittel, the defining feature of metallic binding is that these electrons have lower energy in the metal than in the free atom. The main reason is that their kinetic energy drops. With $\Delta p\,\Delta x=\hbar/2$ and $E\approx\Delta p^2/2m$:

Confinement (kinetic) energySheet 4.3
$$E_{\text{kin}}\approx\frac{(\Delta p)^2}{2m}=\frac{\hbar^2}{8m\,(\Delta x)^2}.$$ (15)

For $\Delta x=a_0$ this is $\frac18\cdot\frac{\hbar^2}{ma_0^2}=\frac{27.2}{8}$ eV $=3.40$ eV. At $\Delta x=9a_0$ it is 81 times smaller. The binding is weak because the large spacings that lower the kinetic energy also take the electron away from its ion. Alkali metals therefore have large lattice constants and small cohesive energies. Metals prefer close-packed structures (fcc, hcp, bcc). Transition metals gain extra binding from their d shells.

Reading (15) as the cohesive energy. It is only the kinetic part. The potential energy rises as the electron leaves its ion, so the true binding is smaller. The factor $\hbar/2$ is also only an order-of-magnitude choice: the real kinetic energy of the H 1s electron is 13.6 eV, not 3.4 eV.

The electron's region doubles in size. What happens to its confinement energy?

By (15), $E\propto1/\Delta x^2$, so it drops to a quarter.

Metals are bound mainly because delocalisation lowers the valence electrons' kinetic energy, $E\sim\hbar^2/8m\Delta x^2$. Going from 1 to 9 Bohr radii gains about 3.4 eV (sheet 4.3). The free-electron gas makes this quantitative.

You can now estimate the kinetic energy a valence electron gives up when it delocalises, and say why that is not the cohesive energy. That is Sheet 4.3, and the free-electron gas of topic 07 takes it from there.

1.4.5Hydrogen bonding

Where you meet this. Ice floats because hydrogen bonds lock water molecules into an open tetrahedral network, about 8% less dense than the liquid (0.917 g/cm³). The same bonds are why water boils at 100 °C while the heavier H₂S boils at −60 °C.

What this covers. How a hydrogen atom can bridge two atoms although it has only one electron, and how strong that bridge is. This is the part of 1.4 the notes do cover (p18).

Why it matters. No sheet problem uses it, but the notes list it, and it explains water and ice, some ferroelectric crystals and the pairing of DNA bases.

1.4.5 · Proton bridge

Hydrogen bonding

1.4.2 Ionic crystals

With one electron, neutral H can form a covalent bond with only one atom. Next to a strongly electronegative atom (F, O, N), though, it gives up most of its electron. The bare proton is tiny, so two anions can approach it closely but a third cannot. It therefore bridges exactly two atoms. The classic example (notes p18, Kittel Fig. 13) is the bifluoride ion HF₂⁻, i.e. F⁻·H⁺·F⁻. The bond is largely ionic in character and weak:

Typical hydrogen-bond energy
$$\begin{aligned}E_{\text{H}}&\approx0.1\ \text{eV}=9.6\ \text{kJ/mol}\\&\approx k_B\times1160\ \text{K}.\end{aligned}$$ (16)

That is stronger than van der Waals (Ar: 0.08 eV per atom, shared among 12 neighbours) but far weaker than covalent or ionic bonds. It is also only a few times $k_BT$ at room temperature (25.9 meV). Hydrogen bonds therefore break and re-form easily, which matters for liquid water and for the unzipping of DNA.

Counting a hydrogen bond as a full covalent bond. H forms one covalent bond. The second link is a weak, mostly electrostatic bridge of about 0.1 eV.

Why can a hydrogen bond join two atoms but not three?

The proton is so small that the two anions it bridges already touch it. There is no room for a third close neighbour.

The hydrogen bond is a proton bridge between two electronegative atoms (F, O, N). It is mostly ionic and about 0.1 eV per bond, only a few $k_BT$ at room temperature.

You can now explain how a one-electron hydrogen bridges two electronegative atoms, give its strength (about 0.1 eV) and compare it with $k_BT$. No sheet problem uses it, but it is the part of 1.4 the notes cover.

Cohesive energy of bcc and fcc Argon

Calculate the ratio of cohesive energies of argon in a bcc and an fcc lattice, using the Lennard-Jones potential.

Method

Write the crystal energy with lattice sums (5), minimise with respect to R for each structure to get (6), and take the ratio. ε and σ cancel, so the answer holds for any Lennard-Jones solid. This is Kittel ch. 3 Problem 2, which asks the same question for neon.

Assumption: the lattice sums are not on the sheet. fcc: 12.13188 and 14.45392 (Kittel eq. 12). bcc: 9.11418 and 12.2533 (Kittel ch. 3 Problem 2). Both were re-summed by brute force in node (1.4.1). Atoms are classical and at rest, and each structure is at its own equilibrium R₀.
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. $U(R)=2N\varepsilon\big[A_{12}(\sigma/R)^{12}-A_6(\sigma/R)^6\big]$, from (5).
  2. $dU/dR=0$: $-12A_{12}\sigma^{12}/R^{13}+6A_6\sigma^6/R^7=0\Rightarrow(\sigma/R_0)^6=A_6/2A_{12}$.
  3. Substitute: $U_0=2N\varepsilon\Big[A_{12}\frac{A_6^2}{4A_{12}^2}-A_6\frac{A_6}{2A_{12}}\Big]=-\frac{A_6^2}{2A_{12}}N\varepsilon$.
  4. fcc: $\frac{14.45392^2}{2\times12.13188}=8.610$. bcc: $\frac{12.2533^2}{2\times9.11418}=8.237$.
  5. $\dfrac{U_0^{\text{bcc}}}{U_0^{\text{fcc}}}=\dfrac{8.237}{8.610}=0.957$.
  6. For Ar ($\varepsilon=1.67\times10^{-21}$ J, $\sigma=3.40$ Å): fcc $R_0=1.090\sigma=3.71$ Å, 89.7 meV/atom; bcc $R_0=1.068\sigma=3.63$ Å, 85.8 meV/atom. fcc is lower by 3.9 meV per atom.
Result: $E_{\text{coh}}^{\text{bcc}}/E_{\text{coh}}^{\text{fcc}}=0.957$. fcc is more stable, as observed for solid Ar.
Sanity check: node gives 0.95663. Kittel's answer for neon is 0.958, the same ratio rounded from 8.24/8.61, as it must be since ε and σ cancel. hcp/fcc = 1.0001, so the Lennard-Jones model barely tells the two close-packed structures apart. Model 1 shows the ratio for every material.

  • B: (i) is meant as the observed value, and the note refers to the gap between calculation and experiment.

Sheet 4 · Problem 1 · 6 pts

Madelung constant and cohesive energy

(i) Compute the Madelung constant for a 1-dimensional chain of NaCl. (ii) With equilibrium distance R₀ between the ions and a repulsive potential energy term of the form A/Rⁿ, what is the cohesive energy per unit cell in equilibrium, expressed by R₀?

Method

(i) Sum (8) along the chain: alternating signs, two ions at each distance. (ii) Write $U(R)$ from (10), set $dU/dR=0$ to eliminate $A$, and evaluate at $R_0$. This is close to Kittel ch. 3 Problem 5(a).

Assumptions: the repulsion $A/R^n$ acts between nearest neighbours only (as in Kittel Problem 5). Each ion has $z=2$ of them. The result does not depend on how $A$ is distributed, since $A$ is eliminated. The unit cell of the chain has length $2R_0$ and holds one Na⁺ and one Cl⁻. "Cohesive energy" here is relative to separated ions, since the sheet gives no ionisation data. Relative to neutral atoms, subtract $I(\text{Na})-A(\text{Cl})$ as in (12).
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. Reference ion at 0. Ions at $\pm R,\pm3R,\dots$ have opposite charge (+), and ions at $\pm2R,\pm4R,\dots$ the same charge (−).
  2. $\alpha=\sum_j(\pm)R/r_j=2\big(1-\frac12+\frac13-\frac14+\dots\big)=2\ln2=1.386$.
  3. N unit cells: $U(R)=N\Big(\dfrac{2A}{R^n}-\dfrac{\alpha q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R}\Big)$, with each pair counted once.
  4. $\dfrac{dU}{dR}\Big|_{R_0}=0\Rightarrow\dfrac{2nA}{R_0^{n+1}}=\dfrac{\alpha q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R_0^2}\Rightarrow\dfrac{2A}{R_0^n}=\dfrac{\alpha q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,nR_0}$.
  5. Substitute: $U(R_0)/N=-\dfrac{\alpha q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R_0}\Big(1-\dfrac1n\Big)$.
Result: (i) $\alpha=2\ln2\approx1.386$. (ii) $E_{\text{coh}}=-U(R_0)/N=\dfrac{2\ln2\,e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R_0}\Big(1-\dfrac1n\Big)$ per unit cell (one ion pair), with $q=e$.
Sanity check:
  • Kittel Problem 5(a) gives $-\frac{2Nq^2\ln2}{R_0}(1-\frac1n)$ (CGS) for 2N ions, i.e. N cells, which is the same result.
  • $n\to\infty$ (a hard wall) leaves the pure Madelung energy.
  • Partial sums in node: 1.3763 after 100 terms and 1.3858 after 2000, against 2 ln 2 = 1.3863 (Model 2).
  • For a feel: $R_0=0.282$ nm and $n=9$ give 6.29 eV, below the 3D NaCl value because α is smaller in 1D.
  • Writing α with board 07's extra ½ would give ln 2 = 0.693, half the correct value.
Sheet 4 · Problem 2 · 6 pts

Oxidation state in ionic crystals

Barium oxide has the NaCl structure. Estimate the cohesive energies per molecule of the hypothetical crystals Ba⁺O⁻ and Ba⁺⁺O⁻⁻ with respect to separated neutral atoms.

  • Observed nearest-neighbour distance $R_0=0.276$ nm.
  • Ionisation potentials of Ba: 5.19 eV (first) and 9.96 eV (second).
  • Electron affinities of O for the first and second added electron: 1.5 eV and −9.0 eV. Each affinity is the energy released in O + e⁻ → O⁻ and O⁻ + e⁻ → O⁻⁻ respectively.

Which valence state do you predict? Assume $R_0$ is the same for both forms, and neglect the repulsive energy.

Method

Use the Born–Haber cycle (12) with $n\to\infty$ (no repulsion). The Madelung energy for charges $\pm ze$ is $z^2$ times the singly charged one. Subtract the ionisation energies and add the affinities. This is Kittel ch. 3 Problem 7, with the same data.

Assumption: α(NaCl) = 1.747565 (Kittel ch. 3, not on the sheet). Board 07's α (with ½ and no signs) must not be used here. Its lattice-energy sum would also leave out the ionisation and affinity terms the sheet asks for.
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. $\dfrac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R_0}=\dfrac{1.440\ \text{eV nm}}{0.276\ \text{nm}}=5.217$ eV.
  2. Madelung energy: $z=1$: $1.747565\times5.217=9.12$ eV. $z=2$: $4\times9.12=36.47$ eV.
  3. Cost of making Ba⁺O⁻ from atoms: $I_1-A_1=5.19-1.5=3.69$ eV.
  4. Cost of making Ba²⁺O²⁻: $(I_1+I_2)-(A_1+A_2)=15.15-(1.5-9.0)=15.15+7.5=22.65$ eV.
  5. $E_{\text{coh}}(\text{Ba}^+\text{O}^-)=9.12-3.69=5.43$ eV. $E_{\text{coh}}(\text{Ba}^{2+}\text{O}^{2-})=36.47-22.65=13.82$ eV.
Result: Ba⁺O⁻: ≈ 5.4 eV per molecule. Ba²⁺O²⁻: ≈ 13.8 eV per molecule. The divalent state Ba²⁺O²⁻ is predicted, as observed.
Sanity check:
  • node gives 5.428 and 13.82 eV. Model 3 reproduces both with the repulsion switched off.
  • Going from z = 1 to 2, the extra cost (9.96 + 9.0 = 18.96 eV) is less than the extra Madelung gain (3 × 9.12 = 27.35 eV), so z = 2 must win.
  • Neglecting repulsion overestimates both values by about 1/n ≈ 10%. For NaCl, the same method with repulsion reproduces the measured lattice energy to 0.1% (1.4.2).
Sheet 4 · Problem 3 · 4 pts

Metallic bonds

Reducing the kinetic energy of valence electrons stabilises metals. In a simple picture, letting the wave function spread out reduces the momentum uncertainty Δp, via Δp·Δx = ħ/2. If an electron has the kinetic energy that belongs to Δp, how much energy is gained per electron when it is released from atomic dimensions (1 Bohr radius) to unit-cell dimensions (about 9 Bohr radii)?

Method

Use (15), $E=\Delta p^2/2m=\hbar^2/(8m\Delta x^2)$, at $\Delta x=a_0$ and $9a_0$, and take the difference. It helps that $\hbar^2/(ma_0^2)$ is one hartree, 27.21 eV.

Assumption: as the sheet says, one direction only and Δx equal to the size of the region. In 3D, with the same Δx in each direction, every energy triples (Model 4).
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. $\Delta p=\hbar/(2\Delta x)$, so $E=\dfrac{\hbar^2}{8m\Delta x^2}$.
  2. $\Delta x=a_0$: $E_1=\dfrac18\dfrac{\hbar^2}{ma_0^2}=\dfrac{27.21}{8}$ eV $=3.40$ eV.
  3. $\Delta x=9a_0$: $E_9=E_1/81=0.042$ eV.
  4. Gain $\Delta E=E_1-E_9=E_1\big(1-\frac1{81}\big)=3.36$ eV.
Result: about 3.4 eV per electron. Almost all of $E_1$ is released.
Sanity check:
  • node with CODATA constants: $\hbar^2/(ma_0^2)=27.2114$ eV, $E_1=3.4014$ eV, gain 3.359 eV.
  • The order of magnitude is right. The exact kinetic energy of the H 1s electron is 13.6 eV (virial theorem), so the ħ/2 estimate is low by a factor 4.
  • The gain is not the cohesive energy. Moving away from its ion raises the electron's potential energy, so real metallic binding is smaller (1.4.4).

Solid krypton

Krypton crystallises in the fcc structure. Its gas-phase Lennard-Jones parameters are ε = 2.25·10⁻²¹ J and σ = 0.365 nm.

  1. Starting from $U=2N\varepsilon[A_{12}(\sigma/R)^{12}-A_6(\sigma/R)^6]$ with $A_{12}=12.13$ and $A_6=14.45$, derive the equilibrium nearest-neighbour distance and the cohesive energy per atom.
  2. Evaluate both for Kr, and give the cubic lattice constant.
  3. The measured values are 0.401 nm and 0.116 eV per atom. Explain the difference.
  4. Estimate the bulk modulus from the curvature of U at $R_0$.
Data source: ε = 225×10⁻¹⁶ erg and σ = 3.65 Å from Kittel Table 4, converted to SI. The measured values and the 6% quantum correction are also from Kittel.
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. Exam-relevant derivation $dU/dR=0\Rightarrow12A_{12}\sigma^{12}R^{-13}=6A_6\sigma^6R^{-7}\Rightarrow R_0=\sigma(2A_{12}/A_6)^{1/6}=1.090\,\sigma$. Substituting, $U_0/N=-\varepsilon A_6^2/(2A_{12})=-8.61\,\varepsilon$.
  2. $R_0=1.090\times0.365=0.398$ nm, and the fcc cube edge is $a=\sqrt2R_0=0.563$ nm. $E_{\text{coh}}=8.61\times2.25\times10^{-21}$ J $=1.94\times10^{-20}$ J $=0.121$ eV per atom (11.7 kJ/mol).
  3. The calculation treats atoms as classical points at rest. Zero-point motion (∝ 1/M) lowers the binding by about 6% for Kr (Kittel) and pushes the atoms apart: $0.121\times0.94=0.114$ eV, close to the measured 0.116 eV, and $R_0$ grows towards 0.401 nm. The gas-phase fit of ε and σ is a second source of error.
  4. For fcc the volume per atom is $v=R^3/\sqrt2$. At the minimum ($U'=0$), $B=v\,\dfrac{d^2u}{dv^2}=\dfrac{\sqrt2}{9R_0}\,\dfrac{d^2u}{dR^2}\Big|_{R_0}$, with $u=U/N$. Evaluating gives $B\approx75\,\varepsilon/\sigma^3=75\times2.25\times10^{-21}/(0.365\times10^{-9})^3=3.5$ GPa.
Result: $R_0=0.398$ nm, $a=0.563$ nm; $E_{\text{coh}}=0.121$ eV per atom (0.114 eV after the 6% quantum correction; measured 0.116 eV); $B\approx3.5$ GPa.
Sanity check:
  • Kittel Fig. 2 gives a = 5.64 Å for Kr at 4 K, against our 5.63 Å.
  • The bulk modulus was computed twice in node: by numerical second derivative (3.48 GPa, i.e. coefficient 75.2) and by the formula 75ε/σ³ (3.47 GPa).
  • The same formula gives 3.2 GPa for Ar. Noble-gas solids are about 7 times softer than NaCl (24 GPa, Kittel Table 7), as their shallow binding curve suggests.
