Overview

The free electron gas of Topic 07 is in equilibrium: the Fermi sphere sits at the origin and carries no current. Transport asks what happens when a force pushes the electrons and scattering pulls them back. The Boltzmann equation tracks the occupation $f(\vec r,\vec k,t)$. In the relaxation-time approximation it gives a Fermi sphere shifted by $\delta\vec k=-e\tau\vec E/\hbar$, and therefore $\sigma=ne^2\tau/m$.

Results to leave with: $\sigma=ne^2\tau/m$ and Ohm's law; $\sigma(\omega)=\sigma_0/(1+i\omega\tau)$ (sheet 9.2); Matthiessen's rule $\rho=\rho_{ph}(T)+\rho_i$; why Umklapp scattering matters for resistance; $\kappa/\sigma=LT$ with $L=\pi^2k_B^2/3e^2$; and the coupled charge and energy fluxes that define the thermopower $Q$ and the Peltier coefficient $\Pi=QT$.

Read alongside Kittel, Introduction to Solid State Physics 8th ed.: ch. 6 ("Electrical Conductivity and Ohm's Law", "Experimental Electrical Resistivity of Metals", "Umklapp Scattering", "Thermal Conductivity of Metals"), Appendix F ("Boltzmann Transport Equation") and ch. 8 ("Thermoelectric Effects"); lecture notes pp. 42–49. The Fermi sphere, $D(\varepsilon_F)$ and the Sommerfeld heat capacity are on Topic 07 and are only recapped here.

Concepts

2.2.1Boltzmann transport equation

Where you meet this. Every transistor in a phone chip is designed with simulation tools. Their drift–diffusion equations are averages of the Boltzmann equation, and for the smallest devices engineers solve the Boltzmann equation itself, electron by electron, with Monte Carlo methods.

What this covers. The occupation function $f(\vec r,\vec k,t)$ and the equation that balances drift, external force and collisions. Lecture notes p42.

Why it matters. Sheet 9.2 (i) starts from it. Every transport coefficient on this page (σ, κ, Q) is a solution of it.

2.2.1 · Drift, force, collisions

The occupation f and its balance

Recap from 2.1.3 and 2.1.6. In equilibrium the electrons fill a Fermi sphere of radius $k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}$, rounded at $T>0$ by the Fermi–Dirac function $f_0=1/(e^{(\varepsilon-\mu)/k_BT}+1)$. The sphere is symmetric, so it carries no current.

$f(\vec r,\vec k,t)$ is the occupation of the state $\vec k$ near position $\vec r$. It describes wave packets that are larger than a lattice cell but smaller than the mean free path. Without collisions, occupation is carried along the trajectories: an electron found at $(\vec r,\vec k)$ now was at $(\vec r-\vec v\,dt,\ \vec k-\vec F\,dt/\hbar)$ a time $dt$ ago. Collisions add or remove occupation at a fixed point. Expanding to first order in $dt$:

Boltzmann transport equationSheet 9.2
$$\begin{aligned}&\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\vec v\cdot\nabla_{\vec r}f+\frac{\vec F}{\hbar}\cdot\nabla_{\vec k}f\\&\qquad=\Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\Big)_{\!s}\end{aligned}$$ (1)

Here $\vec v=\nabla_{\vec k}\varepsilon/\hbar$ is the group velocity and $\vec F$ the external force, $(-e)\vec E$ for an electron in a field. The left side is drift and force; the right side, the scattering term, contains all the microscopic physics (transition rates $\propto|\langle\vec k'|H'|\vec k\rangle|^2$) and is hard to evaluate in general.

Writing $\vec F=+e\vec E$. The electron charge is $-e$, so the force, and the shift of the sphere, point against the field.

Which term of (1) vanishes in a homogeneous sample, and which in steady state?

Homogeneous: $\vec v\cdot\nabla_{\vec r}f=0$. Steady state: $\partial f/\partial t=0$. In a uniform static field only the force term and the collision term remain.

The Boltzmann equation says: change of f = drift in space + push in k-space by the force + collisions. Transport is the balance between the last two.

You can now write the Boltzmann equation and name each term, the starting line of Sheet 9.2 (i). The collision term is simplified next, 2.2.2 →.

2.2.2Relaxation-time approximation

Where you meet this. Switch off a current in copper and the electrons forget their drift within a few relaxation times, τ ≈ 25 fs: gone in well under a picosecond. Any delay you can measure when switching a circuit comes from its inductance and capacitance, never from the metal itself.

What this covers. Replacing the collision term by a single relaxation time τ, and what τ means. Lecture notes p43.

Why it matters. It makes the Boltzmann equation solvable in one line, and τ appears in every result that follows: σ, σ(ω), κ and the mean free path.

2.2.2 · One time τ

Relaxation toward equilibrium

2.2.1 · Drift, force, collisions

Assume that scattering drives $f$ back toward the local equilibrium $f_0=f_{FD}(\varepsilon(\vec k))$ at a rate proportional to the deviation:

Relaxation-time approximationSheet 9.2
$$\Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\Big)_{\!s}=-\,\frac{f-f_0}{\tau}$$ (2)

$1/\tau$ is the scattering rate and $\tau$ (s) the mean time between collisions. Switch off a field at $t=0$ in a homogeneous sample and (1) reduces to $\partial_tf=-(f-f_0)/\tau$ (lecture example, notes p43; Kittel App. F eq. 8):

Decay after the field is switched off
$$f(t)-f_0=\big[f(0)-f_0\big]\,e^{-t/\tau}$$ (3)

The deviation, and with it the current, decays exponentially (Model 2).

Notes slip: the handwritten line on notes p43 has no minus sign. The blackboard photo on the same page, the recap on p45 and Kittel App. F eq. 6 all have it. The minus is required: without it the deviation would grow instead of relaxing.

Do not confuse this τ with Kittel's τ = kBT in ch. 5 and App. F.

Dropping the minus sign in (2). Without it the deviation grows as $e^{+t/\tau}$.

After how many τ is the current down to 1 % of its initial value?

$e^{-t/\tau}=0.01$ gives $t=\ln100\,\tau=4.6\,\tau$, about 0.11 ps for copper.

All scattering is lumped into one time τ: deviations from equilibrium decay as $e^{-t/\tau}$.

You can now write the relaxation-time Boltzmann equation, the full first line of Sheet 9.2 (i), and solve it for a field switched off. Used for σ in 2.2.3 →.

2.2.3Electrical conductivity

Where you meet this. A kettle cable carrying 10 A through 1.5 mm² of copper moves its electrons along at about half a millimetre per second, while each of them individually races at 1.6 × 10⁶ m/s. Conduction is a tiny shift of a huge, fast Fermi sphere.

What this covers. The steady-state solution in a field (the shifted Fermi sphere), $\sigma=ne^2\tau/m$, and its AC extension $\sigma(\omega)$. Lecture notes p43–45; sheet 9.2.

Why it matters. $\sigma=ne^2\tau/m$ is the central result of the chapter, and sheet 9.2 asks for $\sigma(\omega)$. Its band version (only partly filled bands conduct) is in 2.6 →.

2.2.3 · Shifted Fermi sphere

σ = ne²τ/m

2.2.2 · One time τ

For a static, uniform field $\vec E$ the drift and time terms vanish. To first order, $f_0+\tau\frac{(-e)\vec E}{\hbar}\cdot\nabla_{\vec k}f_0=f_{st}$, which is the Taylor expansion of a shifted distribution:

Steady state: shifted sphereExam-relevant derivation
$$\begin{aligned}f_{st}(\vec k)&=f_0\Big(\vec k+\frac{e\tau\vec E}{\hbar}\Big)\\ \delta\vec k&=-\frac{e\tau\vec E}{\hbar}\end{aligned}$$ (4)

The whole Fermi sphere moves by $\delta\vec k$, against the field (the electron charge is $-e$). Only a thin shell changes occupation: states are emptied on one side and filled on the other (Model 1). Summing $(-e)\vec v_{\vec k}/V$ over the occupied states, with 2 for spin and $(2\pi)^3/V$ per $\vec k$ state, gives

Drude conductivitySheet 9.2Cu exercise
$$\begin{aligned}\vec j&=\frac{-e}{V}\,2\sum_{\vec k}\vec v_{\vec k}f_{st}\\&=\frac{ne^2\tau}{m}\vec E\\ \sigma&=\frac{ne^2\tau}{m},\qquad \rho=\frac1\sigma\end{aligned}$$ (5)

Units: $[\sigma]=\mathrm{A\,V^{-1}m^{-1}}=\mathrm{S/m}$, $[\rho]=\Omega\,\mathrm m$. Kittel reaches the same result from $\hbar\,d\vec k/dt=-e\vec E$ with the shift frozen at $t=\tau$ (eqs. 39–43). With the mobility $\mu=e\tau/m$, $\sigma=ne\mu$.

Only the Fermi surface contributes: the same integral gives $\sigma=\tfrac13e^2\tau v_F^2D(\varepsilon_F)$, with $D$ per unit volume. Inserting $D(\varepsilon_F)=3n/2\varepsilon_F$ from 2.1.3 and $\varepsilon_F=\tfrac12mv_F^2$ gives back $ne^2\tau/m$.

Notes slip: notes p44 writes $\vec j=n\,\frac{e\vec E}{m}\tau$, one factor $e$ short. The correct line is $\vec j=(ne)\,\frac{e\vec E}{m}\tau$: charge density × drift velocity. The board's "$\sigma=n\mu$" is missing the same $e$: $\sigma=ne\mu$ (Kittel ch. 8).

Thinking every electron is accelerated by the field. The whole sphere shifts, but only the states at its rim change occupation; deep electrons have nowhere to go.

Doubling τ: what happens to σ, the shift δk and the mean free path?

All three double: $\sigma\propto\tau$, $\delta k\propto\tau$, $\ell=v_F\tau\propto\tau$.

A field shifts the Fermi sphere by $e\tau E/\hbar$ against itself; the imbalance at the rim is the current, $\sigma=ne^2\tau/m$.

2.2.3 · AC conductivity (sheet 9.2)

Conductivity at frequency ω

2.2.3 · Shifted Fermi sphere

The drift velocity obeys Newton's law with a friction term, $m(\frac{d}{dt}+\frac1\tau)\vec v=-e\vec E$. With $\vec E=\vec E_0e^{i\omega t}$, the sheet's convention:

AC conductivitySheet 9.2
$$\begin{aligned}\sigma(\omega)&=\frac{\sigma_0}{1+i\omega\tau}\\&=\sigma_0\,\frac{1-i\omega\tau}{1+\omega^2\tau^2}\\ \sigma_0&=\frac{ne^2\tau}{m}\end{aligned}$$ (6)

For $\omega\tau\ll1$ the current follows the field (Ohmic). For $\omega\tau\gg1$, $\sigma\approx-i\,ne^2/m\omega$: the electrons are freely accelerated between collisions, the current lags the field by 90° and no power is absorbed. The crossover $\omega\tau=1$ is at $f=1/2\pi\tau\approx6$ THz for Cu at room temperature (Model 3).

Sign convention: this page follows the sheet, with time dependence $e^{+i\omega t}$. Kittel ch. 6 Problem 6 uses $e^{-i\omega t}$ and writes $\sigma(\omega)=\sigma(0)\frac{1+i\omega\tau}{1+(\omega\tau)^2}=\frac{\sigma(0)}{1-i\omega\tau}$. The physics is the same; only the sign of $i$ flips (complex conjugate).

Mixing conventions: with $e^{-i\omega t}$ the sign of Im σ flips. State the convention before writing σ(ω).

At ωτ = 1, what are Re σ and Im σ in units of σ₀?

$\sigma/\sigma_0=(1-i)/2$: Re = 0.5, Im = −0.5 (sheet convention). The current lags by 45°.

σ(ω) is Drude with a lag: real and Ohmic below $1/\tau$, imaginary (inductive) above. For metals the crossover is in the terahertz.

You can now derive $\sigma=ne^2\tau/m$ from the shifted sphere and do Sheet 9.2 (i) and (ii). Used in 2.2.4 → and in the Wiedemann–Franz law (2.5 →).

2.2.4Ohm's law

Where you meet this. A 10 m run of 1.5 mm² copper house wiring has a resistance of 0.11 Ω and drops 1.1 V at 10 A. That number, ρL/A, is why heavy appliances need thicker cable: halve the area and both the voltage drop and the heating double.

What this covers. Ohm's law in its microscopic form $\vec j=\sigma\vec E$ and its macroscopic form $V=RI$, with real numbers for Cu, Al and Na. Lecture notes p45 and p49.

Why it matters. It turns $\sigma=ne^2\tau/m$ into measurable quantities, and inverting it is how τ and the mean free path are obtained from a resistivity table (Cu exercise).

2.2.4 · From j = σE to V = RI

Microscopic and macroscopic Ohm's law

2.2.3 · Shifted Fermi sphere

Microscopic: $\vec j=\sigma\vec E$, a local relation between current density and field. Macroscopic: a bar of length $L$ and cross-section $A$ with voltage $V$ has $E=V/L$ and $I=jA$, so

Ohm's lawCu exercise
$$\begin{aligned}\vec j&=\sigma\vec E\\ V&=RI,\qquad R=\rho\frac{L}{A}\end{aligned}$$ (7)

Typical size: copper at 295 K has $\rho=1.70\ \mu\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}=1.70\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\mathrm m$ (Kittel ch. 6 Table 3). With $n=8.45\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$ (Kittel Table 1), $\tau=m/ne^2\rho=2.5\times10^{-14}$ s and $\ell=v_F\tau=39$ nm. A current of 10⁶ A/m² drifts at only $j/ne=7.4\times10^{-5}$ m/s, about 10⁻¹⁰ of $v_F=1.57\times10^6$ m/s (Model 4).

Checked with node: m/(ne²ρ) = 2.47 × 10⁻¹⁴ s; v_Fτ = 38.8 nm; 10⁶/(ne) = 7.39 × 10⁻⁵ m/s.

Mixing μΩ cm and Ω m: 1 μΩ cm = 10⁻⁸ Ω m. Copper is 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m, not 1.7 × 10⁻⁶.

Aluminium, ρ = 2.74 μΩ cm: resistance of 1 m of wire with A = 1 mm²?

$R=2.74\times10^{-8}\cdot1/10^{-6}=0.0274\ \Omega$, 1.6 times copper's 0.017 Ω.

$R=\rho L/A$ is $\vec j=\sigma\vec E$ integrated over a wire. Inverting ρ gives τ ≈ 10⁻¹⁴ s and ℓ ≈ tens of nm at room temperature.

You can now go from a resistivity table to R, τ, ℓ and the drift speed, parts one and two of the Cu exercise. What sets ρ and its temperature dependence is next, 2.4.1 →.

2.4.1Conductivity of metals

Where you meet this. Copper for cryostats and superconducting magnets is specified by its residual-resistivity ratio, ρ(295 K)/ρ(4 K). At room temperature every grade conducts about the same; at 4 K a high-purity grade conducts hundreds of times better, because only impurities are left to scatter the electrons.

What this covers. What scatters electrons in a real metal (phonons and defects), Matthiessen's rule, and the temperature dependence of ρ. Lecture notes p46.

Why it matters. It explains the shape of every ρ(T) curve you will be asked to sketch, and it sets up Umklapp (2.4.2).

2.4.1 · Matthiessen's rule

Phonons plus defects

2.2.3 · Shifted Fermi sphere

Recap from 1.6.5 Phonons and 1.7.1. Lattice vibrations are quantised as phonons of wavevector $\vec q$. Their number grows ∝ T above the Debye temperature Θ_D and freezes out below it.

A perfect periodic lattice does not scatter Bloch electrons (2.6.1 →). Resistance comes from departures from periodicity: lattice vibrations (phonons) and static defects (impurities, vacancies). If the two scattering processes are independent, their rates add:

Matthiessen's rule
$$\begin{aligned}\frac1\tau&=\frac1{\tau_{ph}}+\frac1{\tau_i}\\ \Rightarrow\ \rho&=\rho_{ph}(T)+\rho_i\end{aligned}$$ (8)
  • $\rho_i$ (residual resistivity) is independent of $T$ and set by sample purity: about 1 μΩ cm per atomic percent of impurity (Kittel).
  • $T\gg\Theta_D$: the phonon number grows ∝ $T$, so $\rho_{ph}\propto T$.
  • $T\ll\Theta_D$: $\rho_{ph}\propto T^5$ (stated in the lecture; Kittel App. J). Fewer phonons, and those left have small $q$ and scatter only through small angles.

Kittel's pure-copper example: at 4 K, $\tau\approx2\times10^{-9}$ s and $\ell=v_F\tau\approx0.3$ cm, which is the notes' p46 number. The resistivity ratio $\rho(295\,\mathrm K)/\rho_i$ measures purity (Model 5).

Checked: 1.57 × 10⁶ m/s × 2 × 10⁻⁹ s = 3.1 mm. The implied ρ(4 K) = m/(ne²τ) = 2.1 × 10⁻¹³ Ω m is 8 × 10⁴ times below 295 K, matching Kittel's "nearly 10⁵".

Adding resistivities of independent processes is right; adding their τ's is not. Rates (1/τ) add.

A copper sample has ρ(295 K) = 1.72 μΩ cm and ρ(4 K) = 0.02 μΩ cm. What is its residual-resistivity ratio, and what is ρ_ph at 295 K?

RRR = 1.72/0.02 = 86. At 4 K the phonon part is negligible, so ρ_i = 0.02 and ρ_ph(295 K) = 1.70 μΩ cm.

ρ(T) = phonon part (∝ T⁵ low, ∝ T high) + a constant residual part from defects.

You can now sketch ρ(T) for a metal with its residual, T⁵ and T regions and read purity from it. Why large-angle phonon scattering matters most is next, 2.4.2 →.

2.4.2Umklapp processes

Where you meet this. Diamond is mounted under high-power laser diodes as a heat spreader because it conducts heat better than any metal. Its Debye temperature is 2230 K (Kittel ch. 5 Table 1), so at room temperature hardly any phonons are energetic enough for the Umklapp collisions that limit heat flow in other crystals. In metals the same mechanism sets the electrical resistance.

What this covers. Crystal-momentum conservation up to a reciprocal lattice vector, normal vs Umklapp scattering, and the minimum phonon an Umklapp process needs. Lecture notes p47, p49.

Why it matters. Umklapp is why phonons can relax a current at all, and the lecture and Kittel stress opposite temperature ends of it.

2.4.2 · Normal vs Umklapp

k′ = k + q + G

2.4.1 · Matthiessen's rule

Recap from 1.3.2 Reciprocal lattice. Wavevectors in a crystal are defined only up to a reciprocal lattice vector $\vec G$; the first Brillouin zone holds every distinct one. Phonon wavevectors $\vec q$ (1.6.5) live in the same zone.

In electron–phonon scattering crystal momentum is conserved only up to a reciprocal lattice vector:

Crystal-momentum conservation
$$\vec k'=\vec k+\vec q+\vec G$$ (9)

$\vec G=0$ is a normal process. A small phonon $\vec q$ turns $\vec k$ through a small angle and hardly reduces the current. With $\vec G\neq0$ (Umklapp, "flip-over"), $\vec k+\vec q$ leaves the zone and is folded back by $\vec G$. The electron ends up on the far side of the Fermi sphere, scattered through an angle near π, so it loses almost all its forward velocity. Umklapp is therefore much more effective at relaxing the current, and the heat current too.

If the Fermi sphere does not touch the zone face, Umklapp needs a phonon with at least $q_0=G-2k_F$ (the gap between neighbouring Fermi spheres; Model 6). The two sources stress opposite ends of the temperature scale:

  • Lecture (notes p47): Umklapp needs large $|\vec q|$, so it occurs at higher $T$, where such phonons are thermally excited. Since it is efficient, it is what hinders electrical and thermal conduction there.
  • Kittel ch. 6: Umklapp accounts for most of the electrical resistivity of metals at low temperatures. Low here means low compared with $\Theta_D$ but still above a characteristic $\theta_U$. Even there the few Umklapp events outweigh the many inefficient small-angle normal events. Only for $T\ll\theta_U$ does the supply of phonons with $q\ge q_0$ freeze out, as $e^{-\theta_U/T}$, leaving normal scattering alone. Potassium: $\theta_U=23$ K against $\Theta_D=91$ K, with Umklapp negligible below about 2 K.

Both statements are correct. Umklapp needs a minimum $q$ (the lecture's point), so it disappears at the very lowest $T$. Wherever those phonons exist, it dominates the resistance (Kittel's point).

Number check: Kittel quotes $q_0=0.267\,k_F$ for one electron per atom in bcc. The straight-line gap along the nearest zone face, $q_0=|\vec G_{110}|-2k_F$ with $k_Fa=(6\pi^2)^{1/3}$ and $|\vec G_{110}|a=2\sqrt2\pi$, gives $0.280\,k_F$ (node). Kittel gives no derivation; his 0.267 is reproduced by the same formula with Table 1's rounded $k_F=0.75\times10^8$ cm⁻¹ for K and $a\approx5.23$ Å (0.266–0.268), whereas the unrounded $k_F=0.746\times10^8$ cm⁻¹ gives 0.280. Model 6 uses the exact geometry. Its $\theta_U\approx\Theta_D\,q_0/q_D$ (a Debye phonon of wavevector $q_0$, $q_D$ for 2 atoms per $a^3$) is this page's estimate, not Kittel's: 20 K against the measured 23 K.

Saying Umklapp "violates momentum conservation". Crystal momentum is conserved modulo $\hbar\vec G$; the lattice as a whole takes up $\hbar\vec G$.

Why does a normal process with small q barely change the current?

The electron's velocity turns only through a small angle, so its component along the current is almost unchanged. Umklapp sends it to the opposite side of the sphere, reversing that component.

Umklapp ($\vec G\ne0$) back-scatters electrons and dominates the phonon resistance, but needs phonons with $q\ge q_0$, which freeze out below $\theta_U$.

You can now draw the two-sphere Umklapp picture, explain why Umklapp relaxes the current, and reconcile the lecture's and Kittel's temperature statements. The same scattering limits heat flow, 2.5 →.

2.5Wiedemann–Franz law

Where you meet this. Cryogenic engineers size the heat leak down a cryostat's wiring from a simple resistance measurement: the Wiedemann–Franz law turns each wire's electrical resistance into its thermal conductance, so a four-wire ohmmeter replaces a difficult thermal measurement.

What this covers. Fourier's law, the electronic thermal conductivity from kinetic theory, and the universal ratio κ/σT. Lecture notes p48.

Why it matters. It ties transport back to the Sommerfeld heat capacity of 2.1.6 and is a classic derivation (Cu exercise).

2.5 · κ/σ = LT

Thermal and electrical conductivity together

2.2.3 · Shifted Fermi sphere

Recap from 2.1.6 Specific heat. Only electrons within $k_BT$ of $\varepsilon_F$ take up heat: per unit volume $C_{el}=\frac{\pi^2}{2}\frac{k_BT}{\varepsilon_F}nk_B$.

The heat flux obeys Fourier's law, $\vec j_u=\kappa(-\nabla T)$. Kinetic theory gives $\kappa=\tfrac13Cv\ell$. For the electron gas, use $C_{el}$ with $v=v_F$, $\ell=v_F\tau$ and $\varepsilon_F=\tfrac12mv_F^2$:

Electronic thermal conductivityExam-relevant derivation
$$\kappa_{el}=\frac{\pi^2nk_B^2T\tau}{3m}$$ (10)
Wiedemann–Franz lawCu exercise
$$\frac{\kappa_{el}}{\sigma}=\frac{\pi^2}{3}\Big(\frac{k_B}{e}\Big)^2T=LT$$ (11)

$n$, $\tau$ and $m$ cancel because the same electrons carry the charge and the heat. The Lorenz number is $L=2.44\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{W\,\Omega\,K^{-2}}$ (node; Kittel prints 2.45). Kittel Table 5 gives measured values for Cu of 2.23 at 0 °C and 2.33 at 100 °C, in the same units.

Check for copper: $L\sigma T=2.44\times10^{-8}\times5.88\times10^{7}\times300=431$ W m⁻¹ K⁻¹. Kittel ch. 5 Table 1 measures 401 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹. The law fails at intermediate temperatures, where small-angle inelastic scattering degrades the heat current more than the charge current (Model 7).

Notes p48 gives the units of κ as J/(m K), which is missing a s⁻¹: κ is in W m⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Using the classical $C=\tfrac32nk_B$: it gives $L=\tfrac32(k_B/e)^2=1.11\times10^{-8}$, less than half the measured value.

Aluminium at 295 K, σ = 3.65 × 10⁷ S/m: estimate κ.

$\kappa=L\sigma T=2.44\times10^{-8}\times3.65\times10^7\times295=263$ W m⁻¹ K⁻¹. Kittel ch. 5 Table 1 lists 237 at 300 K, about 10 % below, as for Cu.

The same electrons carry charge and heat, so $\kappa/\sigma T$ is a universal constant, $L=\pi^2k_B^2/3e^2$.

You can now derive $L=\pi^2k_B^2/3e^2$ and estimate a metal's thermal conductivity from its resistivity, the last part of the Cu exercise. When charge and heat currents drive each other, see 2.7.7 →.

2.7.7Thermoelectric effects

Where you meet this. A thermocouple in an oven turns a temperature difference into a voltage, and a Peltier cooler in a camping fridge turns a current into a heat flow. Both are the coupling between charge and heat currents described here, and the sign of the voltage tells you whether a semiconductor is n- or p-type.

What this covers. The coupled charge and heat fluxes, the thermopower Q, the Peltier coefficient Π and the Kelvin relation Π = QT, with signs and sizes. Not in the notes yet: from Kittel ch. 8.

Why it matters. Exam 2022 Q3 asks for exactly these definitions. It also previews the semiconductor chapter (3.1).

2.7.7 · Coupled fluxes

Each flux responds to both forces

2.2.3 · Shifted Fermi sphere, 2.5 · κ/σ = LT

Not yet lectured

The notes give only the separate laws $\vec j=\sigma\vec E$ and $\vec j_u=\kappa(-\nabla T)$. In reality each flux responds to both driving forces. In linear response:

Coupled fluxesExam Q3a
$$\begin{aligned}\vec j&=L_{11}\vec E+L_{12}(-\nabla T)\\ \vec j_u&=L_{21}\vec E+L_{22}(-\nabla T)\end{aligned}$$ (12)

$\vec j$ is the electric current density (A/m²) and $\vec j_u$ the energy flux (W/m²), with energies measured from the chemical potential μ, so it is the heat flux. $L_{11}=\sigma$.

  • Thermopower Q (Seebeck): open circuit, $\vec j=0$. A temperature gradient then builds up a field $\vec E=Q\nabla T$, so $Q=L_{12}/L_{11}$ (V/K).
  • Peltier coefficient Π: uniform temperature, $\nabla T=0$. The current carries energy $\vec j_u=\Pi\vec j$, so $\Pi=L_{21}/L_{11}$ (V = J/C: energy carried per unit charge).
  • Kelvin relation: Onsager reciprocity gives $L_{21}=TL_{12}$, hence $\Pi=QT$.

Eliminating $\vec E$ gives the form to write in an exam:

Exam formExam Q3a–b
$$\begin{aligned}\vec j&=\sigma\big(\vec E-Q\nabla T\big)\\ \vec j_u&=\Pi\,\vec j-\kappa\nabla T\\ \kappa&=L_{22}-\frac{L_{12}L_{21}}{L_{11}}\end{aligned}$$ (13)

Answering Q3a with the uncoupled $\vec j=\sigma\vec E$ and $\vec j_u=-\kappa\nabla T$. The question is about the cross terms.

Open circuit: what is E in terms of Q and ∇T?

$\vec j=0$ in (13) gives $\vec E=Q\nabla T$.

Charge and heat currents each respond to E and ∇T; Q (open circuit) and Π (isothermal) measure the cross terms, linked by Π = QT.

2.7.7 · Sign and size

Sign and size of Π and Q

2.7.7 · Coupled fluxes

Not yet lectured

Kittel's drift picture for a non-degenerate semiconductor (ch. 8, eqs. 54–60): each electron carries the energy $(E_c-\mu)+\tfrac32k_BT$ above the Fermi level, and its energy flux runs opposite to its charge flux. This gives

Peltier coefficients, drift modelExam Q3bHot-probe exercise
$$\begin{aligned}\Pi_e&=-\frac{E_c-\mu+\tfrac32k_BT}{e}\\ \Pi_h&=+\frac{\mu-E_v+\tfrac32k_BT}{e}\\ Q&=\frac{\Pi}{T}\end{aligned}$$ (14)

So $\Pi_e<0$ for electrons and $\Pi_h>0$ for holes. The sign of $Q$ gives the sign of the carriers. Heat one end of an n-type sample: electrons diffuse to the cold end and charge it negatively. This is the quick hot-probe test for n vs p type (Kittel Fig. 23). Example: n-Si with $E_c-\mu=0.25$ eV at 300 K gives $\Pi_e=-0.289$ V and $Q=-0.96$ mV/K.

In a metal only electrons within $k_BT$ of $\varepsilon_F$ contribute, so $Q$ is smaller by about $k_BT/\varepsilon_F$. The free-electron (Mott) result is $Q=-\frac{\pi^2k_B^2T}{2e\varepsilon_F}$, or −1.57 μV/K for Cu at 300 K (Model 8).

Source: the metal formula is the standard Boltzmann result for a constant τ (Mott formula). Kittel ch. 8 does not give it; App. F has the method. Measured Cu has the opposite (positive) sign, a known failure of the free-electron model that band structure explains. No measured value is quoted here because none is in the course sources.

Checked with node: (0.25 + 1.5 × 0.025852) V = 0.2888 V, /300 K = 0.963 mV/K; π²k_B²(300 K)/(2e · 7.00 eV) = 1.570 μV/K.

Forgetting the sign: electrons give Q < 0 and Π < 0, holes Q > 0 and Π > 0. Q3 marks the link between sign and carrier type.

p-type sample, hot end heated: which end goes positive?

Holes diffuse to the cold end and charge it positively; the hot end is negative ($Q>0$, $V_{hot}-V_{cold}=-Q\Delta T<0$).

Semiconductors: $|Q|\sim(\Delta+\tfrac32k_BT)/eT$, about a mV/K, with the carrier's sign. Metals: μV/K, because only the Fermi-edge electrons carry excess energy.

Sheet 9 · Problem 2 · 6 pts

Frequency dependence of electrical conductivity

(i) For a metal modelled as a free electron gas with a time-dependent homogeneous force $\vec F(t)$ on each electron, show that the non-equilibrium distribution is $f_E(t,\vec k)=f_0\big(t-\tau,\ \vec k-\tau\frac{\vec F}{\hbar}\big)$, with τ the relaxation time. (ii) Scattering acts as a friction term, $m\big(\frac{d}{dt}+\frac1\tau\big)\vec v=\vec F$. Show with this ansatz that in a homogeneous field $\vec E(t)$ oscillating at angular frequency ω the AC conductivity is $\sigma(\omega)=\frac{\sigma_{DC}}{1+i\omega\tau}$.

Method

(i) Write the Boltzmann equation in the relaxation-time approximation for a homogeneous system ($\nabla_{\vec r}f=0$). Expand the proposed $f_E$ to first order in τ and check that it satisfies the equation to that order. (ii) Insert $\vec E=\vec E_0e^{i\omega t}$ into the equation of motion, solve for the steady-state drift velocity, and use $\vec j=-ne\vec v$.

Reading of the sheet: "show" in (i) is taken to mean "to first order in τ" (linear response), which is how the lecture derived the static shifted sphere on p43–44. The sheet's form of $\sigma(\omega)$ fixes the time dependence as $e^{+i\omega t}$. Kittel ch. 6 Problem 6 is the same exercise in the $e^{-i\omega t}$ convention.
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. Homogeneous system: $\partial_tf+\frac{\vec F}{\hbar}\cdot\nabla_{\vec k}f=-\frac{f-f_0}{\tau}$.
  2. Expand the ansatz to first order: $f_E\approx f_0-\tau\,\partial_tf_0-\tau\frac{\vec F}{\hbar}\cdot\nabla_{\vec k}f_0$. Hence $-\frac{f_E-f_0}{\tau}=\partial_tf_0+\frac{\vec F}{\hbar}\cdot\nabla_{\vec k}f_0$.
  3. Left side with $f=f_E$: $\partial_tf_E+\frac{\vec F}{\hbar}\cdot\nabla_{\vec k}f_E=\partial_tf_0+\frac{\vec F}{\hbar}\cdot\nabla_{\vec k}f_0+\mathcal O(\tau)$. The correction terms are τ times second derivatives (or $\dot{\vec F}$), which is second order in the perturbation. Both sides agree to first order, which proves (i).
  4. Interpretation: the distribution now is the equilibrium one from one relaxation time ago, displaced by the momentum $\tau\vec F$ gained since the last collision. For a static field $\vec F=-e\vec E$ it reduces to the lecture's shifted sphere $f_0(\vec k+e\tau\vec E/\hbar)$.
  5. (ii) Each electron's mean (drift) velocity obeys $m(\dot{\vec v}+\vec v/\tau)=-e\vec E_0e^{i\omega t}$. Try $\vec v=\vec v_0e^{i\omega t}$: $m\big(i\omega+\frac1\tau\big)\vec v_0=-e\vec E_0$, so $\vec v_0=-\frac{e\tau/m}{1+i\omega\tau}\vec E_0$.
  6. Current density: $\vec j=n(-e)\vec v=\frac{ne^2\tau/m}{1+i\omega\tau}\vec E$. With $\sigma_{DC}=ne^2\tau/m$ (2.2.3) this is $\sigma(\omega)=\frac{\sigma_{DC}}{1+i\omega\tau}$.
Result: $f_E(t,\vec k)=f_0(t-\tau,\vec k-\tau\vec F/\hbar)$ solves the relaxation-time Boltzmann equation to first order; $\sigma(\omega)=\sigma_{DC}/(1+i\omega\tau)=\sigma_{DC}(1-i\omega\tau)/(1+\omega^2\tau^2)$ for $e^{+i\omega t}$.
Sanity check: ω → 0 gives σDC. For ωτ ≫ 1, σ → −i ne²/(mω), independent of τ: free acceleration, current 90° behind the field. Replacing i by −i reproduces Kittel's eq. 62, σ(0)(1 + iωτ)/(1 + ω²τ²). For Cu, ωτ = 1 at f = 1/(2πτ) = 6.4 THz with τ = 2.47 × 10⁻¹⁴ s (node), so household and radio frequencies are fully in the DC limit.

2022 Q3 · Thermoelectric phenomena · not yet lectured — from Kittel

(a) Give expressions for the energy flux and the current density. (b) Define the Peltier coefficient and the absolute thermopower Q. The notes' separate laws $\vec j=\sigma\vec E$ and $\vec j_u=\kappa(-\nabla T)$ are not a full answer: Q3 asks for the coupled fluxes.

Show model answer
  1. (a) In linear response each flux depends on both forces:
    Coupled fluxes
    $$\begin{aligned}\vec j&=L_{11}\vec E+L_{12}(-\nabla T)\\ \vec j_u&=L_{21}\vec E+L_{22}(-\nabla T)\end{aligned}$$
    with $L_{11}=\sigma$ and energies measured from μ. Equivalent form: $\vec j=\sigma(\vec E-Q\nabla T)$, $\vec j_u=\Pi\vec j-\kappa\nabla T$. For a semiconductor with electrons (Kittel): $\vec j=ne\mu_e\vec E$ and $\vec j_u=n\big(E_c-\mu+\tfrac32k_BT\big)(-\mu_e)\vec E$, with mobility $\mu_e>0$.
  2. (b) Thermopower: in open circuit ($\vec j=0$) a temperature gradient produces $\vec E=Q\nabla T$, so $Q=L_{12}/L_{11}$, in V/K. Peltier coefficient: at uniform T the energy flux carried by a current is $\vec j_u=\Pi\vec j$, so $\Pi=L_{21}/L_{11}$, the energy carried per unit charge, in V.
  3. Kelvin relation: $\Pi=QT$ (Onsager, $L_{21}=TL_{12}$). Signs: electrons give $\Pi,Q<0$ ($\Pi_e=-(E_c-\mu+\tfrac32k_BT)/e$); holes give $\Pi,Q>0$.

Points are lost for writing only the uncoupled Ohm and Fourier laws, for sign errors, and for not linking the sign of Q to the carrier type.

Exam-style exercise · thermoelectrics

Hot-probe test on n-type silicon

An n-type Si bar at 300 K has its Fermi level 0.25 eV below the conduction band edge. (a) Find Π and Q in Kittel's drift model. (b) One end is heated by 10 K with no current flowing: which end becomes positive, and what voltage appears? (c) What heat flux does a current density of 10⁴ A/m² carry at uniform temperature?

Method

$\Pi_e=-(E_c-\mu+\tfrac32k_BT)/e$, then $Q=\Pi/T$ (Kelvin). Open circuit means $\vec E=Q\nabla T$; the Peltier heat flux is $j_u=\Pi j$.

Show worked solution
  1. $k_BT=25.85$ meV at 300 K, so $\tfrac32k_BT=38.8$ meV and $\Pi_e=-(0.250+0.0388)\ \mathrm{eV}/e=-0.289$ V.
  2. $Q=\Pi/T=-0.289/300=-0.96$ mV/K.
  3. $V_{hot}-V_{cold}=-Q\,\Delta T=+9.6$ mV: the hot end is positive, because electrons pile up at the cold end.
  4. $j_u=\Pi j=-0.289\ \mathrm V\times10^4\ \mathrm{A/m^2}=-2.9\times10^3\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$. The minus sign means heat flows opposite to $\vec j$, i.e. along the electron motion.
Result: Π = −0.29 V, Q = −0.96 mV/K, hot end +9.6 mV, Peltier heat flux 2.9 kW/m² against the current.
Sanity check: the same numbers come out of Model 8 at its default settings. The order of magnitude matches Kittel Fig. 23, where QT for n-Si is a few tenths of a volt. The magnitude is about 600 times the Cu free-electron value (1.57 μV/K), as expected from (Δ/k_BT)/(k_BT/ε_F)-type scaling.
Exam-style exercise · conduction in copper

Relaxation time, mean free path and thermal conductivity of Cu

Copper at 295 K: ρ = 1.70 μΩ cm, n = 8.45 × 10²⁸ m⁻³, vF = 1.57 × 10⁶ m/s (Kittel). Find τ, ℓ, the AC crossover frequency, and estimate κ from the Wiedemann–Franz law.

Method

Invert $\sigma=ne^2\tau/m$; then $\ell=v_F\tau$, $f=1/2\pi\tau$ and $\kappa=L\sigma T$ with $L=\pi^2k_B^2/3e^2$.

Show worked solution
  1. $\tau=\frac{m}{ne^2\rho}=\frac{9.109\times10^{-31}}{8.45\times10^{28}\,(1.602\times10^{-19})^2\,1.70\times10^{-8}}=2.47\times10^{-14}$ s.
  2. $\ell=v_F\tau=1.57\times10^6\times2.47\times10^{-14}=3.9\times10^{-8}$ m ≈ 39 nm, about 100 lattice spacings.
  3. $f_{\omega\tau=1}=1/2\pi\tau=6.4$ THz.
  4. $\kappa=L\sigma T=2.44\times10^{-8}\times5.88\times10^{7}\times295=424$ W m⁻¹ K⁻¹.
Result: τ ≈ 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ s, ℓ ≈ 39 nm, f ≈ 6 THz, κ ≈ 420 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹.
Sanity check: κ measured (Kittel ch. 5 Table 1, 300 K) is 401 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹, about 6% below the estimate. That matches the measured Lorenz number for Cu (2.23–2.33 × 10⁻⁸, Kittel Table 5) sitting slightly below 2.44 × 10⁻⁸. Model 1 and Model 4 reproduce τ and ℓ at their defaults.
