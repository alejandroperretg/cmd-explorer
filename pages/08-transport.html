Transport: conductivity, Umklapp, Wiedemann–Franz, thermoelectrics

How electrons carry charge and heat through a metal or semiconductor. A field shifts the Fermi sphere and scattering holds it in place. That one picture gives Ohm's law, the AC response, the Wiedemann–Franz law and, once a temperature gradient is added, the thermoelectric effects of exam question 3.

Overview

The free electron gas of Topic 07 is in equilibrium: the Fermi sphere sits at the origin and carries no current. Transport asks what happens when a force pushes the electrons and scattering pulls them back. The Boltzmann equation tracks the occupation $f(\vec r,\vec k,t)$. In the relaxation-time approximation it gives a Fermi sphere shifted by $\delta\vec k=-e\tau\vec E/\hbar$, and therefore $\sigma=ne^2\tau/m$.

Results to leave with: $\sigma=ne^2\tau/m$ and Ohm's law; $\sigma(\omega)=\sigma_0/(1+i\omega\tau)$ (sheet 9.2); Matthiessen's rule $\rho=\rho_{ph}(T)+\rho_i$; why Umklapp scattering matters for resistance; $\kappa/\sigma=LT$ with $L=\pi^2k_B^2/3e^2$; and the coupled charge and energy fluxes that define the thermopower $Q$ and the Peltier coefficient $\Pi=QT$.

Read alongside Kittel, Introduction to Solid State Physics 8th ed.: ch. 6 ("Electrical Conductivity and Ohm's Law", "Experimental Electrical Resistivity of Metals", "Umklapp Scattering", "Thermal Conductivity of Metals"), Appendix F ("Boltzmann Transport Equation") and ch. 8 ("Thermoelectric Effects"); lecture notes pp. 42–49. The Fermi sphere, $D(\varepsilon_F)$ and the Sommerfeld heat capacity are on Topic 07 and are used here without re-deriving them.

Concepts

2.2.1 Boltzmann transport equation

$f(\vec r,\vec k,t)$ is the occupation of the state $\vec k$ near position $\vec r$. It describes wave packets that are larger than a lattice cell but smaller than the mean free path. Without collisions, occupation is carried along the trajectories: an electron found at $(\vec r,\vec k)$ now was at $(\vec r-\vec v\,dt,\ \vec k-\vec F\,dt/\hbar)$ a time $dt$ ago. Collisions add or remove occupation at a fixed point. Expanding to first order in $dt$:

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\vec v\cdot\nabla_{\vec r}f+\frac{\vec F}{\hbar}\cdot\nabla_{\vec k}f=\Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\Big)_{\!s}$$

Here $\vec v=\nabla_{\vec k}\varepsilon/\hbar$ is the group velocity and $\vec F$ the external force, $(-e)\vec E$ for an electron in a field. The left side is drift and force; the right side, the scattering term, contains all the microscopic physics (transition rates $\propto|\langle\vec k'|H'|\vec k\rangle|^2$) and is hard to evaluate in general.

2.2.2 Relaxation-time approximation

Assume that scattering drives $f$ back toward the local equilibrium $f_0=f_{FD}(\varepsilon(\vec k))$ at a rate proportional to the deviation:

$$\Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\Big)_{\!s}=-\,\frac{f-f_0}{\tau}$$

$1/\tau$ is the scattering rate and $\tau$ (s) the mean time between collisions. Switch off a field at $t=0$ and $f-f_0$ decays as $e^{-t/\tau}$ (lecture example, notes p43; Kittel App. F eq. 8).

Notes slip: the handwritten line on notes p43 has no minus sign. The blackboard photo on the same page, the recap on p45 and Kittel App. F eq. 6 all have it. The minus is required: without it the deviation would grow instead of relaxing.

Do not confuse this τ with Kittel's τ = kBT in ch. 5 and App. F.

2.2.3 Electrical conductivity: the shifted Fermi sphere

For a static, uniform field $\vec E$ the drift and time terms vanish. To first order, $f_0+\tau\frac{(-e)\vec E}{\hbar}\cdot\nabla_{\vec k}f_0=f_{st}$, which is the Taylor expansion of a shifted distribution:

$$f_{st}(\vec k)=f_0\!\Big(\vec k+\frac{e\tau\vec E}{\hbar}\Big),\qquad \delta\vec k=-\frac{e\tau\vec E}{\hbar}$$

The whole Fermi sphere moves by $\delta\vec k$, against the field (the electron charge is $-e$). Only a thin shell changes occupation: states are emptied on one side and filled on the other (Model 1). Summing $(-e)\vec v_{\vec k}/V$ over the occupied states, with 2 for spin and $(2\pi)^3/V$ per $\vec k$ state, gives

$$\vec j=\frac{-e}{V}\,2\sum_{\vec k}\vec v_{\vec k}f_{st}=\frac{ne^2\tau}{m}\vec E\quad\Rightarrow\quad\sigma=\frac{ne^2\tau}{m},\ \ \rho=\frac1\sigma$$

Units: $[\sigma]=\mathrm{A\,V^{-1}m^{-1}}=\mathrm{S/m}$, $[\rho]=\Omega\,\mathrm m$. Kittel reaches the same result from $\hbar\,d\vec k/dt=-e\vec E$ with the shift frozen at $t=\tau$ (eqs. 39–43). With the mobility $\mu=e\tau/m$, $\sigma=ne\mu$.

Only the Fermi surface contributes: the same integral gives $\sigma=\tfrac13e^2\tau v_F^2D(\varepsilon_F)$, with $D$ per unit volume. Inserting $D(\varepsilon_F)=3n/2\varepsilon_F$ from Topic 07 and $\varepsilon_F=\tfrac12mv_F^2$ gives back $ne^2\tau/m$.

Notes slip: notes p44 writes $\vec j=n\,\frac{e\vec E}{m}\tau$, one factor $e$ short. The correct line is $\vec j=(ne)\,\frac{e\vec E}{m}\tau$: charge density × drift velocity. The board's "$\sigma=n\mu$" is missing the same $e$: $\sigma=ne\mu$ (Kittel ch. 8).

2.2.4 Ohm's law

Microscopic: $\vec j=\sigma\vec E$, a local relation between current density and field.

Macroscopic: a bar of length $L$ and cross-section $A$ with voltage $V$ has $E=V/L$ and $I=jA$, so

$$V=\frac{\rho L}{A}\,I=RI,\qquad R=\rho\frac{L}{A}$$

Typical size: copper at 295 K has $\rho=1.70\ \mu\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}=1.70\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\mathrm m$ (Kittel ch. 6 Table 3). With $n=8.45\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$ (Kittel Table 1), $\tau=m/ne^2\rho=2.5\times10^{-14}$ s and $\ell=v_F\tau=39$ nm. A current of 10⁶ A/m² drifts at only $j/ne=7.4\times10^{-5}$ m/s, about 10⁻¹⁰ of $v_F=1.57\times10^6$ m/s.

Checked with node: m/(ne²ρ) = 2.47 × 10⁻¹⁴ s; v_Fτ = 38.8 nm; 10⁶/(ne) = 7.39 × 10⁻⁵ m/s.

AC conductivity σ(ω) (sheet 9.2)

The drift velocity obeys Newton's law with a friction term, $m(\frac{d}{dt}+\frac1\tau)\vec v=-e\vec E$. With $\vec E=\vec E_0e^{i\omega t}$, the sheet's convention:

$$\sigma(\omega)=\frac{\sigma_0}{1+i\omega\tau}=\sigma_0\,\frac{1-i\omega\tau}{1+\omega^2\tau^2},\qquad\sigma_0=\frac{ne^2\tau}{m}$$

For $\omega\tau\ll1$ the current follows the field (Ohmic). For $\omega\tau\gg1$, $\sigma\approx-i\,ne^2/m\omega$: the electrons are freely accelerated between collisions, the current lags the field by 90° and no power is absorbed. The crossover $\omega\tau=1$ is at $f=1/2\pi\tau\approx6$ THz for Cu at room temperature (Model 2).

Sign convention: this page follows the sheet, with time dependence $e^{+i\omega t}$. Kittel ch. 6 Problem 6 uses $e^{-i\omega t}$ and writes $\sigma(\omega)=\sigma(0)\frac{1+i\omega\tau}{1+(\omega\tau)^2}=\frac{\sigma(0)}{1-i\omega\tau}$. The physics is the same; only the sign of $i$ flips (complex conjugate).

2.4.1 Conductivity of metals: Matthiessen's rule

A perfect periodic lattice does not scatter Bloch electrons. Resistance comes from departures from periodicity: lattice vibrations (phonons) and static defects (impurities, vacancies). If the two scattering processes are independent, their rates add:

$$\frac1\tau=\frac1{\tau_{ph}}+\frac1{\tau_i}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\rho=\rho_{ph}(T)+\rho_i$$
  • $\rho_i$ (residual resistivity) is independent of $T$ and set by sample purity: about 1 μΩ cm per atomic percent of impurity (Kittel).
  • $T\gg\Theta_D$: the phonon number grows ∝ $T$, so $\rho_{ph}\propto T$.
  • $T\ll\Theta_D$: $\rho_{ph}\propto T^5$ (stated in the lecture; Kittel App. J). Fewer phonons, and those left have small $q$ and scatter only through small angles.

Kittel's pure-copper example: at 4 K, $\tau\approx2\times10^{-9}$ s and $\ell=v_F\tau\approx0.3$ cm, which is the notes' p46 number. The resistivity ratio $\rho(295\,\mathrm K)/\rho_i$ measures purity (Model 3).

Checked: 1.57 × 10⁶ m/s × 2 × 10⁻⁹ s = 3.1 mm. The implied ρ(4 K) = m/(ne²τ) = 2.1 × 10⁻¹³ Ω m is 8 × 10⁴ times below 295 K, matching Kittel's "nearly 10⁵".

2.4.2 Umklapp processes

In electron–phonon scattering crystal momentum is conserved only up to a reciprocal lattice vector:

$$\vec k'=\vec k+\vec q+\vec G$$

$\vec G=0$ is a normal process. A small phonon $\vec q$ turns $\vec k$ through a small angle and hardly reduces the current. With $\vec G\neq0$ (Umklapp, "flip-over"), $\vec k+\vec q$ leaves the zone and is folded back by $\vec G$. The electron ends up on the far side of the Fermi sphere, scattered through an angle near π, so it loses almost all its forward velocity. Umklapp is therefore much more effective at relaxing the current, and the heat current too.

If the Fermi sphere does not touch the zone face, Umklapp needs a phonon with at least $q_0=G-2k_F$ (the gap between neighbouring Fermi spheres; Model 4). The two sources stress opposite ends of the temperature scale:

  • Lecture (notes p47): Umklapp needs large $|\vec q|$, so it occurs at higher $T$, where such phonons are thermally excited. Since it is efficient, it is what hinders electrical and thermal conduction there.
  • Kittel ch. 6: Umklapp accounts for most of the electrical resistivity of metals at low temperatures. Low here means low compared with $\Theta_D$ but still above a characteristic $\theta_U$. Even there the few Umklapp events outweigh the many inefficient small-angle normal events. Only for $T\ll\theta_U$ does the supply of phonons with $q\ge q_0$ freeze out, as $e^{-\theta_U/T}$, leaving normal scattering alone. Potassium: $\theta_U=23$ K against $\Theta_D=91$ K, with Umklapp negligible below about 2 K.

Both statements are correct. Umklapp needs a minimum $q$ (the lecture's point), so it disappears at the very lowest $T$. Wherever those phonons exist, it dominates the resistance (Kittel's point).

Number check: Kittel quotes $q_0=0.267\,k_F$ for one electron per atom in bcc. The straight-line gap along the nearest zone face, $q_0=|\vec G_{110}|-2k_F$ with $k_Fa=(6\pi^2)^{1/3}$ and $|\vec G_{110}|a=2\sqrt2\pi$, gives $0.280\,k_F$ (node). Kittel gives no derivation; his 0.267 is reproduced by the same formula with Table 1's rounded $k_F=0.75\times10^8$ cm⁻¹ for K and $a\approx5.23$ Å (0.266–0.268), whereas the unrounded $k_F=0.746\times10^8$ cm⁻¹ gives 0.280. Model 4 uses the exact geometry. Its $\theta_U\approx\Theta_D\,q_0/q_D$ (a Debye phonon of wavevector $q_0$, $q_D$ for 2 atoms per $a^3$) is this page's estimate, not Kittel's: 20 K against the measured 23 K.

2.5 Wiedemann–Franz law

The heat flux obeys Fourier's law, $\vec j_u=\kappa(-\nabla T)$. Kinetic theory gives $\kappa=\tfrac13Cv\ell$. For the electron gas, use the Sommerfeld heat capacity $C_{el}=\frac{\pi^2}{2}\frac{k_BT}{\varepsilon_F}nk_B$ (Topic 07) with $v=v_F$, $\ell=v_F\tau$ and $\varepsilon_F=\tfrac12mv_F^2$:

$$\kappa_{el}=\frac{\pi^2nk_B^2T\tau}{3m},\qquad\frac{\kappa_{el}}{\sigma}=\frac{\pi^2}{3}\Big(\frac{k_B}{e}\Big)^2T=LT$$

$n$, $\tau$ and $m$ cancel because the same electrons carry the charge and the heat. The Lorenz number is $L=2.44\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{W\,\Omega\,K^{-2}}$ (node; Kittel prints 2.45). Kittel Table 5 gives measured values for Cu of 2.23 at 0 °C and 2.33 at 100 °C, in the same units.

Check for copper: $L\sigma T=2.44\times10^{-8}\times5.88\times10^{7}\times300=431$ W m⁻¹ K⁻¹. Kittel ch. 5 Table 1 measures 401 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹. The law fails at intermediate temperatures, where small-angle inelastic scattering degrades the heat current more than the charge current (Model 5).

Notes p48 gives the units of κ as J/(m K), which is missing a s⁻¹: κ is in W m⁻¹ K⁻¹.

2.7.7 Thermoelectric effects: coupled fluxes

The notes give only the separate laws $\vec j=\sigma\vec E$ and $\vec j_u=\kappa(-\nabla T)$. In reality each flux responds to both driving forces. In linear response:

$$\begin{aligned}\vec j&=L_{11}\vec E+L_{12}(-\nabla T)\\ \vec j_u&=L_{21}\vec E+L_{22}(-\nabla T)\end{aligned}$$

$\vec j$ is the electric current density (A/m²) and $\vec j_u$ the energy flux (W/m²), with energies measured from the chemical potential μ, so it is the heat flux. $L_{11}=\sigma$.

  • Thermopower Q (Seebeck): open circuit, $\vec j=0$. A temperature gradient then builds up a field $\vec E=Q\nabla T$, so $Q=L_{12}/L_{11}$ (V/K).
  • Peltier coefficient Π: uniform temperature, $\nabla T=0$. The current carries energy $\vec j_u=\Pi\vec j$, so $\Pi=L_{21}/L_{11}$ (V = J/C: energy carried per unit charge).
  • Kelvin relation: Onsager reciprocity gives $L_{21}=TL_{12}$, hence $\Pi=QT$.

Eliminating $\vec E$ gives the form to write in an exam:

$$\vec j=\sigma\big(\vec E-Q\nabla T\big),\qquad\vec j_u=\Pi\,\vec j-\kappa\nabla T,\qquad\kappa=L_{22}-\frac{L_{12}L_{21}}{L_{11}}$$

Checked by substitution: E = j/σ + Q∇T inserted into the second line gives ju = (L₂₁/L₁₁) j − (L₂₂ − L₂₁L₁₂/L₁₁) ∇T.

2.7.7 Sign and size of Π and Q

Kittel's drift picture for a non-degenerate semiconductor (ch. 8, eqs. 54–60): each electron carries the energy $(E_c-\mu)+\tfrac32k_BT$ above the Fermi level, and its energy flux runs opposite to its charge flux. This gives

$$\Pi_e=-\frac{E_c-\mu+\tfrac32k_BT}{e}\ (<0),\qquad\Pi_h=+\frac{\mu-E_v+\tfrac32k_BT}{e}\ (>0),\qquad Q=\frac{\Pi}{T}$$

The sign of $Q$ gives the sign of the carriers. Heat one end of an n-type sample: electrons diffuse to the cold end and charge it negatively. This is the quick hot-probe test for n vs p type (Kittel Fig. 23). Example: n-Si with $E_c-\mu=0.25$ eV at 300 K gives $\Pi_e=-0.289$ V and $Q=-0.96$ mV/K.

In a metal only electrons within $k_BT$ of $\varepsilon_F$ contribute, so $Q$ is smaller by about $k_BT/\varepsilon_F$. The free-electron (Mott) result is $Q=-\frac{\pi^2k_B^2T}{2e\varepsilon_F}$, or −1.57 μV/K for Cu at 300 K (Model 6).

Source: the metal formula is the standard Boltzmann result for a constant τ (Mott formula). Kittel ch. 8 does not give it; App. F has the method. Measured Cu has the opposite (positive) sign, a known failure of the free-electron model that band structure explains. No measured value is quoted here because none is in the course sources.

Checked with node: (0.25 + 1.5 × 0.025852) V = 0.2888 V, /300 K = 0.963 mV/K; π²k_B²(300 K)/(2e · 7.00 eV) = 1.570 μV/K.

Homework guides

Sheet 9 · Problem 2 · 6 pts

Frequency dependence of electrical conductivity

(i) For a metal modelled as a free electron gas with a time-dependent homogeneous force $\vec F(t)$ on each electron, show that the non-equilibrium distribution is $f_E(t,\vec k)=f_0\big(t-\tau,\ \vec k-\tau\frac{\vec F}{\hbar}\big)$, with τ the relaxation time. (ii) Scattering acts as a friction term, $m\big(\frac{d}{dt}+\frac1\tau\big)\vec v=\vec F$. Show with this ansatz that in a homogeneous field $\vec E(t)$ oscillating at angular frequency ω the AC conductivity is $\sigma(\omega)=\frac{\sigma_{DC}}{1+i\omega\tau}$.

Method

(i) Write the Boltzmann equation in the relaxation-time approximation for a homogeneous system ($\nabla_{\vec r}f=0$). Expand the proposed $f_E$ to first order in τ and check that it satisfies the equation to that order. (ii) Insert $\vec E=\vec E_0e^{i\omega t}$ into the equation of motion, solve for the steady-state drift velocity, and use $\vec j=-ne\vec v$.

Reading of the sheet: "show" in (i) is taken to mean "to first order in τ" (linear response), which is how the lecture derived the static shifted sphere on p43–44. The sheet's form of $\sigma(\omega)$ fixes the time dependence as $e^{+i\omega t}$. Kittel ch. 6 Problem 6 is the same exercise in the $e^{-i\omega t}$ convention.
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. Homogeneous system: $\partial_tf+\frac{\vec F}{\hbar}\cdot\nabla_{\vec k}f=-\frac{f-f_0}{\tau}$.
  2. Expand the ansatz to first order: $f_E\approx f_0-\tau\,\partial_tf_0-\tau\frac{\vec F}{\hbar}\cdot\nabla_{\vec k}f_0$. Hence $-\frac{f_E-f_0}{\tau}=\partial_tf_0+\frac{\vec F}{\hbar}\cdot\nabla_{\vec k}f_0$.
  3. Left side with $f=f_E$: $\partial_tf_E+\frac{\vec F}{\hbar}\cdot\nabla_{\vec k}f_E=\partial_tf_0+\frac{\vec F}{\hbar}\cdot\nabla_{\vec k}f_0+\mathcal O(\tau)$. The correction terms are τ times second derivatives (or $\dot{\vec F}$), which is second order in the perturbation. Both sides agree to first order, which proves (i).
  4. Interpretation: the distribution now is the equilibrium one from one relaxation time ago, displaced by the momentum $\tau\vec F$ gained since the last collision. For a static field $\vec F=-e\vec E$ it reduces to the lecture's shifted sphere $f_0(\vec k+e\tau\vec E/\hbar)$.
  5. (ii) Each electron's mean (drift) velocity obeys $m(\dot{\vec v}+\vec v/\tau)=-e\vec E_0e^{i\omega t}$. Try $\vec v=\vec v_0e^{i\omega t}$: $m\big(i\omega+\frac1\tau\big)\vec v_0=-e\vec E_0$, so $\vec v_0=-\frac{e\tau/m}{1+i\omega\tau}\vec E_0$.
  6. Current density: $\vec j=n(-e)\vec v=\frac{ne^2\tau/m}{1+i\omega\tau}\vec E$. With $\sigma_{DC}=ne^2\tau/m$ (2.2.3) this is $\sigma(\omega)=\frac{\sigma_{DC}}{1+i\omega\tau}$.
Result: $f_E(t,\vec k)=f_0(t-\tau,\vec k-\tau\vec F/\hbar)$ solves the relaxation-time Boltzmann equation to first order; $\sigma(\omega)=\sigma_{DC}/(1+i\omega\tau)=\sigma_{DC}(1-i\omega\tau)/(1+\omega^2\tau^2)$ for $e^{+i\omega t}$.
Sanity check: ω → 0 gives σDC. For ωτ ≫ 1, σ → −i ne²/(mω), independent of τ: free acceleration, current 90° behind the field. Replacing i by −i reproduces Kittel's eq. 62, σ(0)(1 + iωτ)/(1 + ω²τ²). For Cu, ωτ = 1 at f = 1/(2πτ) = 6.4 THz with τ = 2.47 × 10⁻¹⁴ s (node), so household and radio frequencies are fully in the DC limit.

Exam angle

2022 Q3 · Thermoelectric phenomena · not yet lectured — from Kittel

(a) Give expressions for the energy flux and the current density. (b) Define the Peltier coefficient and the absolute thermopower Q. The notes' separate laws $\vec j=\sigma\vec E$ and $\vec j_u=\kappa(-\nabla T)$ are not a full answer: Q3 asks for the coupled fluxes.

Show model answer
  1. (a) In linear response each flux depends on both forces: $$\vec j=L_{11}\vec E+L_{12}(-\nabla T),\qquad\vec j_u=L_{21}\vec E+L_{22}(-\nabla T),$$ with $L_{11}=\sigma$ and energies measured from μ. Equivalent form: $\vec j=\sigma(\vec E-Q\nabla T)$, $\vec j_u=\Pi\vec j-\kappa\nabla T$. For a semiconductor with electrons (Kittel): $\vec j=ne\mu_e\vec E$ and $\vec j_u=n\big(E_c-\mu+\tfrac32k_BT\big)(-\mu_e)\vec E$, with mobility $\mu_e>0$.
  2. (b) Thermopower: in open circuit ($\vec j=0$) a temperature gradient produces $\vec E=Q\nabla T$, so $Q=L_{12}/L_{11}$, in V/K. Peltier coefficient: at uniform T the energy flux carried by a current is $\vec j_u=\Pi\vec j$, so $\Pi=L_{21}/L_{11}$, the energy carried per unit charge, in V.
  3. Kelvin relation: $\Pi=QT$ (Onsager, $L_{21}=TL_{12}$). Signs: electrons give $\Pi,Q<0$ ($\Pi_e=-(E_c-\mu+\tfrac32k_BT)/e$); holes give $\Pi,Q>0$.

Points are lost for writing only the uncoupled Ohm and Fourier laws, for sign errors, and for not linking the sign of Q to the carrier type.

Exam-style exercise · thermoelectrics

Hot-probe test on n-type silicon

An n-type Si bar at 300 K has its Fermi level 0.25 eV below the conduction band edge. (a) Find Π and Q in Kittel's drift model. (b) One end is heated by 10 K with no current flowing: which end becomes positive, and what voltage appears? (c) What heat flux does a current density of 10⁴ A/m² carry at uniform temperature?

Method

$\Pi_e=-(E_c-\mu+\tfrac32k_BT)/e$, then $Q=\Pi/T$ (Kelvin). Open circuit means $\vec E=Q\nabla T$; the Peltier heat flux is $j_u=\Pi j$.

Show worked solution
  1. $k_BT=25.85$ meV at 300 K, so $\tfrac32k_BT=38.8$ meV and $\Pi_e=-(0.250+0.0388)\ \mathrm{eV}/e=-0.289$ V.
  2. $Q=\Pi/T=-0.289/300=-0.96$ mV/K.
  3. $V_{hot}-V_{cold}=-Q\,\Delta T=+9.6$ mV: the hot end is positive, because electrons pile up at the cold end.
  4. $j_u=\Pi j=-0.289\ \mathrm V\times10^4\ \mathrm{A/m^2}=-2.9\times10^3\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$. The minus sign means heat flows opposite to $\vec j$, i.e. along the electron motion.
Result: Π = −0.29 V, Q = −0.96 mV/K, hot end +9.6 mV, Peltier heat flux 2.9 kW/m² against the current.
Sanity check: the same numbers come out of Model 6 at its default settings. The order of magnitude matches Kittel Fig. 23, where QT for n-Si is a few tenths of a volt. The magnitude is about 600 times the Cu free-electron value (1.57 μV/K), as expected from (Δ/k_BT)/(k_BT/ε_F)-type scaling.
Exam-style exercise · conduction in copper

Relaxation time, mean free path and thermal conductivity of Cu

Copper at 295 K: ρ = 1.70 μΩ cm, n = 8.45 × 10²⁸ m⁻³, vF = 1.57 × 10⁶ m/s (Kittel). Find τ, ℓ, the AC crossover frequency, and estimate κ from the Wiedemann–Franz law.

Method

Invert $\sigma=ne^2\tau/m$; then $\ell=v_F\tau$, $f=1/2\pi\tau$ and $\kappa=L\sigma T$ with $L=\pi^2k_B^2/3e^2$.

Show worked solution
  1. $\tau=\frac{m}{ne^2\rho}=\frac{9.109\times10^{-31}}{8.45\times10^{28}\,(1.602\times10^{-19})^2\,1.70\times10^{-8}}=2.47\times10^{-14}$ s.
  2. $\ell=v_F\tau=1.57\times10^6\times2.47\times10^{-14}=3.9\times10^{-8}$ m ≈ 39 nm, about 100 lattice spacings.
  3. $f_{\omega\tau=1}=1/2\pi\tau=6.4$ THz.
  4. $\kappa=L\sigma T=2.44\times10^{-8}\times5.88\times10^{7}\times295=424$ W m⁻¹ K⁻¹.
Result: τ ≈ 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ s, ℓ ≈ 39 nm, f ≈ 6 THz, κ ≈ 420 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹.
Sanity check: κ measured (Kittel ch. 5 Table 1, 300 K) is 401 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹, about 6% below the estimate. That matches the measured Lorenz number for Cu (2.23–2.33 × 10⁻⁸, Kittel Table 5) sitting slightly below 2.44 × 10⁻⁸. Model 1 reproduces τ and ℓ at its default τ.

Not asked in 2022: the Boltzmann equation, σ, Matthiessen, Umklapp and Wiedemann–Franz. Sheet 9.2 is the likeliest exercise pattern. In 5 minutes without notes you should be able to: write the Boltzmann equation with the RTA term (with its minus sign); derive $\sigma=ne^2\tau/m$ from the shifted sphere; derive $\sigma(\omega)$ and its limits; sketch ρ(T) with residual, $T^5$ and $T$ regions; draw the two-sphere Umklapp picture; and derive $L=\pi^2k_B^2/3e^2$.
