Lattice Heat Capacity

Each phonon mode is a quantum harmonic oscillator, so the crystal's partition function factorises and the heat capacity is a sum of one-oscillator terms. That sum gives the classical Dulong–Petit value at high temperature, a $T^3$ law at low temperature, and the Debye and Einstein models in between. The Debye temperature $\Theta_D$ defined here returns in transport (topic 08) and in the electron–phonon cross-over (topic 07).

Overview

The lecture takes the statistical-mechanics route. Write the energy of the harmonic crystal as a sum over modes $(\mathbf k,s)$, sum the geometric series in each mode's partition function, and take $u=-\frac1V\partial_\beta\ln Z$ and $c_v=\partial u/\partial T$. The result (★) below is exact for a harmonic lattice. Everything else in the section is a limit of it (high $T$, low $T$) or an approximation to the dispersion $\omega_s(\mathbf k)$ (Debye, Einstein, combined).

Key results to leave with: (★) and where the Bose factor comes from; Dulong–Petit with its $1/T^2$ corrections; the $T^3$ law and why it becomes $T^d$ in $d$ dimensions (sheet 7.1); the Debye formula and how to pull $\Theta_D$ out of measured data (sheet 7.2); and why Einstein's flat mode freezes out exponentially. The electronic heat capacity and the sheet 9.1 cross-over are on topic 07 and are not repeated here.

Read alongside Kittel, Introduction to Solid State Physics 8th ed., ch. 5 (Phonon Heat Capacity: Planck Distribution, Normal Mode Enumeration, Density of States in Three Dimensions, Debye Model, Debye $T^3$ Law, Einstein Model), and the lecture notes pp. 33–38.

Concepts

1.7.1 Lattice specific heat from the partition function

A crystal of $N$ unit cells with $n$ atoms each has $3nN$ normal modes: $N$ wavevectors in the first BZ times $3n$ branches (3 acoustic, $3n-3$ optical). In the harmonic approximation each mode is an independent oscillator. A microstate is the list of occupation numbers $\{n_s(\mathbf k)\}$ ($3nN$ non-negative integers), with $E=E_{eq}+\sum_{\mathbf k,s}\hbar\omega_s(\mathbf k)\big(n_s(\mathbf k)+\tfrac12\big)$. Because $E$ is a sum, $Z$ is a product, and each factor is a geometric series starting at $n_s=0$:

$$Z=e^{-\beta E_{eq}}\prod_{\mathbf k,s}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_s(\mathbf k)(n+\frac12)}=e^{-\beta E_{eq}}\prod_{\mathbf k,s}\frac{e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_s/2}}{1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_s}} .$$

Then $u=-\frac1V\partial_\beta\ln Z$ gives the energy density as static lattice, plus Bose occupation, plus zero-point motion:

$$u=u_{eq}+\frac1V\sum_{\mathbf k,s}\hbar\omega_s(\mathbf k)\Big(\frac{1}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega_s(\mathbf k)}-1}+\frac12\Big).$$

With $\partial_T=-k_B\beta^2\partial_\beta$, and with $u_{eq}$ and the zero-point term independent of $T$:

$$c_v=\frac{\partial u}{\partial T}\Big|_V=\frac{k_B}{V}\sum_{\mathbf k,s}\frac{x^2e^x}{(e^x-1)^2},\qquad x=\frac{\hbar\omega_s(\mathbf k)}{k_BT}.\qquad(\star)$$

$c_v$ is in J K⁻¹ m⁻³. Each mode contributes $k_B\,w(x)$, where $w(x)=x^2e^x/(e^x-1)^2$ rises from 0 (frozen, $\hbar\omega\gg k_BT$) to 1 (classical, $\hbar\omega\ll k_BT$). Model 1 shows this weight over a real spectrum. This is the same equipartition breakdown as in any quantum oscillator system: a level spacing much larger than $k_BT$ cannot absorb heat.

Per volume, per crystal, per mole, per gram

Four normalisations appear in the sources, and mixing them up is the most common numerical slip:

  • Lecture: $c_v$ is per volume (the $\frac1V$ in (★)), in J K⁻¹ m⁻³.
  • Kittel: $C_V$ is for the whole crystal of $N$ atoms, in J K⁻¹. So $C_V=V\,c_v$.
  • Tables and sheet 9.1: per mole of atoms, $N=N_A$, so $Nk_B=R=8.314$ J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  • Sheet 7.2 graph: per gram, in cal g⁻¹ K⁻¹. Divide the molar value by the molar mass (12.011 g mol⁻¹ for C) and by 4.184 J/cal.

On this page $c_v$ (lower case) is the lecture's density and $C$ (upper case) is a total or molar value. Models plot $C/(3Nk_B)$, which is the same in every normalisation. Dulong–Petit is $3R=24.94$ J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, or 0.496 cal g⁻¹ K⁻¹ for carbon.

Notation clashes: lecture $\Theta_D$, $\bar c$ versus Kittel $\theta$, $v$. Kittel ch. 5 writes $\tau=k_BT$, which is not the relaxation time of topic 08. In the notes, $n$ is both the number of basis atoms and the atom density; this page writes the atom density as $n_{at}=nN/V$. The notes' $N$ counts cells on p33–34 but atoms in the Debye construction on p36.

1.7.1.1 High-temperature limit

For $k_BT\gg\hbar\omega_s$ for every mode, i.e. $x\ll1$, expand the mode weight:

$$\frac{x^2e^x}{(e^x-1)^2}=1-\frac{x^2}{12}+\frac{x^4}{240}-\dots\;\Rightarrow\; c_v=\frac{3nN}{V}k_B-\frac{k_B}{12V}\sum_{\mathbf k,s}\Big(\frac{\hbar\omega_s}{k_BT}\Big)^2+\dots$$

The leading term is Dulong–Petit, $c_v=3n_{at}k_B$: every mode carries $k_B$, as equipartition gives for two quadratic degrees of freedom. The quantum correction is $\mathcal O(1/T^2)$, set by the mean square frequency. For the Debye spectrum, $\langle\omega^2\rangle=\frac35\omega_D^2$, which gives $C/3Nk_B=1-\frac1{20}(\Theta_D/T)^2$. For Einstein it is $1-\frac1{12}(\Theta_E/T)^2$.

"High" means compared with $\Theta_D$. Cu ($\Theta_D=343$ K) is at 94 % of $3R$ at 300 K. Diamond ($\Theta_D=2230$ K) is at only 17 %, so at room temperature it is far from classical.

Check: the series against the exact $w(x)$ at $x=0.5$: 0.979427 vs 0.979424. The Debye correction at $T=\Theta_D$ predicts 0.950; the exact value is 0.952 (node). The 300 K values come from the full Debye function with Kittel's Θ_D.

1.7.1.2 Low-temperature limit: the $T^3$ law

For $k_BT$ well below every optical frequency and below the zone-boundary acoustic frequencies, only long-wavelength acoustic modes are thawed. They are linear, $\omega_s=c_s(\hat{\mathbf k})k$. Replace the sum by $V\!\int\!d^3k/(2\pi)^3$ with $d^3k=k^2dk\,d\Omega$ and substitute $x=\hbar c_sk/k_BT$. The upper limit can go to $\infty$, because the integrand has died by then:

$$u-u_0=\frac{(k_BT)^4}{\hbar^3}\Big(\frac13\sum_{s=1}^{3}\oint\frac{1}{c_s^3}\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\Big)\frac{3}{2\pi^2}\int_0^\infty\frac{x^3\,dx}{e^x-1},\qquad\int_0^\infty\frac{x^3dx}{e^x-1}=\frac{\pi^4}{15}.$$

The bracket defines the averaged sound speed $1/\bar c^3$. Differentiating:

$$c_v^{low}=\frac{2\pi^2}{5}\,k_B\Big(\frac{k_BT}{\hbar\bar c}\Big)^3\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad C=\frac{12\pi^4}{5}Nk_B\Big(\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\Big)^3=1944\,\frac{\text{J}}{\text{mol K}}\Big(\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\Big)^3 .$$

The right-hand form is Kittel's. It is the lecture's result times $V$, once $\Theta_D$ is defined as on the next card. Counting picture (Kittel Fig. 10): modes with $\hbar\omega\lesssim k_BT$ fill a sphere of radius $k_T=k_BT/\hbar\bar c$, a fraction $(T/\Theta_D)^3$ of all $3N$ modes, each with energy $\sim k_BT$. So $U\sim3Nk_BT(T/\Theta_D)^3$ and $C\propto T^3$. In $d$ dimensions the sphere becomes a $d$-ball, so $C\propto T^d$ (Model 3, sheet 7.1).

In real crystals the clean $T^3$ regime needs roughly $T\lesssim\Theta_D/50$ (Kittel), because the dispersion bends away from $ck$ well before the zone boundary. Inside the Debye model itself, $T^3$ is accurate to 0.002 % at $T=\Theta_D/20$ and to 3 % at $\Theta_D/10$.

Check: with $k_D=(6\pi^2n_{at})^{1/3}$ and $k_B\Theta_D=\hbar\bar ck_D$, the ratio $V\frac{2\pi^2}{5}k_B(k_BT/\hbar\bar c)^3\big/\frac{12\pi^4}{5}Nk_B(T/\Theta_D)^3$ evaluates to 1.000000 (node). $\int x^4e^x/(e^x-1)^2dx=4\pi^4/15=25.976$ numerically. Debye/T³ ratios: 0.99998 at $T/\Theta_D=0.05$, 0.973 at 0.1.

1.7.1.3 Intermediate T: the Debye model

(★) is exact but needs $\omega_s(\mathbf k)$ over the whole BZ. Debye makes two replacements: (a) three acoustic branches $\omega=\bar c\,k$ for all $k$; (b) the BZ becomes a sphere of the same volume, i.e. the same number of $k$-points:

$$\frac{4\pi}{3}k_D^3=\frac{(2\pi)^3}{V}N\;\Rightarrow\;k_D=\big(6\pi^2n_{at}\big)^{1/3},\qquad \hbar\omega_D=\hbar\bar ck_D=k_B\Theta_D .$$

Redoing the low-T integral with the upper limit $x_D=\Theta_D/T$ kept finite:

$$c_v^{Debye}=9\,n_{at}k_B\Big(\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\Big)^3\int_0^{\Theta_D/T}\frac{x^4e^x}{(e^x-1)^2}\,dx .$$

For $\Theta_D/T\to\infty$ the integral is $4\pi^4/15$ (the $T^3$ law). For $\Theta_D/T\to0$ it is $\frac13(\Theta_D/T)^3$, giving $3n_{at}k_B$ (Dulong–Petit). Both limits are therefore right, and in between it is a one-parameter curve. $\Theta_D$ plays the role for phonons that $\varepsilon_F/k_B$ plays for electrons: it separates "quantum" from "classical". The Debye sphere is not the BZ; it only has the same volume. For $n>1$ basis atoms, count all $nN$ atoms in $k_D$, or use the combined model below.

1.7.1.3 Einstein model and the combined model

Einstein: every atom vibrates at one frequency $\omega_E$ in three directions, independent of $\mathbf k$. Then (★) has $3N$ identical terms:

$$c_v^{Einstein}=3n_{at}k_B\,\frac{(\Theta_E/T)^2\,e^{\Theta_E/T}}{\big(e^{\Theta_E/T}-1\big)^2},\qquad k_B\Theta_E=\hbar\omega_E .$$

For $T\gg\Theta_E$ this gives Dulong–Petit. For $T\ll\Theta_E$ it gives $c_v\approx3n_{at}k_B(\Theta_E/T)^2e^{-\Theta_E/T}$, an exponential freeze-out. Experiment shows $T^3$ instead, because a real crystal always has acoustic modes of arbitrarily low frequency near $k=0$, and those stay thawed at any $T>0$. The Einstein model describes an optical branch well, since optical branches are nearly flat.

Combined (lecture p38): for $n$ atoms per cell, the 3 acoustic branches go to Debye (sphere built from the $N$ cells) and the $3(n-1)$ optical branches to Einstein:

$$\frac{C}{3nNk_B}=\frac{1}{n}\Big[D\big(T/\Theta_D\big)+(n-1)\,E\big(T/\Theta_E\big)\Big],$$

where $D$ and $E$ are the Debye and Einstein curves normalised to 1 at high $T$ (Model 2).

Phonon density of states

The summand in (★) depends on $\mathbf k$ only through $\omega$, so the sum can be grouped by frequency:

$$c_v=\int_0^\infty k_B\,w\!\Big(\frac{\hbar\omega}{k_BT}\Big)\,g(\omega)\,d\omega,\qquad g(\omega)=\sum_s\int_{BZ}\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\delta\big(\omega-\omega_s(\mathbf k)\big)=\sum_s\oint_{\omega_s=\omega}\frac{dS_k}{(2\pi)^3\,|\nabla_{\mathbf k}\omega_s|}.$$

$g$ is per volume and per unit $\omega$, and $\int g\,d\omega=3n_{at}$ (Kittel's $D(\omega)=Vg$). Debye: $g=\frac{3}{2\pi^2}\frac{\omega^2}{\bar c^3}$ up to $\omega_D$. Einstein: $g=3n_{at}\,\delta(\omega-\omega_E)$. A real $g$ (e.g. Al, sketched on p38) follows $\omega^2$ only at low $\omega$, then has van Hove peaks where $\nabla_{\mathbf k}\omega=0$. The dispersion relations themselves are on topic 05.

Homework guides

Sheet 7 · Problem 1 · 3 pts

Heat capacity of layer lattice

An insulator is built from layers of atoms with rigid coupling between the layers, so the atoms can only move within the plane of their layer. Show that the phonon heat capacity in the Debye approximation at low temperatures is proportional to $T^2$.

Method

Redo the 1.7.1.2 calculation in two dimensions. Start from (★), now per layer area $A$. In-plane motion leaves two acoustic branches (one longitudinal, one transverse) with in-plane wavevectors. At low $T$ use $\omega_s=c_sk$, $\sum_{\mathbf k}\to A\int d^2k/(2\pi)^2$ and $d^2k=k\,dk\,d\varphi$. The extra power of $k$ that the 3D case had is gone, so $u\propto T^3$ and $c\propto T^2$.

Assumption: I read the problem as Kittel ch. 5 problem 4(a), of which it is a copy: each layer is a 2D elastic sheet, out-of-plane displacement is suppressed, and the rigid coupling adds no modes along the stacking direction. Insulator means no electronic term. Sound speeds are taken as isotropic in the plane; anisotropy only changes the average $\bar c$.
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. Thermal energy per area (the zero-point term does not depend on $T$ and drops out of $c$): $\displaystyle u-u_0=\sum_{s=1}^{2}\int\frac{d^2k}{(2\pi)^2}\frac{\hbar c_sk}{e^{\hbar c_sk/k_BT}-1}$.
  2. Polar coordinates: $\int d^2k=\int_0^{2\pi}d\varphi\int k\,dk$, so $\displaystyle u-u_0=\sum_s\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{k_D}\frac{\hbar c_s k^2\,dk}{e^{\hbar c_sk/k_BT}-1}$.
  3. Substitute $x=\hbar c_sk/k_BT$. At low $T$ the upper limit $x_D=\Theta_D/T\to\infty$: $\displaystyle u-u_0=\sum_s\frac{(k_BT)^3}{2\pi\,\hbar^2c_s^2}\int_0^\infty\frac{x^2dx}{e^x-1}=\sum_s\frac{(k_BT)^3}{2\pi\,\hbar^2c_s^2}\cdot2\zeta(3)$, with $\zeta(3)=1.202$.
  4. Define the 2D average $\frac{2}{\bar c^2}=\sum_s\frac1{c_s^2}$: $\displaystyle u-u_0=\frac{2\zeta(3)}{\pi}\frac{(k_BT)^3}{(\hbar\bar c)^2}$.
  5. Differentiate: $\displaystyle c=\frac{\partial u}{\partial T}=\frac{6\zeta(3)}{\pi}\,k_B\Big(\frac{k_BT}{\hbar\bar c}\Big)^2\propto T^2$, in J K⁻¹ m⁻², the 2D analogue of the lecture's per-volume $c_v$.
  6. Debye form, for the whole layer of $N$ atoms: the Debye disc $\pi k_D^2=(2\pi)^2N/A$ gives $k_D^2=4\pi N/A$. With $k_B\Theta_D=\hbar\bar ck_D$, $C=Ac=24\zeta(3)\,Nk_B\,(T/\Theta_D)^2\approx28.8\,Nk_B(T/\Theta_D)^2$.
Result: $c=\dfrac{6\zeta(3)}{\pi}k_B\Big(\dfrac{k_BT}{\hbar\bar c}\Big)^2$ per area, or $C=24\zeta(3)Nk_B(T/\Theta_D)^2$ for $N$ atoms: $C\propto T^2$. Equivalently, the 2D Debye density of states per area, $g(\omega)=\omega/(\pi\bar c^2)$, is linear in $\omega$, so the energy $\int\hbar\omega\langle n\rangle g\,d\omega$ scales as $T^3$.
Sanity check: the same steps in 3D ($d^3k=4\pi k^2dk$, 3 branches, $\int x^3/(e^x-1)=\pi^4/15$) reproduce the lecture's $\frac{2\pi^2}{5}k_B(k_BT/\hbar\bar c)^3$. Numerically (node), the closed form agrees with the full 2D Debye integral to $10^{-9}$ at $T/\Theta_D=0.005$, and the log–log slope is 2.000 (Model 3). Units: $k_BT/\hbar\bar c$ is an inverse length, so $c$ is in J K⁻¹ m⁻² ✓.
Sheet 7 · Problem 2 · 4 pts

Debye temperature $T_D$

(i) From the specific-heat data of diamond by Touloukian and Buyco (graph on the sheet: $c_V$ in cal g⁻¹ K⁻¹ against $T$ in K, log–log, 10 K to 1000 K, with "Data of Touloukian and Buyco (1970b)", "Einstein formula", "Einstein data" and "Debye formula"), estimate the Debye temperature $T_D$ of diamond. (ii) Compare with the literature value $T_{D,\text{diamond}}=2250$ K (a recent first-principles calculation gives 2240 K) and comment on the difference.

Method

Convert the graph's per-gram units to per mole. Then either (a) use the $T^3$ law on a low-$T$ point, $\Theta_D=T\,(C_0/C)^{1/3}$, or (b) invert the full Debye formula at a point where $T^3$ no longer holds. Do both at several temperatures. The spread of the answers is part (ii).

Assumptions: molar mass of carbon 12.011 g mol⁻¹ and 1 cal = 4.184 J (standard values). Graph readings by hand are good to about one line width; my digitisation of the figure (pixel calibration on its frame, decade = 392 px in T and 144 px in c) is good to about ±2 % in $c$, i.e. ±1 % in $\Theta_D$. $c_V$ and $c_p$ are not distinguished: for a stiff solid like diamond the difference is negligible compared with the spread found below.
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. Units. Per mole, $C=c\cdot M$; in J, multiply by 4.184. Dulong–Petit $3R=24.94$ J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ is $0.496$ cal g⁻¹ K⁻¹ for carbon. The graph flattens towards about 0.45 at 1000 K, so the units are consistent.
  2. $T^3$ law in graph units. $C=\frac{12\pi^4}{5}R(T/\Theta_D)^3=1944\,(T/\Theta_D)^3$ J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, i.e. $c=38.7\,(T/\Theta_D)^3$ cal g⁻¹ K⁻¹. So $\Theta_D=T\,(38.7/c)^{1/3}$.
  3. Low-T reading. At $T=20$ K the data sit at $c\approx6\times10^{-5}$ cal g⁻¹ K⁻¹: $\Theta_D=20\cdot(38.7/6\times10^{-5})^{1/3}\approx1730$ K. Here $T/\Theta_D\approx0.012<1/50$, so the $T^3$ law should apply. The figure's own "Debye formula" line is a pure $T^3$ line, and it digitises to $\Theta_D\approx1735$ K, fitted to these lowest points.
  4. Is it really $T^3$? Between 18.5 K and 46 K the data rise by a factor of 10.1 over a factor of 2.49 in $T$: the slope is $\ln10.1/\ln2.49=2.5$, not 3. Point by point, $\Theta_D$ drifts from 1700 to 1960 K across the region where $T^3$ should hold.
  5. Full Debye fit at higher T. At $T=200$ K, $c\approx5.0\times10^{-2}$ cal g⁻¹ K⁻¹, i.e. $C/3R=0.101$. Solving $3\,(T/\Theta_D)^3\int_0^{\Theta_D/T}\frac{x^4e^x}{(e^x-1)^2}dx=0.101$ numerically gives $\Theta_D\approx1805$ K. Over 130–300 K every point inverts to 1783–1814 K; a least-squares fit gives 1800 K with 1.3 % rms misfit (Model 4).
  6. All data. Θ_D(T) runs from about 1700 K (18 K) up to about 2100 K (60–85 K), back down to about 1800 K (130–300 K), and up to about 2150 K (800 K). A fit to all points gives 1890 K, with 17 % rms misfit.
Result (i): $T_D\approx1800$ K, from the full Debye formula over 130–300 K, where the data are densest and most consistent. Depending on the temperature range used, single points give 1700–2150 K. (ii): this is about 20 % below the literature 2250 K.

Comment for (ii)

  • $\Theta_D$ is a fit parameter, not a constant. The Debye model replaces the real $g(\omega)$ by $\omega^2$ up to a cut-off. A real spectrum has extra weight at intermediate frequencies, because transverse branches flatten towards the zone boundary (van Hove peaks). That gives more heat capacity than Debye at intermediate $T$, so $\Theta_D(T)$ dips below its $T\to0$ value. Literature tables quote the $T\to0$ limit, which probes only the elastic (sound-speed) regime. That is also what a first-principles stiffness calculation gives (2240 K), so both reference values measure the same thing.
  • The low-T data are the weak point. Below $\Theta_D/50\approx45$ K diamond's heat capacity is tiny ($10^{-5}$–$10^{-4}$ cal g⁻¹ K⁻¹). Any extra contribution (impurities such as nitrogen, the sample holder, calibration errors) makes $c$ too large and so $\Theta_D$ too small. The slope of 2.5 instead of 3 points to exactly that. To get 2250 K at 20 K the reading would have to be $2.7\times10^{-5}$, a factor 2.2 below the plotted points, i.e. about 50 pixels on the figure. That is far beyond any reading error.
  • The method matters. A $T^3$ fit and a full-Debye fit agree only where $T\ll\Theta_D/10$. The Einstein curve in the same figure ($\Theta_E\approx1300$ K) fits only above about 250 K, the region Einstein originally used.
Sanity check: the figure's dotted Einstein curve, digitised with the same calibration, inverts to an almost constant $\Theta_E=1295$–$1308$ K from 85 K to 150 K. A wrong axis calibration would make it drift, so the calibration is good. The figure's $T^3$ line gives $\Theta_D=1727$–$1741$ K along its whole length (40–117 K). The hand inversion at 200 K (node bisection on the Debye integral) and the T³ estimate (1836 K) differ by 2 %, as expected at $T/\Theta_D=0.11$ (Model 2: Debye/T³ = 0.97 there).

Debye and Einstein for copper

Copper is fcc with $a=3.61$ Å and $\Theta_D=343$ K. (a) Find the atom density, the Debye wavevector $k_D$, $\hbar\omega_D$ in meV, and the averaged sound speed $\bar c$. (b) Find the molar lattice heat capacity at 5 K and at 300 K, and compare with Dulong–Petit. (c) Choose $\Theta_E$ so that the Einstein and Debye models have the same $1/T^2$ correction at high $T$. Compare the two models at 5 K and explain the difference.

Show worked solution
  1. (a) 4 atoms per cubic cell: $n_{at}=4/a^3=8.50\times10^{28}$ m⁻³. $k_D=(6\pi^2n_{at})^{1/3}=1.71\times10^{10}$ m⁻¹. $\hbar\omega_D=k_B\Theta_D=343\times0.08617$ meV $=29.6$ meV. $\bar c=k_B\Theta_D/(\hbar k_D)=2.62\times10^3$ m/s, a typical sound speed in a metal.
  2. (b) 5 K is below $\Theta_D/50=6.9$ K, so the $T^3$ law applies: $C=1944\,(5/343)^3=6.0\times10^{-3}$ J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. At 300 K the full Debye function gives $C/3R=0.938$, so $C=23.4$ J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, 94 % of $3R=24.9$.
  3. (c) Match $\frac1{12}(\Theta_E/T)^2=\frac1{20}(\Theta_D/T)^2$: $\Theta_E=\sqrt{3/5}\,\Theta_D=266$ K. At 300 K Einstein gives $C/3R=0.937$, essentially the same. At 5 K it gives $3R\,(\Theta_E/T)^2e^{-\Theta_E/T}\approx6\times10^{-19}$ J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, about $10^{-16}$ times the Debye value. With every mode at 23 meV $\gg k_BT=0.43$ meV, everything is frozen. Real copper keeps acoustic modes down to $\omega\to0$, a sphere of radius $k_BT/\hbar\bar c$ of them stays thawed, and that gives $T^3$.
Result: $n_{at}=8.50\times10^{28}$ m⁻³, $k_D=1.71\times10^{10}$ m⁻¹, $\hbar\omega_D=29.6$ meV, $\bar c=2.6$ km/s; $C(5\,\text{K})=6.0$ mJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, $C(300\,\text{K})=23.4$ J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ (94 % of Dulong–Petit); Einstein with $\Theta_E=266$ K agrees at 300 K and is too small by $\sim10^{16}$ at 5 K.
Sanity check: all values by node with CODATA constants; the full Debye integral at 5 K agrees with the $T^3$ value to $10^{-9}$. $k_D$ is about twice $\pi/a=0.87\times10^{10}$ m⁻¹, reasonable since the fcc zone reaches out to $2\pi/a$ along ⟨100⟩. $\hbar\omega_D=29.6$ meV is comparable to $k_B\cdot300\,\text{K}=25.9$ meV, consistent with Cu being nearly classical at room temperature.
