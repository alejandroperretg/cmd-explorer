Free Electron Gas

The valence electrons of a simple metal, treated as independent particles in a box with a flat potential. Counting their allowed states gives the Fermi sphere, the Fermi energy, a large pressure at T = 0, and an electronic heat capacity about 100 times below the classical value. All of transport (topic 08) is built on this picture.

Overview

Put $N$ non-interacting electrons in a cube $V=L^3$ with zero potential inside. The Schrödinger equation gives plane waves, and the boundary conditions turn $\mathbf k$ into a cubic grid with one point per $(2\pi)^3/V$. Filling that grid two electrons per point, lowest energy first, gives a sphere of radius $k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}$. From there everything follows by integrating over the sphere: $\varepsilon_F$, the density of states $D(\varepsilon)$, the energy $U_0=\tfrac35N\varepsilon_F$, the pressure $p=\tfrac23U_0/V$, and, with Fermi–Dirac smearing, $C_{el}=\tfrac{\pi^2}{2}Nk_B\,T/T_F$.

Key results to leave with: the counting argument behind $n=k_F^3/3\pi^2$ (exam Q2d), the lowest state for each boundary condition (Q2b), and why only electrons within about $k_BT$ of $\varepsilon_F$ can absorb heat. The lecture notes state most of these results without derivation, so the derivations are given in full below. Transport (topic 08) and energy bands (topic 09) build directly on this page.

Read alongside Kittel, Introduction to Solid State Physics 8th ed., ch. 6 (Energy Levels in One Dimension; Free Electron Gas in Three Dimensions; Heat Capacity of the Electron Gas; Experimental Heat Capacity of Metals), and the lecture notes pp. 39–42.

Concepts

In lecture order: the notes solve the Schrödinger equation first (2.1.2) and impose the boundary conditions second (2.1.1).

2.1.2Free-electron solution of the Schrödinger equation

Where you meet this. In 1993 IBM researchers arranged 48 iron atoms in a ring of 7 nm radius on a copper surface and imaged the electrons trapped inside with a scanning tunnelling microscope. The picture shows concentric ripples: the |ψ|² of electron standing waves in a box, the solutions of this section made visible.

What this covers. Why a metal's valence electrons can be treated as free, the Schrödinger equation with $U=0$, its normalised plane-wave solutions, and what $\mathbf k$ means for energy, momentum, velocity and wavelength. Lecture notes p39 and p41.

Why it matters. Exam Q2a asks for the equation and Q2b for the normalised lowest state. Every later result on this page and on topic 08 uses $\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}=\hbar^2k^2/2m$.

2.1.2 · The model

Why the electrons can be treated as free

Recap from 1.4.4 Metallic crystals. A metal is bound because its valence electrons delocalise over the whole crystal, which lowers their kinetic energy. The lecture starts 2.1 from exactly this picture.

Model (notes p41): $N$ electrons in a cube of side $L$, $V=L^3$, density $n=N/V$. The ion cores are smeared out into a flat potential, $U=0$ inside, and the Coulomb repulsion between electrons is neglected. Each electron then moves on its own, and the many-electron problem reduces to one electron in a box, filled $N$ times.

Which two interactions does the free-electron model drop?

The periodic potential of the ions (replaced by a flat average, restored in 2.6.2) and the electron–electron Coulomb repulsion.

Free electrons: flat potential, no electron–electron interaction, so one-electron states filled one by one.

2.1.2 · Plane waves

Plane-wave solutions

2.1.2 · The model

With $\hat{\mathbf p}=-i\hbar\nabla$ and $U=0$ inside the box:

Schrödinger equation, free electronExam Q2a
$$\begin{aligned}&-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi(\mathbf r)=\varepsilon\,\psi(\mathbf r)\\&\nabla^2=\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2+\partial_z^2\end{aligned}$$ (1)

Separate $\psi=X(x)Y(y)Z(z)$: each factor obeys $X''=-k_x^2X$, so $X\propto e^{ik_xx}$. Normalising, $\int_V|\psi|^2d^3r=1$ fixes the prefactor:

Normalised plane waveExam Q2b
$$\begin{aligned}\psi_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)&=\frac{1}{\sqrt V}\,e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}\\ \varepsilon_{\mathbf k}&=\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}\end{aligned}$$ (2)
Momentum, velocity, wavelength
$$\mathbf p=\hbar\mathbf k,\quad \mathbf v=\frac{\hbar\mathbf k}{m},\quad \lambda=\frac{2\pi}{k}$$ (3)

$\psi_{\mathbf k}$ is an eigenstate of momentum, so it is spread evenly over the whole box: $|\psi|^2=1/V$ everywhere (Model 1). Real combinations such as $\sin(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r)$ are also solutions with the same energy, but they are standing waves with $\langle\mathbf p\rangle=0$.

Dropping the $1/\sqrt V$. Q2b asks for the normalised solution, and the prefactor is where the marks are.

What is |ψ|² for the plane wave in a box of 1 nm³, and where is it largest?

$|\psi|^2=1/V=1$ nm⁻³, the same everywhere. A plane wave has no "largest" point; only standing waves ripple.

Free electrons are plane waves $V^{-1/2}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}$ with $\varepsilon=\hbar^2k^2/2m$; $\mathbf k$ fixes momentum, velocity and wavelength at once.

You can now write the free-electron Schrödinger equation and its normalised solution: exam Q2a and the first half of Q2b. Which $\mathbf k$ are allowed is the next step, 2.1.1 →.

2.1.1Boundary conditions

Where you meet this. Quantum-dot displays set their colours by particle size alone: squeeze electrons into a crystal a few nanometres across and the box levels spread apart by tenths of an eV. For free electrons the lowest hard-wall level is 1.13 eV in a 1 nm cube but only 10⁻¹⁴ eV in a 1 cm one, which is why bulk metal does not care which boundary condition you pick.

What this covers. Hard-wall and periodic (Born–von Kármán) boundary conditions, the k-grid each produces, and the lowest state of each. Lecture notes p41.

Why it matters. Exam Q2b asks for the lowest state, which depends on the boundary condition, and Q2c for the k-space volume per point. Periodic conditions return in Bloch's theorem (2.6.1 →).

2.1.1 · Two boundary conditions

Hard walls and periodic boundaries

2.1.2 · Plane waves

  • Hard walls (fixed): $\psi=0$ on the walls. Standing waves $\sin(k_xx)\sin(k_yy)\sin(k_zz)$, only the positive octant of k-space.
  • Periodic (Born–von Kármán): $\psi(x+L,y,z)=\psi(x,y,z)$, same for $y,z$. Plane waves need $e^{ik_xL}=1$; all of k-space.
Allowed k and volume per k-pointExam Q2c
$$\begin{aligned}&\text{hard walls:}\\ &\quad k_i=\frac{\pi n_i}{L},\ \ n_i=1,2,\dots\\ &\quad\Delta^3k=(\pi/L)^3\\ &\text{periodic:}\\ &\quad k_i=\frac{2\pi n_i}{L},\ \ n_i\in\mathbb Z\\ &\quad\Delta^3k=\frac{(2\pi)^3}{V}\end{aligned}$$ (4)

Both give the same number of states below a given energy ($\tfrac18$ of the sphere at $8\times$ the density), so bulk results agree for large $L$ (Model 2, in 1D). Periodic conditions are preferred: travelling waves can carry current, and they match Bloch's theorem.

Lowest stateExam Q2b
$$\begin{aligned}&\text{periodic:}\\ &\quad\psi=V^{-1/2},\ \ \varepsilon=0\\ &\text{hard walls:}\\ &\quad\psi=\Big(\frac2L\Big)^{3/2}\sin\frac{\pi x}{L}\\ &\qquad\times\sin\frac{\pi y}{L}\sin\frac{\pi z}{L}\\ &\quad\varepsilon=\frac{3\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}\end{aligned}$$ (5)

The hard-wall ground state is 1.13 eV for $L=1$ nm and $1.1\times10^{-14}$ eV for $L=1$ cm.

Calling the k-grid fcc, or mixing the two conditions (hard-wall spacing π/L with all signs of n). The grid is simple cubic either way.

Periodic conditions, L = 2 nm: k-space volume per point?

$(2\pi/L)^3=(\pi\ \text{nm}^{-1})^3=31.0$ nm⁻³.

Hard walls: standing waves, $k_i=\pi n_i/L$, $n_i\ge1$. Periodic: travelling waves, $k_i=2\pi n_i/L$, any integer. Same count of states, same bulk physics.

You can now state both boundary conditions with their k-grids and lowest states, the rest of Q2b and all of Q2c. Used in 2.1.3 → and in Bloch's theorem (2.6.1 →).

2.1.3Filling the Fermi sphere

Where you meet this. Photoemission experiments map copper's Fermi surface directly, and it is very nearly the sphere counted here, with small necks where it touches the zone faces (Kittel ch. 9). Its radius fixes the speed of the electrons that carry every current in your wiring: 1.6 × 10⁶ m/s, even at absolute zero.

What this covers. Filling the k-grid two electrons per point, the Fermi wavevector, energy, velocity and temperature, and the density of states $D(\varepsilon)$. Lecture notes p41.

Why it matters. Exam Q2d asks why $n$ and $k_F$ are related as they are, and sheet 8.1 applies it to ³He. The Fermi sphere is the starting point of transport (2.2.3 →), and $D(\varepsilon_F)$ enters the heat capacity (2.1.6).

2.1.3 · Counting

Fermi wavevector and Fermi energy

2.1.1 · Two boundary conditions; ← from 1.3.2 Reciprocal lattice

At $T=0$ the $N$ electrons occupy the lowest states, two per k-point (spin ↑↓, Pauli). Because $\varepsilon$ depends only on $|\mathbf k|$, the filled region is a sphere of radius $k_F$. Count: states inside = 2 × sphere volume / volume per point,

Counting the sphereExam-relevant derivationExam Q2d
$$N=2\cdot\frac{\tfrac43\pi k_F^3}{(2\pi)^3/V}=\frac{V k_F^3}{3\pi^2}$$ (6)
Fermi wavevector and energyExam Q2dSheet 8.1
$$\begin{aligned}k_F&=\big(3\pi^2n\big)^{1/3}\\ \varepsilon_F&=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\big(3\pi^2n\big)^{2/3}\end{aligned}$$ (7)

Also $v_F=\hbar k_F/m$ and $T_F=\varepsilon_F/k_B$ ($T_F$ is a reference scale, not the electron temperature). Cu, $n=8.45\times10^{28}$ m⁻³: $k_F=1.36\times10^{10}$ m⁻¹, $\varepsilon_F=7.0$ eV, $v_F=1.57\times10^6$ m/s, $T_F=8.1\times10^4$ K, $\lambda_F=0.46$ nm (Model 3).

Check: the Cu numbers recomputed from $n$ (node, CODATA) give $k_F=1.358\times10^{10}$ m⁻¹, $\varepsilon_F=7.02$ eV, $v_F=1.572\times10^6$ m/s, matching Kittel ch. 6 Table 1 (1.36, 7.00, 1.57).

Forgetting the spin factor 2. It makes $k_F$ too large by $2^{1/3}=1.26$; it is the most common slip in Q2d.

Doubling n: by what factor do k_F and ε_F change?

$k_F\propto n^{1/3}$ grows by $2^{1/3}=1.26$; $\varepsilon_F\propto n^{2/3}$ by $2^{2/3}=1.59$.

Two electrons per k-point fill a sphere: $n=k_F^3/3\pi^2$. The density alone fixes $k_F$, $\varepsilon_F$, $v_F$ and $T_F$.

2.1.3 · Density of states

Density of states D(ε)

2.1.3 · Counting

Replace $k_F$ by $k$: the number of states with energy below $\varepsilon$ is $N(\varepsilon)=\frac{V}{3\pi^2}\big(\frac{2m\varepsilon}{\hbar^2}\big)^{3/2}$. The same counting gave the phonon density of states (← 1.6.4). Differentiating,

Density of states, 3D free electronsSheet 8.3
$$\begin{aligned}D(\varepsilon)&=\frac{dN}{d\varepsilon}\\&=\frac{V}{2\pi^2}\Big(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\Big)^{3/2}\sqrt\varepsilon\\ D(\varepsilon_F)&=\frac{3N}{2\varepsilon_F}\end{aligned}$$ (8)

$D$ is the total DOS including spin (states per J); the lecture's $g(\varepsilon)=D/V$ is per volume. For Cu, $D(\varepsilon_F)/N=0.21$ states per eV per electron.

Mixing $D$ (whole sample) with $g=D/V$ (per volume). Check the units: $D$ is per J, $g$ per J per m³.

Why is D(ε_F) = 3N/2ε_F?

$N(\varepsilon)\propto\varepsilon^{3/2}$, so $D=dN/d\varepsilon=\frac32N(\varepsilon)/\varepsilon$; at $\varepsilon_F$, $N(\varepsilon_F)=N$.

Used in 2.8 · Pauli paramagnetism → (D(ε_F) sets the spin susceptibility of a metal)

In 3D, $D(\varepsilon)\propto\sqrt\varepsilon$ and $D(\varepsilon_F)=3N/2\varepsilon_F$: the number used by every finite-temperature result.

You can now derive $k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}$ by counting (exam Q2d) and do Sheet 8.1. Used in 2.1.4 →, and as the shifted sphere of transport (2.2.3 →).

2.1.4Total energy of the electron gas

Where you meet this. Sodium's conduction electrons carry 1.9 eV of kinetic energy each on average, even at absolute zero: about 75 times $k_BT$ at room temperature, and no amount of cooling removes it. This Pauli energy is what the metallic bond has to pay for and what pushes back when you compress a metal (2.1.5).

What this covers. The ground-state energy $U_0$ as an integral over the filled states, and the mean energy per electron. Lecture notes p41.

Why it matters. $U_0$ is the input to the pressure and bulk modulus (2.1.5), and the reference energy for the heat capacity (2.1.6).

2.1.4 · Ground-state energy

U₀ = (3/5) N ε_F

2.1.3 · Density of states

At $T=0$, add up $\varepsilon$ over the filled states, using $D=\frac{3N}{2}\varepsilon^{1/2}/\varepsilon_F^{3/2}$:

Sum over the filled statesExam-relevant derivation
$$\begin{aligned}U_0&=\int_0^{\varepsilon_F}\varepsilon\,D(\varepsilon)\,d\varepsilon\\&=\frac{3N}{2\varepsilon_F^{3/2}}\int_0^{\varepsilon_F}\varepsilon^{3/2}d\varepsilon\end{aligned}$$ (9)
Ground-state energyNa exercise
$$U_0=\frac35N\varepsilon_F$$ (10)

The mean energy per electron is $\tfrac35\varepsilon_F$, 4.2 eV for Cu, about 160 times $k_BT$ at room temperature. Filling the real k-grid shell by shell gives the same $\tfrac35$ once a few hundred electrons are in (Model 4). A classical gas would have $U=0$ at $T=0$; this energy is purely a consequence of the Pauli principle.

Using $\tfrac32k_BT$ per electron. That is the classical result; at $T=0$ it gives zero, while the Fermi gas has $\tfrac35\varepsilon_F$.

Why is the mean energy 3/5 ε_F and not ε_F/2?

More states lie near the top: $D\propto\sqrt\varepsilon$ weights high energies, so the average sits above the midpoint. A 2D gas, with constant $D$, has exactly $\varepsilon_F/2$.

The filled Fermi sphere holds $U_0=\tfrac35N\varepsilon_F$, a few eV per electron, at zero temperature.

You can now compute the ground-state energy of any free-electron metal, part (ii) of the Na exercise. Used in 2.1.5 →.

2.1.5Pressure of the electron gas

Where you meet this. A white dwarf is a star of about one solar mass squeezed to the size of the Earth. Gravity does not crush it further because its electrons resist compression exactly as in this section: the degeneracy pressure of a Fermi gas, no heat required.

What this covers. The pressure $p=-\partial U_0/\partial V$ of the electron gas and its bulk modulus, compared with measured metals. Lecture notes p41.

Why it matters. It is a two-line consequence of $U_0$ and a fair exam exercise (Na exercise). It explains why alkali metals are as soft as they are.

2.1.5 · Degeneracy pressure

Pressure and bulk modulus

2.1.4 · Ground-state energy

At fixed $N$, $\varepsilon_F\propto(N/V)^{2/3}$, so $U_0=\tfrac35N\varepsilon_F\propto V^{-2/3}$ and

Pressure at T = 0Na exercise
$$\begin{aligned}p&=-\frac{\partial U_0}{\partial V}\Big|_N=\frac23\frac{U_0}{V}\\&=\frac25\,n\,\varepsilon_F\end{aligned}$$ (11)
Recap from 1.5.5 Bulk modulus. The bulk modulus is $B=-V\,dp/dV$, the stiffness against uniform compression; measured values are in Kittel ch. 3 Table 3.
Bulk modulus of the electron gas
$$B=-V\frac{\partial p}{\partial V}=\frac53p=\frac23n\varepsilon_F$$ (12)

For Cu, $p=38$ GPa and $B=63$ GPa at $T=0$ (Kittel ch. 6 problem 2). This degeneracy pressure is not thermal: squeezing the box raises every $k$ and with it the energy. In a real metal it is balanced by the electron–ion attraction. Against Kittel's measured bulk moduli (ch. 3 Table 3) the free-electron $B$ is close for the alkalis (K: 3.2 vs 3.2 GPa; Na: 9.2 vs 6.8 GPa) but misses Cu (63 vs 137 GPa, the filled d shells add stiffness) and Al (225 vs 72 GPa) (Model 5).

Writing $p=\tfrac23U_0$ without the $1/V$: pressure is energy per volume.

Why B = (5/3)p?

$p\propto V^{-5/3}$ at fixed $N$, so $-V\,\partial p/\partial V=\tfrac53p$.

The Fermi gas pushes outward with $p=\tfrac25n\varepsilon_F$, tens of GPa, and has $B=\tfrac53p$: right for alkali metals, off for metals with d electrons or several valence electrons.

You can now compute the pressure and bulk modulus of the electron gas, part (ii) of the Na exercise, and say when the free-electron estimate of $B$ can be trusted.

2.1.6Specific heat of the electron gas

Where you meet this. Classical physics predicted that a metal's free electrons should add $\tfrac32R$ per mole to the heat capacity, 50 % on top of the lattice's $3R$. Nobody ever measured it. The Pauli principle explains the missing heat: at room temperature only about 1 % of copper's electrons can take any. Below a few kelvin the electrons win over the phonons, and that linear term is how experimenters measure effective masses.

What this covers. The Fermi–Dirac distribution, the Sommerfeld result $C_{el}=\frac{\pi^2}{2}Nk_BT/T_F$, and the low-temperature heat capacity $\gamma T+AT^3$ of a metal. Lecture notes p41–42.

Why it matters. Sheets 8.2 and 8.3 use the Fermi–Dirac distribution; sheet 9.1 is the electron–phonon cross-over. $C_{el}$ is the input to the Wiedemann–Franz law (2.5 →).

2.1.6 · Fermi–Dirac distribution

Occupation at T > 0

2.1.3 · Density of states

At temperature $T$ the probability that a state of energy $\varepsilon$ is occupied is

Fermi–Dirac distributionSheet 8.2Sheet 8.3
$$f(\varepsilon,T)=\frac{1}{e^{(\varepsilon-\mu)/k_BT}+1}$$ (13)

It is a step at $T=0$, rounded over a width of a few $k_BT$ for $T>0$, with $f(\mu)=\tfrac12$. $\mu$ is fixed by $N=\int_0^\infty f\,D\,d\varepsilon$. In 3D it barely moves, $\mu\approx\varepsilon_F[1-\tfrac{\pi^2}{12}(T/T_F)^2]$, because $k_BT\ll\varepsilon_F$ in every metal ($k_BT=25.85$ meV at 300 K, 0.4 % of Cu's $\varepsilon_F$). $D(\varepsilon)$ counts the states, $f$ says which are filled; the energy is $U=\int\varepsilon\,D\,f\,d\varepsilon$ and needs both.

Setting μ = ε_F at all T. It holds in a metal to 10⁻⁵, but not for ³He near 1 K or at high T, where μ falls (sheet 8.2, Model 6).

What is f at ε = μ + 2k_BT?

$1/(e^2+1)=0.12$; at $\mu-2k_BT$ it is $0.88$. Almost all the change sits within $\pm2k_BT$.

Used in 2.6 · Model 5, E(k) with Fermi–Dirac filling →

$f$ is a step rounded over a few $k_BT$ around μ; μ is set by conserving N and stays at $\varepsilon_F$ in metals.

2.1.6 · Sommerfeld heat capacity

Why Cel ∝ T

2.1.6 · Fermi–Dirac distribution

Estimate. Heating to $T$ can only lift electrons within about $k_BT$ of $\varepsilon_F$ into empty states just above; deeper electrons have no empty state within reach. That is a fraction $\sim k_BT/\varepsilon_F$ of $N$, each gaining $\sim k_BT$: $U-U_0\sim Nk_BT\cdot k_BT/\varepsilon_F\propto T^2$, so $C\propto T$.

Derivation (Sommerfeld, Kittel eqs. 29–36). $N$ is fixed, so $\int D\,\partial_Tf\,d\varepsilon=0$, and $\varepsilon_F$ times this can be subtracted:

Only the shell near ε_F counts
$$\begin{aligned}C_{el}&=\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\\&=\int_0^\infty(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_F)\,D(\varepsilon)\\&\qquad\times\frac{\partial f}{\partial T}\,d\varepsilon\\&\approx D(\varepsilon_F)\,k_B^2T\\&\quad\times\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{x^2e^x\,dx}{(e^x+1)^2}\end{aligned}$$ (14)

Here $\partial_Tf$ is peaked within a few $k_BT$ of $\varepsilon_F$, so $D$ is taken out at $\varepsilon_F$, $\mu\to\varepsilon_F$, $x=(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_F)/k_BT$, and the lower limit $-\varepsilon_F/k_BT\to-\infty$. The integral is $\pi^2/3$:

Electronic heat capacitySheet 9.1Sheet 8.3
$$\begin{aligned}C_{el}&=\frac{\pi^2}{3}D(\varepsilon_F)\,k_B^2T\\&=\frac{\pi^2}{2}\,Nk_B\,\frac{T}{T_F}\equiv\gamma T\end{aligned}$$ (15)

$N$ counts electrons. For Cu at 300 K, $C_{el}$ is 1.2 % of the classical $\tfrac32Nk_B$.

Notation: the lecture writes $c_v=\frac{\pi^2}{2}\frac{k_BT}{\varepsilon_F}Nk_B$. Here lower-case $c_v$ is the total heat capacity (it carries $N$), unlike the per-volume $c_v$ of the phonon chapter. The p42 line "$c_v=\gamma T+AT$" is missing the cube on $T$ (p41 has it).

Using the number of atoms for N in a polyvalent metal. Al has three conduction electrons per atom, so its γ per mole is 3 × larger (sheet 9.1).

Cu at 30 K: what fraction of the classical 3/2 Nk_B is C_el?

$\frac{\pi^2}{3}\frac{T}{T_F}=\frac{\pi^2}{3}\cdot\frac{30}{8.1\times10^4}=1.2\times10^{-3}$, ten times less than at 300 K.

Only the $k_BT$-thin shell at $\varepsilon_F$ absorbs heat, so $C_{el}=\frac{\pi^2}{3}D(\varepsilon_F)k_B^2T$: linear in T and about 1 % of classical at room temperature.

2.1.6 · Electrons plus phonons

C = γT + AT³ in a real metal

2.1.6 · Sommerfeld heat capacity

Recap from 1.7.1 Lattice specific heat. Below about Θ_D/50 the phonons follow the Debye law $C_{ph}=AT^3$ with $A=\frac{12\pi^4}{5}R/\Theta_D^3$ per mole of atoms.
Low-temperature heat capacity of a metalSheet 9.1
$$\frac CT=\gamma+AT^2$$ (16)

A plot of $C/T$ against $T^2$ is a straight line with intercept $\gamma$ (Model 7). Measured $\gamma$ exceeds the free-electron value by a thermal effective mass $m_{th}/m=\gamma_{obs}/\gamma_{free}$: 1.38 (Cu), 1.26 (Na), 1.48 (Al) from Kittel ch. 6 Table 2, due to the lattice potential and to electron–phonon and electron–electron interactions.

Comparing γT and AT³ per mole of electrons on one side and per mole of atoms on the other. Use one basis, moles of atoms, with $N=ZN_A$ electrons.

At the cross-over T×, how does C/T compare with γ?

$AT_\times^2=\gamma$, so $C/T=2\gamma$: twice the intercept.

Electrons dominate below a few kelvin ($T_\times=\sqrt{\gamma/A}$), phonons above; the $C/T$ vs $T^2$ plot separates them.

Try

Sheet 8 · Problem 1 · 4 pts

Liquid He³

³He has spin 1/2 and is a fermion. The density of liquid ³He is 0.081 g/cm³ at T → 0 K. Calculate the Fermi energy ε_F and the Fermi temperature T_F.

Method

Treat the atoms as a free Fermi gas of spin-½ particles: two per k-point, so the 3D formulas of 2.1.3 apply unchanged, with the atomic mass in place of $m_e$. Number density from mass density, then $k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}$, $\varepsilon_F=\hbar^2k_F^2/2m_3$, $T_F=\varepsilon_F/k_B$.

Assumption: atomic mass of ³He $m_3=3.016\,$u (standard value, e.g. CRC Handbook). Interactions between the atoms are neglected, as in Kittel ch. 6 problem 5, of which this is a copy.
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. $m_3=3.016\times1.6605\times10^{-27}\,\text{kg}=5.008\times10^{-27}$ kg; $\rho=0.081$ g/cm³ $=81$ kg/m³.
  2. $n=\rho/m_3=1.617\times10^{28}$ m⁻³.
  3. $k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}=7.82\times10^{9}$ m⁻¹.
  4. $\varepsilon_F=\dfrac{\hbar^2k_F^2}{2m_3}=6.80\times10^{-23}\,\text{J}=4.24\times10^{-4}$ eV (1 eV $=1.602\times10^{-19}$ J).
  5. $T_F=\varepsilon_F/k_B=4.9$ K.
Result: $\varepsilon_F\approx4.2\times10^{-4}$ eV, $T_F\approx4.9$ K.
Sanity check: scaling from Cu, $\varepsilon_F\propto n^{2/3}/m$: $7.02\,\text{eV}\times(1.617/8.45)^{2/3}\times m_e/m_3=4.24\times10^{-4}$ eV (node), the same value by a second route. The mass ratio $m_3/m_e\approx5500$ explains why $T_F$ is in kelvin, not $10^4$ K. Using $m_e$ by mistake gives 2.3 eV.
Sheet 8 · Problem 2 · 4 pts

Chemical potential in 2D

Show that the chemical potential of a quantum electron gas in two dimensions is $\mu(T)=k_BT\ln\!\Big[\exp\!\Big(\dfrac{\pi\hbar^2}{m_ek_BT}\dfrac NA\Big)-1\Big]$ with N electrons per area A. Hint: $D(\varepsilon)=A\,m_e/(\pi\hbar^2)$.

Method

Fix $\mu$ by particle conservation, $N=\int_0^\infty f(\varepsilon)D\,d\varepsilon$. In 2D, $D$ is constant, so the Fermi integral can be done in closed form and inverted.

Show worked solution

Steps

  1. Where the hint comes from: in 2D, $N=2\cdot\pi k^2/(2\pi/L)^2=Ak^2/2\pi$ and $\varepsilon=\hbar^2k^2/2m_e$, so $N(\varepsilon)=Am_e\varepsilon/\pi\hbar^2$ and $D=dN/d\varepsilon=Am_e/\pi\hbar^2$.
  2. $N=\dfrac{Am_e}{\pi\hbar^2}\displaystyle\int_0^\infty\frac{d\varepsilon}{e^{(\varepsilon-\mu)/k_BT}+1}$.
  3. Multiply numerator and denominator by $e^{-(\varepsilon-\mu)/k_BT}$: the integrand is $-k_BT\,\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\ln\!\big(1+e^{-(\varepsilon-\mu)/k_BT}\big)$, so the integral is $k_BT\ln\!\big(1+e^{\mu/k_BT}\big)$.
  4. $\dfrac NA=\dfrac{m_ek_BT}{\pi\hbar^2}\ln\!\big(1+e^{\mu/k_BT}\big)\;\Rightarrow\;e^{\mu/k_BT}=\exp\!\Big(\dfrac{\pi\hbar^2}{m_ek_BT}\dfrac NA\Big)-1$.
  5. Take the log and multiply by $k_BT$: the stated result.
Result: $\mu(T)=k_BT\ln\big[\exp(\pi\hbar^2n/m_ek_BT)-1\big]$, $n=N/A$. With $\varepsilon_F=\pi\hbar^2n/m_e$ this is $\mu=\varepsilon_F+k_BT\ln\big(1-e^{-T_F/T}\big)$.
Sanity check: $T\to0$: $\mu\to\pi\hbar^2n/m_e$, the 2D Fermi energy from step 1 with $N(\varepsilon_F)=N$. High $T$: $\mu\approx k_BT\ln(\pi\hbar^2n/m_ek_BT)<0$, the classical result. Numerically (node), integrating $f\,D$ with this μ returns exactly N at $T/T_F=0.1$ and 1. The deviation from $\varepsilon_F$ is exponentially small, so μ stays flat up to about $0.2\,T_F$ (Model 6, lower right).
Sheet 8 · Problem 3 · 4 pts

Entropy of an electron gas

From $C_V=(\partial U/\partial T)_V=T(\partial S/\partial T)_V$, deduce

Entropy to be shown
$$\begin{aligned}S=-k_B\int_0^\infty\big[&f_{FD}\ln f_{FD}\\&+(1-f_{FD})\\&\times\ln(1-f_{FD})\big]\\&\times D(\varepsilon)\,d\varepsilon\end{aligned}$$

with $f_{FD}=1/[1+e^{(\varepsilon-\mu)/k_BT}]$, $U=\int_0^\infty f_{FD}\,\varepsilon\,D\,d\varepsilon$ and $D(\varepsilon)=\frac{V}{2\pi^2}(2m/\hbar^2)^{3/2}\varepsilon^{1/2}$.

Method

Show that the $T$-derivative of the proposed $S$ equals $\frac1T\,\partial U/\partial T$, then check that both sides agree at $T=0$. Two facts do the work: $\ln\frac{1-f}{f}=\frac{\varepsilon-\mu}{k_BT}$, and $N$ is fixed at constant $V$.

Show worked solution

Steps

  1. Let $s(f)=-[f\ln f+(1-f)\ln(1-f)]$. Then $\dfrac{ds}{df}=\ln\dfrac{1-f}{f}=\dfrac{\varepsilon-\mu}{k_BT}$.
  2. $D$ does not depend on $T$ at fixed $V$, so $\dfrac{\partial S}{\partial T}=k_B\displaystyle\int\frac{ds}{df}\frac{\partial f}{\partial T}D\,d\varepsilon=\frac1T\int(\varepsilon-\mu)\frac{\partial f}{\partial T}D\,d\varepsilon$.
  3. $N=\int fD\,d\varepsilon$ is constant, so $\int\frac{\partial f}{\partial T}D\,d\varepsilon=0$ (the $T$-dependence of $\mu$ sits inside $\partial f/\partial T$). The $\mu$ term drops out.
  4. What remains is $\dfrac{\partial S}{\partial T}=\dfrac1T\displaystyle\int\varepsilon\frac{\partial f}{\partial T}D\,d\varepsilon=\frac1T\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}=\frac{C_V}{T}$, as the identity requires.
  5. Integration constant: at $T=0$ every $f$ is 0 or 1, and $0\ln0=0$, so the formula gives $S(0)=0$, the third law. Then $S(T)=\int_0^TC_V/T'\,dT'$ is reproduced at all $T$.
Result: $S=-k_B\int_0^\infty[f\ln f+(1-f)\ln(1-f)]D\,d\varepsilon$. Each one-electron state adds the two-outcome entropy of being occupied with probability $f$ (like the site entropy of a lattice gas), so only the shell within a few $k_BT$ of $\mu$ contributes. At low $T$, $S=\frac{\pi^2}{3}D(\varepsilon_F)k_B^2T=C_{el}$.
Sanity check: numerically (node, 3D gas, μ(T) solved), at $T/T_F=0.2$ the formula gives $S/Nk_B=0.9398$ and $\int_0^TC/T'\,dT'$ gives 0.9396. At $T/T_F=0.01$ it gives 0.04934 against $\frac{\pi^2}{2}T/T_F=0.04935$.
Sheet 9 · Problem 1 · 4 pts

Heat capacity cross-over electron to phonon system

← from 1.7.1.2 T³ law (the phonon term)

In metals the electrons give a heat capacity ∝ T at low temperature, the phonons one ∝ T³. Find the cross-over temperature where both are equal for (i) Cu, (ii) Na, (iii) Al, from open-source material data. State data sources, assumptions and formulas.

Method

Per mole of atoms, the electrons give $\gamma T$ with Kittel eq. 36, $C_{el}=\frac12\pi^2Nk_BT/T_F$, where $N$ is the number of electrons: $N=ZN_A$ for valence $Z$. The phonons give the Debye law $AT^3$ with $N_A$ atoms. Set them equal:

Cross-over temperature
$$\begin{aligned}\gamma&=\frac{\pi^2}{2}\frac{ZR}{T_F}\\ A&=\frac{12\pi^4}{5}\frac{R}{\Theta_D^3}\\ T_\times&=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{A}}=\sqrt{\frac{5Z\,\Theta_D^3}{24\pi^2\,T_F}}\end{aligned}$$
Assumptions and sources: free-electron γ from $T_F$ (Kittel ch. 6 Table 1), with $Z$ = 1, 1, 3. $\Theta_D$ is the low-temperature value from Kittel ch. 5 Table 1. The T³ law holds because $T_\times<\Theta_D/50$ in all three cases (Kittel's criterion for a clean T³ regime). The alternative with measured γ (Kittel ch. 6 Table 2) is given too. Other handbooks list slightly different $\Theta_D$; since $T_\times\propto\Theta_D^{3/2}$, a 5 % change moves $T_\times$ by about 7.5 %.

  4. With measured γ, $T_\times=\sqrt{\gamma_{obs}/A}$: 3.80, 1.67, 7.38 K.
Result (free electrons): Cu ≈ 3.2 K, Na ≈ 1.5 K, Al ≈ 6.1 K. With measured γ: 3.8 K, 1.7 K, 7.4 K.

Exam 2022 · Q2 · model answer

Quantum electron gas

a) Schrödinger equation for a single electron. b) Normalised solution for a single electron with the lowest kinetic energy. c) In reciprocal space the k-vectors form a simple cubic grid: what is the k-space volume per k-point? d) Why are electron density and Fermi wavevector related as they are?

Show model answer
  1. a) Free electron, $U=0$ in the box $V=L^3$: $-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi(\mathbf r)=\varepsilon\psi(\mathbf r)$.
  2. b) State the boundary condition first. Periodic: $\psi_{\mathbf k}=V^{-1/2}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}$, $\varepsilon=\hbar^2k^2/2m$; the lowest state is $\mathbf k=0$, $\psi=1/\sqrt V$, $\varepsilon=0$. The prefactor follows from $\int_V|\psi|^2d^3r=1$. Hard walls: $\psi=(2/L)^{3/2}\sin\frac{\pi x}{L}\sin\frac{\pi y}{L}\sin\frac{\pi z}{L}$, $\varepsilon=3\pi^2\hbar^2/2mL^2$.
  3. c) Periodic BC: $e^{ik_iL}=1\Rightarrow k_i=2\pi n_i/L$, a simple cubic grid of spacing $2\pi/L$ (not fcc). Volume per point: $(2\pi/L)^3=8\pi^3/V$.
  4. d) At $T=0$ the electrons fill the lowest states, two per k-point (Pauli, spin ↑↓), and $\varepsilon\propto k^2$ makes the filled region a sphere. Counting: $N=2\cdot\frac43\pi k_F^3/(8\pi^3/V)$, so $n=N/V=k_F^3/3\pi^2$, i.e. $k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}$.
Points lost in practice: spin factor 2, a missing normalisation constant, mixing the two boundary conditions, calling the grid fcc.

Show worked solution
