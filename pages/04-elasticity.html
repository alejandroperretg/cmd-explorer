Elasticity

How a crystal deforms under small forces: strain, stress, and the linear law that ties them together through a 6×6 matrix of elastic constants. Cubic symmetry cuts that matrix down to three numbers, C11, C12 and C44. These three give the bulk modulus, the direction-dependent Young's modulus, the stability conditions and, later, the sound speeds that set the Debye temperature.

Overview

Section 1.5 treats a crystal as a continuous elastic body. Its size is much larger than the lattice constant, and the deformations are small enough to be linear. A strain tensor ε describes how the crystal deforms (1.5.1), a stress tensor σ describes the forces per unit area inside it (1.5.2), and Hooke's law σ = C e links the two (1.5.3). Because the elastic energy density u = ½ eᵀC e (1.5.4) exists, C is symmetric, which leaves 21 constants. Cubic symmetry reduces these to C11, C12 and C44, and the bulk modulus is B = (C11 + 2C12)/3 (1.5.5).

Key results to leave with: the Voigt bookkeeping with its factor 2 in the shears; σ = C e and e = S σ with S = C⁻¹; u = ½ eᵀC e; the cubic C matrix and its three eigenvalues C11 + 2C12, C11 − C12 and C44; B; the stability conditions (sheet 5.2); and E and ν along [100] and [110] (sheet 5.1). The 2022 exam had no question on elasticity, so the exam angle below gives an exercise in its style.

Read alongside Kittel, Introduction to Solid State Physics 8th ed., ch. 3 (second half: "Analysis of Elastic Strains" to "Elastic Waves in Cubic Crystals"), and the lecture notes pp. 18–25. Directions such as [110] are as on page 01. The sound waves that follow from C are on page 05.

Concepts

1.5.1Elastic strain in a crystal

Where you meet this. The strain gauges glued to bridges, aircraft wings and the load cell of a kitchen scale measure exactly this ε: a thin metal foil whose resistance changes by about twice its strain (gauge factor ≈ 2), good to about 10⁻⁶. Turn the beam rigidly and the gauge reads zero, because a rotation is not a strain.

What this covers. How a homogeneous deformation maps lattice vectors, why only the symmetric part of ε is strain, the dilation δ = tr ε, and the six Voigt components with their factor 2. Notes pp. 18–19; Kittel "Analysis of Elastic Strains", "Dilation".

Why it matters. Every later formula is written in the Voigt vector e, so the factor 2 in the shears decides whether sheet 5.1(ii) and the energy density come out right. δ is what the bulk modulus (1.5.5) measures.

1.5.1 · Strain

Strain: how a crystal deforms

Under a homogeneous deformation, every lattice vector is mapped by the same 3×3 matrix:

Deformation of a lattice vector
$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf R\to\mathbf R'&=\mathbf R+\underline{\underline\varepsilon}\,\mathbf R,\\ \underline{\underline\varepsilon}&=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{xx}&\varepsilon_{xy}&\varepsilon_{xz}\\ \varepsilon_{yx}&\varepsilon_{yy}&\varepsilon_{yz}\\ \varepsilon_{zx}&\varepsilon_{zy}&\varepsilon_{zz}\end{pmatrix},\\ |\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}|&\ll1 .\end{aligned}$$ (1)

The $\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}$ are dimensionless. The unit axes become $\mathbf x'=\hat{\mathbf x}+\underline{\underline\varepsilon}\,\hat{\mathbf x}=(1+\varepsilon_{xx},\,\varepsilon_{yx},\,\varepsilon_{zx})^T$ and similarly for $\mathbf y'$ and $\mathbf z'$. The new axes are no longer orthogonal and no longer of unit length. "Homogeneous" means the strained region is much larger than the lattice constant, so the crystal can be treated as a continuum. Kittel also allows a displacement field that varies in space, with $e_{xx}=\partial u_x/\partial x$, but the course uses only homogeneous strain.

Any matrix splits into a symmetric and an antisymmetric part. To first order, the antisymmetric part is a rigid rotation: it turns the crystal without changing any length or angle, so it stores no energy. Only the symmetric part is strain. With rotations excluded, $\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}=\varepsilon_{\beta\alpha}$, which leaves 6 independent components.

Notes slips and conventions: p18 writes $\Delta\mathbf R=\mathbf R-\mathbf R'$. The displacement is $\mathbf R'-\mathbf R=\underline{\underline\varepsilon}\,\mathbf R$. The notes and board 08 let ε act on column vectors, so the first index is the component and the second is the axis. Kittel writes $\mathbf x'=(1+\epsilon_{xx})\hat{\mathbf x}+\epsilon_{xy}\hat{\mathbf y}+\dots$, which transposes the labels. Once ε is symmetric the two agree, so this page uses the course convention.
Is $\varepsilon_{xy}=-\theta$, $\varepsilon_{yx}=+\theta$ (all else 0) a strain?

No. It is the antisymmetric part only, a rotation by θ about z. To first order $|\mathbf x'|=|\mathbf y'|=1$ and $\mathbf x'\cdot\mathbf y'=0$; lengths change only at order θ². Model 1's rotation slider shows this: the cube turns, and the stress bars and u stay at zero.

Strain is the symmetric part of the deformation matrix, which has 6 numbers. The antisymmetric part is a rotation and costs no energy.

1.5.1 · Dilation and Voigt

Dilation and the six strain components

1.5.1 · Strain

The unit cube becomes a parallelepiped with volume

Dilation
$$\begin{gathered}\begin{aligned}V'&=\mathbf x'\cdot(\mathbf y'\times\mathbf z')\\&=\det(1+\underline{\underline\varepsilon})\\&=1+\operatorname{tr}\underline{\underline\varepsilon}+O(\varepsilon^2),\end{aligned}\\[4pt] \delta\equiv\frac{V'-V}{V}=\varepsilon_{xx}+\varepsilon_{yy}+\varepsilon_{zz}.\end{gathered}$$ (2)

The six independent components are collected into a vector (Voigt notation), cycling 1 = xx, 2 = yy, 3 = zz, 4 = yz, 5 = zx, 6 = xy:

Strain components (Voigt)Sheet 5.1
$$\begin{aligned}e_1&=\varepsilon_{xx}, & e_4&=\varepsilon_{yz}+\varepsilon_{zy},\\ e_2&=\varepsilon_{yy}, & e_5&=\varepsilon_{zx}+\varepsilon_{xz},\\ e_3&=\varepsilon_{zz}, & e_6&=\varepsilon_{xy}+\varepsilon_{yx}.\end{aligned}$$ (3)

$e_1,e_2,e_3$ are relative length changes of the axes. $e_4,e_5,e_6$ are changes of the angle between two axes, in radians: $e_6=\mathbf x'\cdot\mathbf y'+O(\varepsilon^2)$, the cosine of an angle that was 90°.

Putting the tensor component into the Voigt vector. For symmetric ε, $e_6=2\varepsilon_{xy}$, not $\varepsilon_{xy}$. These are "engineering" shears. Mixing up the two gives factor-2 errors in the energy density and in sheet 5.1(ii). The stress components $\sigma_4,\sigma_5,\sigma_6$ do not carry this factor.

$\varepsilon_{xy}=\varepsilon_{yx}=0.01$, nothing else. What are $e_6$, the angle change, and δ?

$e_6=0.02$, so the angle between x′ and y′ closes by 0.02 rad = 1.15°. δ = 0: a pure shear does not change the volume to first order.

The relative volume change is the trace of ε. The six-vector e uses doubled off-diagonal elements, which are the angle changes.

You can now split a deformation into strain and rotation, get the volume change from tr ε, and write the Voigt vector e with the factor 2 in the shears. That is the set-up step of Sheet 5.1(ii), where $e_6=2\varepsilon_{xy}$.

1.5.2Stress

Where you meet this. At the bottom of the Mariana Trench, 11 km down, the sea presses on every face of a submersible with about 110 MPa: σ₁ = σ₂ = σ₃ ≈ −110 MPa and no shear at all. Even so, a copper part shrinks in volume by only 0.08 %, as you will compute in 1.5.5.

What this covers. The nine force-per-area components Xx … Zz, why zero torque leaves six, and their Voigt labels σ₁ … σ₆. Notes p. 20; Kittel "Stress Components".

Why it matters. Sheet 5.1 starts by writing the applied tension as a stress vector σ. Stress times strain is the energy density of 1.5.4.

1.5.2 · Stress

Stress components

1.5.1 · Dilation and Voigt

Stress is force per unit area. $X_y$ is the x component of the force on a unit area whose normal is $\hat{\mathbf y}$, and similarly for the other eight combinations. The zero-torque condition (the cube does not start to spin) forces $X_y=Y_x$, $Y_z=Z_y$ and $Z_x=X_z$, so stress is also symmetric with 6 components:

Stress components (Voigt), in N/m²
$$\begin{aligned}\sigma_1&=X_x, & \sigma_4&=Y_z=Z_y,\\ \sigma_2&=Y_y, & \sigma_5&=Z_x=X_z,\\ \sigma_3&=Z_z, & \sigma_6&=X_y=Y_x.\end{aligned}$$ (4)
Notes slip (p20): the torque line reads "$Y_x=Z_y$"; it should be $Y_z=Z_y$, as the notes' own $\sigma_4$ line has it.
Why is N/m² the same unit as J/m³, and why does that matter here?

N/m² = N·m/m³ = J/m³. Stress times a dimensionless strain is an energy per volume, which is exactly the elastic energy density of 1.5.4.

Six stress components, with no factor 2. $X_y$ means a force along x acting on the face whose normal is y.

You can now write an applied load as the six-vector σ: tension along [100] is $(\sigma,0,0,0,0,0)$, and along [110] it is $(\tfrac\sigma2,\tfrac\sigma2,0,0,0,\tfrac\sigma2)$. That is the first line of both parts of Sheet 5.1.

1.5.3Hooke's law

Where you meet this. The accelerometer in your phone is a tiny silicon mass on silicon springs, and its reading relies on deflection being linear in force. In a crystal the spring constant depends on direction: silicon's Young's modulus is 130 GPa along [100] but 169 GPa along [110], so MEMS designers need to know which way the wafer is cut.

What this covers. The linear law σ = C e with the 6×6 stiffness matrix C, and its inverse e = S σ with the compliance S. Notes pp. 21–22; Kittel "Elastic Compliance and Stiffness Constants".

Why it matters. Sheet 5.1 is e = S σ applied to a tensile test. Two slips sit here: the notes call S the "stiffness" on p25, and S₁₁ is not 1/C₁₁.

1.5.3 · Stiffness and compliance

Hooke's law: stiffness C and compliance S

1.5.1 · Dilation and Voigt and 1.5.2 · Stress

For small strains, stress is linear in strain, like a spring with $F=kx$. As tensors, $\sigma_{\alpha\beta}=\sum_{\gamma\delta}C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\,\varepsilon_{\gamma\delta}$, a rank-4 object with 81 entries. With the Voigt vectors, the same law becomes a 6×6 matrix equation:

Hooke's law, matrix formSheet 5.1
$$\begin{aligned}\underline\sigma&=\underline{\underline C}\,\underline e,\\ \underline e&=\underline{\underline S}\,\underline\sigma,\qquad \underline{\underline S}=\underline{\underline C}^{-1}.\end{aligned}$$ (5)

$C_{ij}$ are the elastic stiffness constants, in N/m² (like k). $S_{ij}$ are the elastic compliance constants, in m²/N (like 1/k). The index pairs follow the Voigt cycle: $C_{xx,xx}=C_{11}$, $C_{xx,zz}=C_{13}$, $C_{yz,yz}=C_{44}$.

Calling S the stiffness. Notes p25 and the board label $S=C^{-1}$ "stiffness constants". p22 and Kittel have it right: C is the stiffness, S is the compliance. A stiff crystal has large C and small S.

Taking $S_{11}=1/C_{11}$. The inverse is of the whole matrix, so $S_{11}$ depends on $C_{12}$ too. For Cu, $1/C_{11}=0.59$ but $S_{11}=1.50$, both in $10^{-11}$ m²/N (eq. (9) below).

Which law gives the strain when the stress is known, as in a tensile test?

$\underline e=\underline{\underline S}\,\underline\sigma$. In a tensile test you control the force, so σ is known and has one non-zero component, and S gives all six strains at once. That is why sheet 5.1 is easiest in terms of S.

σ = C e and e = S σ with S = C⁻¹. C is the stiffness, S the compliance, and S is the inverse of the whole matrix, not entry by entry.

You can now pick the right direction of Hooke's law: e = S σ when the force is given, σ = C e when the shape is held. That is the method of Sheet 5.1 and part (c) of the Al exercise.

1.5.4Elastic energy density

Where you meet this. Materials databases such as the Materials Project compute the full C matrix of thousands of crystals. Before a predicted structure is trusted, they check that C is positive definite, so that no strain lowers the energy. That check is the stability test of sheet 5.2.

What this covers. u = ½ eᵀC e, why it makes C symmetric (21 constants), how cubic symmetry of u cuts C down to C₁₁, C₁₂, C₄₄ (the notes number this part 1.5.5), and why stability means C is positive definite. Notes pp. 22–25; Kittel "Elastic Energy Density", "Elastic Stiffness Constants of Cubic Crystals", Problem 14.

Why it matters. Sheet 5.2 is this section: positive u for every strain. The cubic C (7) and its inverse (9) are the input to every number on sheet 5, to the bulk modulus, and to the sound speeds of 1.6.

1.5.4 · Energy density

Elastic energy density

1.5.3 · Stiffness and compliance

Straining by $d\underline e$ against the stress costs work $du=\underline\sigma\cdot d\underline e=(\underline{\underline C}\,\underline e)\cdot d\underline e$ per unit volume, just as $dU=kx\,dx$ for a spring. Integrating from zero strain:

Elastic energy densitySheet 5.2
$$\begin{aligned}u=\frac UV&=\frac12\,\underline e^{T}\underline{\underline C}\,\underline e\quad[\mathrm{J/m^3}]\\&=\frac12\sum_{i,j=1}^{6}C_{ij}\,e_ie_j,\\ \sigma_i&=\frac{\partial u}{\partial e_i}.\end{aligned}$$ (6)

So $C_{ij}=\partial^2u/\partial e_i\partial e_j$ is the Hessian of u, and it is symmetric. That leaves 6 diagonal and 15 off-diagonal entries, $6\cdot7/2=21$ independent constants out of 36. The engineering shears of 1.5.1 are what make $\underline\sigma\cdot\underline e$ equal the full tensor sum $\sum_{\alpha\beta}\sigma_{\alpha\beta}\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}$, in which each shear appears twice.

Board 11 arithmetic: it gets "21 d.o.f." from $6(6-1)/2$, which is 15, the off-diagonal count alone. The result 21 is right, and the arithmetic is $6+15=6\cdot7/2$.
Cu is stretched 1 % along x with the sides held fixed ($e_1=0.01$, all other $e_i=0$). Give σ₁, σ₂ and u.

$\sigma_1=C_{11}e_1=1.68$ GPa, $\sigma_2=\sigma_3=C_{12}e_1=1.21$ GPa (the side walls must push to stop the crystal narrowing), $u=\tfrac12C_{11}e_1^2=8.4$ MJ/m³. Model 1 reproduces these with e₁ = 0.01.

u = ½ eᵀC e is a quadratic form, and C is its Hessian. Symmetry of C (21 constants) follows from u existing at all.

1.5.4 · Cubic crystals

Cubic crystals: three constants (notes §1.5.5)

1.5.4 · Energy density; cubic symmetry operations on page 01

u must not change under any symmetry operation of the crystal. Two kinds of operation do all the work:

  • 120° rotation about a body diagonal [111]: x → y → z → x. It cycles $e_1\to e_2\to e_3$ and $e_4\to e_5\to e_6$, so $C_{11}=C_{22}=C_{33}$, $C_{12}=C_{23}=C_{31}$ and $C_{44}=C_{55}=C_{66}$.
  • 180° rotation about a cube axis, e.g. about z: x → −x, y → −y. It flips the signs of $e_4$ and $e_5$ and leaves $e_1,e_2,e_3,e_6$ unchanged. Any cross term such as $e_1e_4$ or $e_5e_6$ changes sign under one of these rotations, so its coefficient must vanish. For $e_4e_5$, the 180° rotation about x does the job.
Cubic stiffness matrixSheet 5.1Sheet 5.2
$$\begin{gathered}\underline{\underline C}_{\rm cubic}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathsf N&0\\0&C_{44}\mathbb 1_3\end{pmatrix},\\ \mathsf N=\begin{pmatrix}C_{11}&C_{12}&C_{12}\\C_{12}&C_{11}&C_{12}\\C_{12}&C_{12}&C_{11}\end{pmatrix}\end{gathered}$$ (7)

This is the full 6×6 matrix in 3×3 blocks. N acts on the normal strains $e_1,e_2,e_3$, $C_{44}\mathbb 1_3$ (the 3×3 unit matrix times $C_{44}$) acts on the shears $e_4,e_5,e_6$, and each 0 is a 3×3 block of zeros: no normal strain couples to a shear. Written out (Kittel eq. 43), the energy density is

Cubic energy densitySheet 5.2
$$\begin{aligned}&u=\tfrac12C_{11}(e_1^2+e_2^2+e_3^2)\\&+\tfrac12C_{44}(e_4^2+e_5^2+e_6^2)\\&+C_{12}(e_2e_3+e_3e_1+e_1e_2).\end{aligned}$$ (8)

Notes p24 says that apart from $C_{11}$ and $C_{12}$ "all others vanish". That leaves out $C_{44}=C_{55}=C_{66}$, which the p25 matrix includes. Without $C_{44}$ a cube would offer no resistance to shear, and C would be singular.

Inverting (7) is easy because C is block-diagonal, and its 3×3 block has eigenvectors (1,1,1) and (1,−1,0), (1,1,−2). S has the same eigenvectors with reciprocal eigenvalues, which gives the relations from notes p25:

Compliance of a cubic crystalSheet 5.1
$$\begin{gathered}\begin{aligned}C_{11}-C_{12}&=\frac1{S_{11}-S_{12}},\\ C_{11}+2C_{12}&=\frac1{S_{11}+2S_{12}},\\ C_{44}&=\frac1{S_{44}};\\ S_{11}&=\frac{C_{11}+C_{12}}{D},\\ S_{12}&=\frac{-C_{12}}{D},\end{aligned}\\[4pt] D\equiv(C_{11}-C_{12})(C_{11}+2C_{12}).\end{gathered}$$ (9)
300 K, in 10¹¹ N/m²C₁₁C₁₂C₄₄S₁₁, S₁₂ (10⁻¹¹ m²/N)
Cu1.6841.2140.7541.50, −0.63
Al1.0680.6070.2821.59, −0.58
Si1.660.6390.7960.77, −0.21
Diamond10.761.255.760.095, −0.010

Reading the table in N/m². Notes p25 and the board write the Cu and diamond values with the unit "[N/m²]", without the factor. The table is in units of $10^{11}$ N/m² = 100 GPa (Kittel Tables 11–12), so Cu has $C_{11}=1.68\times10^{11}$ N/m² = 168 GPa.

Notes p24, cycle list: the four 3-fold axes are [111], [1̄11], [11̄1] and [111̄]. The handwritten list repeats [1̄11] (once in each sense) and misses [111̄], and the notes mark it "Prof wasn't sure". Only one 3-fold axis is needed for the argument above.

Looking ahead. The same three constants set the sound speeds. A longitudinal wave along [100] has $v_L=\sqrt{C_{11}/\rho}$ and a transverse one has $v_T=\sqrt{C_{44}/\rho}$ (Kittel Fig. 20). These are the speeds behind the Debye temperature on page 06.

Why do exactly three combinations appear in (9)?

They are the three distinct eigenvalues of $C_{\rm cubic}$: $C_{11}+2C_{12}$ for (1,1,1,0,0,0), $C_{11}-C_{12}$ twice in the volume-conserving normal-strain plane, and $C_{44}$ three times for the shears. Inverting a symmetric matrix inverts each eigenvalue on the same eigenvector.

Cubic means three constants. The 3-fold axes make the diagonal entries equal, and the 2-fold axes kill the cross terms. Table values are in 10¹¹ N/m².

1.5.4 · Stability

Stability means C is positive definite

1.5.4 · Energy density and 1.5.4 · Cubic crystals

A crystal is stable only if every small strain costs energy: $u=\tfrac12\underline e^T\underline{\underline C}\,\underline e>0$ for all $\underline e\neq0$. That is the definition of a positive-definite matrix. If one eigenvalue were negative, u would be a saddle, and the crystal would deform spontaneously along that eigenvector. For a real symmetric matrix, positive definite means that all eigenvalues are positive. For the cubic C (7) they are

Cubic stability criteriaSheet 5.2
$$\begin{aligned}&\underbrace{C_{11}+2C_{12}>0}_{\text{volume }(1,1,1,0,0,0),\ =3B}\\[6pt] &\underbrace{C_{11}-C_{12}>0}_{\substack{\text{tetragonal, }\times2:\\(1,-1,0,\dots),\,(1,1,-2,\dots)}}\\[6pt] &\underbrace{C_{44}>0}_{\text{shear }e_4,e_5,e_6,\ \times3}\end{aligned}$$ (10)

The first two together say $-\tfrac12C_{11}<C_{12}<C_{11}$, which forces $C_{11}>0$. The same result is Kittel ch. 3 Problem 14.

"All $C_{ij}$ positive" is neither necessary nor sufficient. $C_{12}$ may be negative, down to $-C_{11}/2$. And $C_{12}>C_{11}$ with every entry positive is unstable against the volume-conserving tetragonal strain (1, −1, 0, 0, 0, 0).

Get (10) from Sylvester's criterion instead of eigenvalues.

The leading principal minors are $C_{11}$, then $C_{11}^2-C_{12}^2=(C_{11}-C_{12})(C_{11}+C_{12})$, then $\det=(C_{11}-C_{12})^2(C_{11}+2C_{12})$, and then the same times $C_{44}$, $C_{44}^2$, $C_{44}^3$. All are positive exactly when (10) holds: given the third minor, $C_{11}+C_{12}>0$ follows from the other two conditions.

Stable ⇔ C positive definite ⇔ its three distinct eigenvalues, C₁₁ + 2C₁₂, C₁₁ − C₁₂ and C₄₄, are all positive.

You can now write u for a cubic crystal, explain why only C₁₁, C₁₂ and C₄₄ survive, and turn "u > 0 for every strain" into three inequalities. That is Sheet 5.2 and parts (a) and (d) of the Al exercise.

1.5.5Bulk modulus

Where you meet this. Diamond anvil cells squeeze samples between two diamond tips to over 300 GPa, close to the pressure at the centre of the Earth (about 360 GPa). Diamond works because its bulk modulus, (C₁₁ + 2C₁₂)/3 = 442 GPa from the table in 1.5.4, is among the highest of any material: more than three times that of copper (137 GPa).

What this covers. The bulk modulus B = (C₁₁ + 2C₁₂)/3 from hydrostatic strain, and, as a second card, the other two moduli of sheet 5.1: Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio along [100], [110] and any direction. Notes p. 25 (board photo); Kittel "Bulk Modulus and Compressibility", Fig. 21, Problem 8.

Why it matters. B is the one boxed result of the lecture. Sheet 5.1 asks for E and ν along [100] and [110], and the exam-style exercise below uses both. Model 2 shows the anisotropy of E (the Zener ratio).

1.5.5 · Bulk modulus

Bulk modulus of a cubic crystal

1.5.1 · Dilation and Voigt and 1.5.4 · Cubic crystals

The bulk modulus is the stiffness against a change of volume, $B=-V\,(\partial p/\partial V)_T$. Equivalently, $u=\tfrac12B\delta^2$ for a uniform dilation δ. Insert hydrostatic strain, $e_1=e_2=e_3=\delta/3$ and $e_4=e_5=e_6=0$, into (8):

Derivation (board, p25)
$$\begin{aligned}u&=\tfrac12C_{11}\cdot3\,\frac{\delta^2}{9}+C_{12}\cdot3\,\frac{\delta^2}{9}\\&=\frac{C_{11}+2C_{12}}{6}\,\delta^2\equiv\frac12B\,\delta^2 .\end{aligned}$$ (11)
Bulk modulus of a cubic crystal
$$\begin{aligned}B&=\tfrac13\,(C_{11}+2C_{12}),\\ K&=\frac1B\ \text{(compressibility)}.\end{aligned}$$ (12)

Cu: $B=(1.684+2\cdot1.214)/3=1.37\times10^{11}$ N/m² = 137 GPa. One gigapascal of pressure therefore shrinks Cu by $\Delta V/V=-1/137=-0.73$ %. The syllabus calls this subsection 1.5.5; the notes number it 1.5.5.1, under "Elastic constants in cubic systems".

Putting δ, rather than δ/3, on each axis. A uniform dilation δ stretches each axis by δ/3, because the three relative length changes add up to the relative volume change.

Check (12) a second way, from e = S σ with hydrostatic pressure p.

$\sigma_1=\sigma_2=\sigma_3=-p$, so $e_1=-(S_{11}+2S_{12})\,p$ and $\delta=3e_1=-3(S_{11}+2S_{12})p$. By (9), $3(S_{11}+2S_{12})=3/(C_{11}+2C_{12})=1/B$. ✓

B = (C₁₁ + 2C₁₂)/3: the hydrostatic eigenvalue of C, divided by 3.

1.5.5 · Young's modulus

Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio of a cubic crystal

1.5.3 · Stiffness and compliance and 1.5.4 · Cubic crystals

Not in the notes. The definitions are on sheet 5 and in Kittel Fig. 21; the results below follow from 1.5.3 and the cubic C of 1.5.4. Pull a rod along a unit direction $\hat{\mathbf n}$ with stress σ and leave the sides free. Young's modulus is $E=\sigma/(\Delta L/L)$, and Poisson's ratio is $\nu=-(\Delta d/d)/(\Delta L/L)$ for a lateral direction $\hat{\mathbf m}\perp\hat{\mathbf n}$. In cube axes the stress tensor is $\sigma_{ij}=\sigma\,n_in_j$. Since σ is known, use $\underline e=\underline{\underline S}\,\underline\sigma$.

Along [100]: $\underline\sigma=(\sigma,0,0,0,0,0)$ gives $e_1=S_{11}\sigma$ and $e_2=e_3=S_{12}\sigma$. With (9):

E and ν along [100]Sheet 5.1(i)
$$\begin{aligned}E_{[100]}&=\frac1{S_{11}}=\frac{D}{C_{11}+C_{12}},\\ \nu_{[100]}&=-\frac{S_{12}}{S_{11}}=\frac{C_{12}}{C_{11}+C_{12}}.\end{aligned}$$ (13)

Any direction. The longitudinal strain is $\hat{\mathbf n}^T\underline{\underline\varepsilon}\,\hat{\mathbf n}$. Working this out with the cubic S gives

Directional Young's modulusSheet 5.1(ii)
$$\begin{aligned}&\frac1{E(\hat{\mathbf n})}=S_{11}\\&\quad-2\Big(S_{11}-S_{12}-\tfrac12S_{44}\Big)\\&\quad\times\big(n_1^2n_2^2+n_2^2n_3^2+n_3^2n_1^2\big).\end{aligned}$$ (14)

The bracket of direction cosines is 0 along ⟨100⟩, ¼ along ⟨110⟩ and ⅓ along ⟨111⟩. So along [110]:

Young's modulus along [110]Sheet 5.1(ii)
$$\frac1{E_{[110]}}=\tfrac12\Big(S_{11}+S_{12}+\tfrac12S_{44}\Big).$$

The crystal is elastically isotropic only if the anisotropy factor vanishes, which is the condition that the Zener ratio is 1:

Zener anisotropy ratio
$$A=\frac{2C_{44}}{C_{11}-C_{12}}=\frac{2(S_{11}-S_{12})}{S_{44}}.$$ (15)

Cu has A = 3.2, so it is three times stiffer along [111] (191 GPa) than along [100] (67 GPa). Al (A = 1.22) is nearly isotropic. Model 2 draws E(n̂) for both.

Expecting one Poisson's ratio. Along [110] the two side faces, (001) and (1̄10), contract differently. For Cu, ν toward [001] is 0.82 and ν toward [1̄10] is −0.14: pulled along [110], Cu gets wider along [1̄10]. The rule ν ≤ ½ holds only for isotropic solids.

Show that an isotropic crystal (A = 1) has E[110] = E[100].

A = 1 means $S_{11}-S_{12}=\tfrac12S_{44}$, so the bracket in (14) is multiplied by zero, and $1/E=S_{11}$ in every direction.

Use S for a tensile test. $E_{[100]}=1/S_{11}$ and $\nu_{[100]}=-S_{12}/S_{11}$. Away from ⟨100⟩, C₄₄ enters through the Zener ratio, and ν depends on which side you measure.

You can now get B, E and ν along [100] and [110] from the three cubic constants and say how anisotropic a crystal is from A. That is all of Sheet 5.1 and parts (b), (c) and (e) of the Al exercise.

Sheet 5 · Problem 1 · 6 pts

Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio

A crystal with intrinsic cubic symmetry, in the shape of a cube, is under tension. Give Young's modulus E and Poisson's ratio ν in terms of the elastic stiffness constants Cij, (i) for tension along x, i.e. [100]; (ii) for tension along a face diagonal, e.g. [110]: the macroscopic surface is (110), with side faces (1 1̄ 0) and (001). Definitions given: F/A₀ = E·ΔL/L and Δd/d₀ = −ν·Δl/l₀.

Method

Tension fixes the stress, so write σ in cube axes and get every strain from e = S σ (1.5.3). Then convert S to C with (9). For (ii), rotate the uniaxial stress into cube axes with direction cosines, $\sigma_{ij}=\sigma\,n_in_j$ (board 11), and keep the factor 2 between $e_6$ and $\varepsilon_{xy}$ (1.5.1).

Reading of the statement: "tension" is taken as uniaxial stress with free side faces, as in the sheet's cylinder sketch. "Poisson's ratio" in (ii) is computed for both side-face normals, [1̄10] and [001], since the two differ for a cubic crystal.
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. (i) $\underline\sigma=(\sigma,0,0,0,0,0)$. Then $e_1=S_{11}\sigma$, $e_2=e_3=S_{12}\sigma$ and $e_4=e_5=e_6=0$. Hence $E=\sigma/e_1=1/S_{11}$ and $\nu=-e_2/e_1=-S_{12}/S_{11}$.
  2. With (9), $S_{11}=\dfrac{C_{11}+C_{12}}{(C_{11}-C_{12})(C_{11}+2C_{12})}$ and $S_{12}=\dfrac{-C_{12}}{(C_{11}-C_{12})(C_{11}+2C_{12})}$, so
    Sheet 5.1(i) result
    $$\begin{aligned}&E_{[100]}\\&=\frac{(C_{11}-C_{12})(C_{11}+2C_{12})}{C_{11}+C_{12}},\\ &\nu_{[100]}=\frac{C_{12}}{C_{11}+C_{12}}.\end{aligned}$$
    By cubic symmetry both side faces give the same ν.
  3. (ii) $\hat{\mathbf n}=(1,1,0)/\sqrt2$, so $\sigma_{xx}=\sigma_{yy}=\sigma_{xy}=\sigma/2$, i.e. $\underline\sigma=(\tfrac\sigma2,\tfrac\sigma2,0,0,0,\tfrac\sigma2)$. Then $e_1=e_2=\tfrac\sigma2(S_{11}+S_{12})$, $e_3=S_{12}\sigma$, $e_6=\tfrac\sigma2S_{44}$, so the tensor shear is $\varepsilon_{xy}=e_6/2=\tfrac\sigma4S_{44}$.
  4. Longitudinal strain: $\hat{\mathbf n}^T\underline{\underline\varepsilon}\,\hat{\mathbf n}=\tfrac12(\varepsilon_{xx}+\varepsilon_{yy})+\varepsilon_{xy}$ $=\tfrac\sigma2\big(S_{11}+S_{12}+\tfrac12S_{44}\big)$. So $1/E_{[110]}=\tfrac12(S_{11}+S_{12}+\tfrac12S_{44})$. With $D\equiv(C_{11}-C_{12})(C_{11}+2C_{12})$ as in (9), $S_{11}+S_{12}=C_{11}/D$ and $S_{44}=1/C_{44}$:
    Sheet 5.1(ii): Young’s modulus along [110]
    $$E_{[110]}=\frac{4C_{44}\,D}{2C_{11}C_{44}+D}.$$
  5. Transverse strain along [001]: $\varepsilon_{zz}=S_{12}\sigma$, so $\nu_{[110]\to[001]}=-S_{12}E_{[110]}$. Along $\hat{\mathbf m}=(1,-1,0)/\sqrt2$: $\hat{\mathbf m}^T\underline{\underline\varepsilon}\,\hat{\mathbf m}=\tfrac12(\varepsilon_{xx}+\varepsilon_{yy})-\varepsilon_{xy}$ $=\tfrac\sigma2(S_{11}+S_{12}-\tfrac12S_{44})$. With the same D:
    Sheet 5.1(ii): the two Poisson ratios
    $$\begin{aligned}\nu_{[110]\to[001]}&=\frac{4C_{12}C_{44}}{2C_{11}C_{44}+D},\\ \nu_{[110]\to[1\bar10]}&=\frac{D-2C_{11}C_{44}}{D+2C_{11}C_{44}}.\end{aligned}$$
Result: (i) E = (C₁₁ − C₁₂)(C₁₁ + 2C₁₂)/(C₁₁ + C₁₂), ν = C₁₂/(C₁₁ + C₁₂). (ii) E = 4C₄₄D/(2C₁₁C₄₄ + D), with two Poisson ratios: 4C₁₂C₄₄/(2C₁₁C₄₄ + D) toward [001] and (D − 2C₁₁C₄₄)/(D + 2C₁₁C₄₄) toward [1̄10], where D = (C₁₁ − C₁₂)(C₁₁ + 2C₁₂). Cu: E[100] = 66.7 GPa, ν = 0.419; E[110] = 130.3 GPa, ν = 0.819 (to [001]) and −0.136 (to [1̄10]).
Sanity check: for isotropy, C₄₄ = (C₁₁ − C₁₂)/2, so 2C₁₁C₄₄ = C₁₁(C₁₁ − C₁₂). Then E[110] reduces to E[100], and both ν[110] reduce to C₁₂/(C₁₁ + C₁₂), as they must. The Cu numbers were checked in node by rotating the full rank-4 compliance tensor and computing n̂ᵀε n̂ and m̂ᵀε m̂ directly. They agree with these closed forms to all digits shown, and with Model 2's readout.

Points usually lost: using $\varepsilon_{xy}=e_6$ (gives the wrong S₄₄ weight); writing $S_{11}=1/C_{11}$; giving one ν in (ii).

Sheet 5 · Problem 2 · 4 pts

Stability criteria

A crystal with cubic symmetry is stable if every combination of strain components gives a positive elastic energy density. What restrictions does that impose on the Cij? (Hint: conditions for a real symmetric quadratic form to be positive definite.)

Method

$u=\tfrac12\underline e^T\underline{\underline C}\,\underline e>0$ for all $\underline e\ne0$ is positive definiteness of C (1.5.4 · Stability). Diagonalise the cubic C (7), or use Sylvester's criterion.

Show worked solution

Steps

  1. C is block-diagonal: a 3×3 block $N=(C_{11}-C_{12})\mathbb 1+C_{12}J$ acting on $(e_1,e_2,e_3)$, where J is the all-ones matrix, and the block $C_{44}\mathbb 1_3$ acting on $(e_4,e_5,e_6)$. Equivalently, (8) has no terms that mix normal strains with shears.
  2. J has eigenvalue 3 on (1,1,1) and 0 on the plane orthogonal to it. So N has eigenvalue $C_{11}+2C_{12}$ on (1,1,1) and $C_{11}-C_{12}$ (twice) on (1,−1,0) and (1,1,−2).
  3. Positive definite ⇔ all eigenvalues positive:
    Sheet 5.2 result
    $$\begin{aligned}&C_{44}>0,\\ &C_{11}-C_{12}>0,\\ &C_{11}+2C_{12}>0 .\end{aligned}$$
    Equivalently, $C_{44}>0$ and $-\tfrac12C_{11}<C_{12}<C_{11}$ (which implies $C_{11}>0$).
  4. Physical meaning: $C_{11}+2C_{12}=3B$ (no collapse under pressure); $C_{11}-C_{12}$ is the stiffness against volume-conserving tetragonal stretching; $C_{44}$ is the stiffness against shear.
Result: C₄₄ > 0, C₁₁ > C₁₂ and C₁₁ + 2C₁₂ > 0. The answer to Kittel ch. 3 Problem 14 (the same question) is often written as C₄₄ > 0, C₁₁ > |C₁₂|, C₁₁ + 2C₁₂ > 0. This is the same set: for C₁₂ < 0, C₁₁ + 2C₁₂ > 0 already gives C₁₁ > −C₁₂.
Sanity check: Sylvester's leading minors (self-test in 1.5.4 · Stability) give the same three conditions. Numerically, for Cu the eigenvalues are 4.112, 0.470 (×2) and 0.754 (×3) ×10¹¹ N/m², all positive. In node, a random-vector test of eᵀC e on 10⁵ strains finds no negative value for Cu, while with C₁₂ = 1.05 C₁₁ about 0.2 % of the strains give u < 0. Model 3 shows the same boundary.

Points usually lost: stating only "C₁₁, C₁₂, C₄₄ > 0"; forgetting the hydrostatic condition; not saying why (the eigenvectors).

Exam-style exercise · about 12 pts · not from the course

Elastic response of an aluminium single crystal

Al is fcc with a = 4.05 Å. At 300 K, C₁₁ = 1.068, C₁₂ = 0.607 and C₄₄ = 0.282, in 10¹¹ N/m². (a) Write Hooke's law for a cubic crystal in Voigt form and explain why three constants suffice. (b) Find the bulk modulus and compressibility. By how much do the volume and the lattice constant change under a hydrostatic pressure of 1 GPa? (c) Find E and ν for tension along [100]. A rod is loaded with 100 MPa along [100]: give the axial strain, the lateral strain and the stored energy density. (d) Is Al stable? Give the eigenvalues of C. (e) Is Al elastically isotropic? Compare E along [100] and [111].

Data: the elastic constants are Kittel Table 11 (300 K). The lattice constant is the one used on page 09. The exercise is in the pattern of the 2022 paper (short parts, statements plus numbers), but it is not a past question.
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. (a) σ = C e with e = (εxx, εyy, εzz, 2εyz, 2εzx, 2εxy) and C as in (7). In general C is symmetric because u = ½ eᵀC e exists, which leaves 21 constants. The 3-fold axes ⟨111⟩ permute x, y, z, so C₁₁ = C₂₂ = C₃₃, C₁₂ = C₁₃ = C₂₃ and C₄₄ = C₅₅ = C₆₆. The 2-fold cube axes flip the signs of pairs of shears, so all normal–shear and shear–shear cross terms vanish.
  2. (b) $B=\tfrac13(1.068+2\cdot0.607)\times10^{11}=0.761\times10^{11}$ N/m² = 76.1 GPa, and $K=1/B=1.31\times10^{-11}$ Pa⁻¹. Under p = 1 GPa: $\Delta V/V=-p/B=-1.31$ %, and each axis shrinks by a third of that, $\Delta a/a=-0.438$ %, so $\Delta a=-0.0177$ Å (a: 4.050 → 4.032 Å).
  3. (c) $D=(1.068-0.607)(1.068+1.214)$ $=0.461\cdot2.282=1.052$. $E_{[100]}=D/(C_{11}+C_{12})$ $=1.052/1.675=0.628\times10^{11}$ N/m² = 62.8 GPa, and $\nu=0.607/1.675=0.362$. At 100 MPa: axial strain $\sigma/E=1.59\times10^{-3}$, lateral strain $-\nu\cdot1.59\times10^{-3}=-5.77\times10^{-4}$, energy density $u=\sigma^2/2E$ $=(10^8)^2/(2\cdot6.28\times10^{10})$ $=8.0\times10^4$ J/m³.
  4. (d) Eigenvalues: C₁₁ + 2C₁₂ = 2.282 (once), C₁₁ − C₁₂ = 0.461 (twice), C₄₄ = 0.282 (three times), in 10¹¹ N/m². All are positive, so C is positive definite and Al is stable.
  5. (e) $A=2C_{44}/(C_{11}-C_{12})=0.564/0.461=1.22$: close to, but not exactly, isotropic. From (14) with the ⟨111⟩ bracket ⅓: $E_{[111]}=75.3$ GPa, against $E_{[100]}=62.8$ GPa, a 20 % difference (Cu: 191 against 67 GPa).
Result: (b) B = 76.1 GPa, K = 1.31 × 10⁻¹¹ Pa⁻¹, ΔV/V = −1.31 %, Δa = −0.018 Å. (c) E = 62.8 GPa, ν = 0.362; strains 1.59 × 10⁻³ and −5.8 × 10⁻⁴; u = 8.0 × 10⁴ J/m³. (d) stable, eigenvalues 2.28, 0.461, 0.282 × 10¹¹ N/m². (e) A = 1.22, E[111] = 75.3 GPa.
Sanity check: (b) the per-axis strain was recomputed independently from e₁ = −p(S₁₁ + 2S₁₂) = −4.38 × 10⁻³, the same as δ/3. (c) and (e) were checked in node by inverting the 6×6 C numerically and by rotating the rank-4 compliance tensor (same E[100], ν and E[111]). Model 2 with the Al preset shows the same values. Order of magnitude: B of a metal is tens to hundreds of GPa, and an elastic strain of 0.16 % at 100 MPa is typical.

Points usually lost: reading C in N/m² without the 10¹¹; using δ instead of δ/3 per axis; quoting C₁₁ as Young's modulus (C₁₁ = 107 GPa is the stiffness with the sides clamped, E = 63 GPa with them free).
