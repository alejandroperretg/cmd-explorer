Phonons

The atoms of a crystal are coupled by springs, so they move in collective waves with a definite frequency for each wave vector, ω(k). Those waves, quantised as phonons, carry sound and heat, set the lattice heat capacity (topic 06) and scatter electrons (topic 08). This is also the cleanest place to see what a wave vector k means on a lattice.

Overview

A static lattice cannot explain the $T^3$ heat capacity of insulators, thermal expansion, the heat conduction of insulators, the temperature dependence of a metal's resistance, the infrared response of ionic crystals or sound. All of these need the atoms to move. With the electrons following the ions instantly (adiabatic approximation) and small displacements (harmonic approximation), Newton's equations for a chain of masses and springs are linear and are solved by plane waves $u_j\propto e^{i(kja-\omega t)}$.

Key results to leave with: the monatomic dispersion $\omega=\sqrt{4f/m}\,|\sin(ka/2)|$ and why $k$ only matters modulo $2\pi/a$ (1.6.1); the acoustic and optical branches of a two-atom chain, with the gap at $\pi/a$, solved here in full, since the notes stop at the determinant (1.6.2); the density of states $g(\omega)\propto 1/|d\omega/dk|$ and its singularity where the band is flat (1.6.4); phonons as quanta $\hbar\omega(n+\tfrac12)$ with crystal momentum $\hbar k$ (1.6.5); and why neutrons map the whole zone while light sees only $k\approx0$ (1.6.6).

Read alongside Kittel, Introduction to Solid State Physics 8th ed., ch. 4 (Vibrations of Crystals with Monatomic Basis; Two Atoms per Primitive Basis; Quantization of Elastic Waves; Phonon Momentum; Inelastic Scattering by Phonons) and ch. 5 (Density of States in One Dimension), and the lecture notes pp. 26–33. The lecture writes $f, m, k$ for Kittel's $C, M, K$; this page follows the lecture and the sheet.

Concepts

1.6.1Simple linear chain

Where you meet this. Knock on one end of a steel rail and the sound reaches the far end through the steel at about 5 km/s, some 15 times faster than through the air (343 m/s). That speed is the slope of this section's dispersion curve at small k, $v_s=a\sqrt{f/m}$: stiff springs and light atoms make fast sound.

What this covers. Why the atoms can be treated as masses on springs (adiabatic and harmonic approximations), the dispersion ω(k) of a one-atom chain with its sound and group velocities, and what the wave vector k means on a lattice. Lecture notes pp. 26–30.

Why it matters. Sheet 6.1 repeats this derivation with longer-range springs, and sheet 6.2 starts from its result. Every later section builds on it, and the rule "k and k + G are the same wave" returns for electrons in Bloch states (topic 09).

1.6.1 · Approximations

Setting up: adiabatic and harmonic approximations

Electrons are thousands of times lighter than ions and adjust to any ion arrangement almost instantly, so they stay in their ground state as the ions move. The energy is then a function of the ion positions alone, $U(\{\mathbf r(\mathbf R,j)\})$, with $\mathbf R$ the lattice point and $j$ the basis atom. The force on an ion is $\mathbf F=-\partial U/\partial\mathbf r$.

Write each position as its lattice site plus a small displacement, $\mathbf r=\mathbf R+\mathbf v_j+\mathbf u(\mathbf R,j)$ with $\langle u^2\rangle\ll r_{NN}^2$. Expand $U$ to second order: the linear term vanishes at equilibrium, so $U$ is a quadratic form in the $u$'s, the forces are linear (Hooke) and the problem is a set of coupled harmonic oscillators. Its normal modes are the lattice waves.

Why does the expansion of U have no linear term in the displacements?

The expansion is about the equilibrium positions, where every force $-\partial U/\partial\mathbf r$ is zero. The first derivatives vanish, so the first non-constant term is quadratic.

Adiabatic: the energy depends on the ion positions only. Harmonic: it is quadratic in small displacements. Together they turn the crystal into coupled springs with linear equations of motion.

1.6.1 · Dispersion

The monatomic chain

1.6.1 · Approximations; ← from 1.5.4 Cubic crystals (sound speeds)

Atoms of mass $m$, spacing $a$, nearest-neighbour springs $f$, displacement $u_j$ of atom $j$:

Potential and equation of motionSheet 6.1
$$\begin{aligned}U&=\sum_j\tfrac12 f\,(u_j-u_{j+1})^2\\ m\ddot u_j&=-\frac{\partial U}{\partial u_j}\\&=f\,(u_{j+1}+u_{j-1}-2u_j)\end{aligned}$$ (1)

Insert $u_j=u_0\,e^{i(kja-\omega t)}$ and cancel $u_0e^{i(kja-\omega t)}$:

After the plane-wave ansatz
$$\begin{aligned}-m\omega^2&=f\,(e^{ika}+e^{-ika}-2)\\&=-2f\,(1-\cos ka)\\&=-4f\sin^2\tfrac{ka}{2}\end{aligned}$$ (2)

so

Monatomic dispersionSheet 6.2
$$\begin{aligned}\omega(k)&=\sqrt{\frac{4f}{m}}\,\Big|\sin\frac{ka}{2}\Big|\\ \omega_m&=\sqrt{4f/m}\end{aligned}$$ (3)
  • Long waves, $ka\ll1$: $\omega\approx a\sqrt{f/m}\,|k|$. Sound with speed $v_s=a\sqrt{f/m}$, independent of frequency, as in a continuum.
  • Group velocity $v_g=d\omega/dk=v_s\cos(ka/2)$ for $k>0$: the speed of a packet and of energy. It falls to zero at $k=\pi/a$.
  • Zone boundary $k=\pm\pi/a$: $u_j\propto(-1)^j$, neighbours exactly opposed. A standing wave, the lattice version of Bragg reflection with $\lambda=2a$.
Notes, p27: the chain potential is written with $(u_l+u_{l+1})^2$. It must be a difference, $(u_l-u_{l+1})^2$: a rigid shift of the whole chain costs no energy, and the force on the next line only follows from the minus sign.

Taking the phase velocity ω/k as the speed of sound or energy. Signals and energy travel at the group velocity dω/dk, which is zero at the zone boundary even though ω/k is not.

At k = π/a, what do neighbouring atoms do, and how fast does energy travel?

$u_{j+1}/u_j=e^{i\pi}=-1$: neighbours move exactly opposite. $v_g=v_s\cos(\pi/2)=0$, so the mode is a standing wave and carries no energy.

$\omega=\sqrt{4f/m}\,|\sin(ka/2)|$: linear (sound) at small k, flat with zero group velocity at the zone boundary $\pm\pi/a$.

1.6.1 · What k means

k and k + 2π/a are the same wave

1.6.1 · Dispersion; ← from 1.3.2 Reciprocal lattice, 1.3.3 Brillouin zone

Recap from 1.3.2 (topic 02). The reciprocal lattice is the set of G with $e^{iGR}=1$ for every lattice vector R; the first Brillouin zone is its Wigner–Seitz cell, the k closer to G = 0 than to any other G. For a chain of spacing a: $G=2pi n/a$, zone $-pi/alt klepi/a$.

The displacement only exists at the atoms, $x=ja$. Replace $k$ by $k'=k+2\pi n/a$:

Shifting k by n·2π/a
$$\begin{aligned}u_j'&=u_0\,e^{i(k'ja-\omega t)}\\&=u_0\,e^{i(kja-\omega t)}\,e^{i2\pi nj}\\&=u_j\end{aligned}$$ (4)

because $e^{i2\pi nj}=1$ for every integer $j$. Every atom does exactly the same thing, at the same frequency (ω(k) is periodic with $2\pi/a$). The two continuous sine curves you could draw through the atoms differ, but the space between atoms has no displacement, so the difference is not physical. This is aliasing, as with a strobe light or a sampled signal (Kittel ch. 4 Fig. 5).

What $k$ really specifies is the phase step from one atom to the next, $u_{j+1}/u_j=e^{ika}$. A phase is only defined modulo $2\pi$, so $ka$ only matters modulo $2\pi$, and one interval of length $2\pi/a$ holds every distinct mode. The conventional choice is $-\pi/a\lt k\le\pi/a$, the first Brillouin zone. $2\pi/a$ is the reciprocal lattice vector $G$ of the chain, so the rule reads: $k$ and $k+G$ label the same mode.

  • Counting: on a ring of $N$ atoms, $e^{ikNa}=1$ gives $k=2\pi s/Na$; distinct modes need distinct $e^{ika}$, so there are exactly $N$, one per atom (degrees of freedom are conserved). The same periodic-boundary counting as for electrons on topic 07.
  • Velocities: $v_g=d\omega/dk$ is the same at $k$ and $k+G$ (the derivative of a periodic function is periodic). The phase velocity $\omega/k$ is not, which shows it has no physical meaning on a lattice.
  • Why it matters later: the same rule makes electron Bloch states periodic in $k$ (topic 09, "What k looks like") and lets phonon collisions change total $k$ by $G$ (Umklapp, topic 08).

Thinking the wave at k + 2π/a is a different, shorter wave of the crystal. Between the atoms there is nothing to displace, so the two curves describe one and the same motion.

A chain wave has k = 1.3π/a. Which k in the first zone describes the same motion?

Subtract $G=2\pi/a$: $k'=1.3\pi/a-2\pi/a=-0.7\pi/a$. It is the opening state of Model 1.

k is the phase step per atom, so it only matters modulo 2π/a. The first Brillouin zone, $-\pi/a\lt k\le\pi/a$, holds all N modes of an N-atom chain.

You can now derive the monatomic dispersion from the chain potential, give its sound speed and zone-boundary behaviour, and explain why k is restricted to the first zone. Sheet 6.1 is the same derivation with more springs; try it next, then play with Model 1.

1.6.2Linear chain with basis

Where you meet this. Rock-salt (NaCl) windows are a standard part of infrared spectrometers because they are transparent out to about 15 µm. Further out, near 61 µm, the crystal absorbs strongly: there light shakes Na⁺ and Cl⁻ against each other in the optical branch you derive here.

What this covers. A chain with two different masses per cell: the equations of motion, the 2×2 matrix and its determinant, the full solution with acoustic and optical branches, the gap, the eigenvectors, and branch counting in 3D. Lecture notes pp. 30–31; the notes stop at the determinant, so the solution follows Kittel ch. 4.

Why it matters. Every crystal with a basis (NaCl, diamond, GaAs) has optical branches. They are what light couples to (1.6.6) and what the Einstein model describes on topic 06, and the count of 3p branches gives the 3nN modes of the heat capacity. The exam-style exercise below is this section with numbers.

1.6.2 · Setting up

Two masses per cell

1.6.1 · Dispersion

Two masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ alternate, joined by equal springs $f$. $a$ is the repeat distance (between atoms of the same kind) and $j$ the cell index. Call the displacements $u_j$ (mass $m_1$) and $v_j$ (mass $m_2$, to the right of $u_j$). Atom $u_j$ touches $v_j$ and $v_{j-1}$; atom $v_j$ touches $u_j$ and $u_{j+1}$:

Equations of motion
$$\begin{aligned}m_1\ddot u_j&=f\,(v_j+v_{j-1}-2u_j)\\ m_2\ddot v_j&=f\,(u_{j+1}+u_j-2v_j)\end{aligned}$$ (5)

Try a wave with a different amplitude on each sublattice, $u_j=u\,e^{i(kja-\omega t)}$, $v_j=v\,e^{i(kja-\omega t)}$. Then $v_{j-1}=v_j\,e^{-ika}$ and $u_{j+1}=u_j\,e^{+ika}$, and

Linear system for the amplitudes (Kittel eq. 20)
$$\begin{aligned}&(2f-m_1\omega^2)\,u\\&\quad-f\,(1+e^{-ika})\,v=0\\[4pt] &-f\,(1+e^{ika})\,u\\&\quad+(2f-m_2\omega^2)\,v=0\end{aligned}$$ (6)

A non-zero $(u,v)$ needs a zero determinant. The off-diagonal product is $f^2(1+e^{-ika})(1+e^{ika})=f^2(2+2\cos ka)$, so

Determinant condition (Kittel eq. 22)
$$\begin{aligned}&m_1m_2\,\omega^4-2f(m_1+m_2)\,\omega^2\\&\quad+2f^2(1-\cos ka)=0\end{aligned}$$ (7)

a quadratic in $\omega^2$.

The lecture's route (mass weighting) gives the same answer. With $w_1=\sqrt{m_1}\,u$, $w_2=\sqrt{m_2}\,v$ the equations read $\omega^2\mathbf w=\underline D\,\mathbf w$ with the dynamical matrix $\underline D$:

Dynamical matrix (lecture)
$$\begin{aligned}D_{11}&=\frac{2f}{m_1},\qquad D_{22}=\frac{2f}{m_2}\\ D_{12}&=-\frac{f\,(1+e^{-ika})}{\sqrt{m_1m_2}}\\ D_{21}&=-\frac{f\,(1+e^{ika})}{\sqrt{m_1m_2}}\\ 0&=\det(\underline D-\omega^2\mathbb 1)\end{aligned}$$ (8)

where $\mathbb 1$ is the identity matrix $I$. $\underline D$ is Hermitian, so its eigenvalues $\omega^2$ are real.

Notes, p31, two slips: (1) both off-diagonal entries carry $e^{ika}$. Because $u_1$ couples to $u_2(x-a)$, the (1,2) entry must carry $e^{-ika}$ and the (2,1) entry $e^{+ika}$. With equal exponents $\underline D$ is not Hermitian and the determinant contains the complex number $(1+e^{ika})^2$. (2) In the determinant line the off-diagonal entries have lost the factor $f$ (the diagonal has it). Which neighbour you call "same cell" flips both exponents, but only the convention-free product $|1+e^{ika}|^2=4\cos^2(ka/2)$ enters the result.

Giving both off-diagonal entries the same exponent, as the notes do. The two entries must be complex conjugates; otherwise the determinant is complex and ω² comes out complex.

Why can only the product of the off-diagonal entries enter the determinant, and what is it?

A 2×2 determinant is $D_{11}D_{22}-D_{12}D_{21}$. The product is $f^2(1+e^{-ika})(1+e^{ika})/m_1m_2=f^2\,|1+e^{ika}|^2/m_1m_2=4f^2\cos^2(ka/2)/m_1m_2$, real and independent of the "same cell" convention.

Two atoms per cell give a 2×2 problem for each k. Its determinant (7) is a quadratic in ω², so each k has two frequencies.

1.6.2 · Solution

Acoustic and optical branches

1.6.2 · Setting up

Divide (7) by $m_1m_2$, write $1-\cos ka=2\sin^2(ka/2)$ and solve the quadratic in $\omega^2$. With the reduced mass $\mu$:

Two-atom chain dispersion
$$\begin{aligned}\omega_\pm^2(k)&=\frac{f}{\mu}\pm f\,R(k)\\ R(k)&=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu^2}-\frac{4\sin^2(ka/2)}{m_1m_2}}\\ \frac1\mu&=\frac1{m_1}+\frac1{m_2}\end{aligned}$$ (9)

The minus sign is the acoustic branch, the plus sign the optical branch. Take $m_1>m_2$:

  • $k\to0$: acoustic $\omega_-^2\approx\dfrac{f}{2(m_1+m_2)}k^2a^2$, sound at $v_s=a\sqrt{f/2(m_1+m_2)}$ (Kittel eq. 24). Optical $\omega_+=\sqrt{2f/\mu}$, the maximum (eq. 23).
  • $k=\pm\pi/a$: the square root becomes $|1/m_2-1/m_1|$, so $\omega_-=\sqrt{2f/m_1}$ and $\omega_+=\sqrt{2f/m_2}$ (eq. 25).
  • Gap: no wave solution has $\sqrt{2f/m_1}\lt\omega\lt\sqrt{2f/m_2}$. It closes when $m_1=m_2$.
Band edges
$$\begin{aligned}\omega_+(0)&=\sqrt{2f/\mu}\\ \omega_+(\pi/a)&=\sqrt{2f/m_2}\\ \omega_-(\pi/a)&=\sqrt{2f/m_1}\end{aligned}$$

Eigenvectors from the first row: $u/v=f(1+e^{-ika})/(2f-m_1\omega^2)$.

  • Acoustic, $k\to0$: $u/v\to1$. Both atoms move together, like sound.
  • Optical, $k=0$: $u/v=-m_2/m_1$. The atoms move against each other and the centre of mass $m_1u+m_2v$ stays put. In an ionic crystal this is an oscillating dipole, which couples to light; hence "optical" (eq. 26).
  • $k=\pi/a$: $1+e^{-i\pi}=0$ decouples the sublattices. In the acoustic mode only the heavy atoms move; in the optical mode only the light ones (Kittel problem 4.3).

Check with equal masses. $m_1=m_2=m$ gives $\omega_\pm^2=\frac{2f}{m}(1\pm|\cos\frac{ka}{2}|)$, i.e. $\omega_-=\sqrt{4f/m}\,|\sin\frac{ka}{4}|$ and $\omega_+=\sqrt{4f/m}\,|\cos\frac{ka}{4}|$. That is the monatomic chain of spacing $a/2$, whose zone runs to $2\pi/a$, with its outer half shifted by $2\pi/a$ into $[-\pi/a,\pi/a]$. Choosing a cell twice too large folds the band; a real mass difference then opens the gap at the fold. Model 2 shows both.

In 3D, with $p$ atoms per primitive cell and $N$ cells: $3p$ branches, 3 acoustic (one longitudinal, two transverse) and $3p-3$ optical, each with $N$ values of $k$. Total $3pN$ modes, one per degree of freedom.

Using the nearest-neighbour distance for a. Here a is the repeat distance between atoms of the same kind, twice the m₁–m₂ spacing; with the wrong a the zone boundary and the sound speed come out wrong by a factor 2.

m₁ = 2m₂. Give both frequencies at k = π/a in units of √(2f/m₂).

Acoustic $\sqrt{2f/m_1}=\sqrt{f/m_2}=0.707$; optical $\sqrt{2f/m_2}=1$. The gap runs from 0.707 to 1.

How many phonon branches does a crystal with 2 atoms per primitive cell have?

6: 3 acoustic (LA, 2 TA) and 3 optical (LO, 2 TO).

Acoustic: atoms in phase, ω → 0 at k = 0. Optical: atoms against each other, highest at k = 0. Between them, a gap from √(2f/m₁) to √(2f/m₂) at the zone boundary.

You can now set up the two-atom chain, get ω± at k = 0 and π/a, sketch both branches with the gap and describe their eigenvectors. That is parts (a) to (d) of the NaCl exercise; check your numbers against Model 2, which opens in the same state.

1.6.4Density of states

Where you meet this. Every heat capacity in a data table, such as copper's 24.4 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ at room temperature, is a sum over the vibration modes, each weighted by how strongly it is excited. The density of states is the list that sum runs over: how many modes sit at each frequency. Topic 06 does the sum; this section builds the list.

What this covers. The definition of g(ω) as a zone integral over a δ-function, its 1D reduction to the roots of ω(k) = ω, the van Hove singularity where the band is flat, and the closed form for the monatomic chain. The lecture derives it on notes p38, inside heat capacity (numbered 1.7.1.4 there); the topic list files it here, and topic 06 recaps it where the lecture uses it.

Why it matters. Sheet 6.2 asks for exactly the 1D DOS. The Debye and Einstein models on topic 06 are two approximations to g(ω), and the same construction gives the electron DOS on topic 07.

1.6.4 · From ω(k) to g(ω)

Counting modes per unit frequency

1.6.1 · Dispersion, 1.6.1 · What k means

The density of states counts modes per unit frequency. Summing over branches $s$ and integrating over the first Brillouin zone,

Density of states, per unit volumeSheet 6.2
$$\begin{aligned}g(\omega)&=\sum_s\int_{1.\mathrm{BZ}}\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\\&\qquad\times\delta\big(\omega-\omega_s(\mathbf k)\big)\end{aligned}$$ (10)

per unit volume (Kittel's $D(\omega)=V g(\omega)$ is for the whole crystal). The weight $d^3k/(2\pi)^3$ is one allowed $k$ per $(2\pi)^3/V$ from periodic boundary conditions. In 1D the integral collapses onto the roots $k_i$ of $\omega(k_i)=\omega$ (sheet 6.2's hint):

1D: sum over the rootsSheet 6.2
$$g(\omega)=\frac1{2\pi}\sum_i\frac{1}{|d\omega/dk|_{k_i}}$$ (11)

A frequency band $d\omega$ cuts the dispersion where it is steep in a narrow $dk$ (few modes) and where it is flat in a wide $dk$ (many modes). Wherever $v_g=0$ the DOS diverges: a van Hove singularity. For the monatomic chain this happens at $\omega_m$:

DOS of the monatomic chainSheet 6.2
$$\begin{aligned}g(\omega)&=\frac{2}{\pi a}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\omega_m^2-\omega^2}}\\&\qquad(0\le\omega\lt\omega_m)\\ \int_0^{\omega_m}g\,d\omega&=\frac1a\end{aligned}$$ (12)

One mode per atom per branch, as the counting demands. The DOS is what heat capacity integrates over (Debye and Einstein models, topic 06). The derivation is homework guide 6.2. Used in 1.7.1 → heat capacity as a DOS integral, Debye and Einstein models.

Counting only one root. Each ω below ω_m is reached at both +k₀ and −k₀ in the zone; dropping one halves g and breaks the normalisation to one mode per atom.

Where does the chain's DOS diverge, and why is the total number of modes still finite?

At $\omega_m$, where $v_g=0$. Near it $g\propto(\omega_m-\omega)^{-1/2}$, which is integrable: the integral is $1/a$, one mode per atom.

$g(\omega)\propto1/|d\omega/dk|$: flat parts of the dispersion pile up modes, and zero group velocity gives a van Hove singularity.

You can now derive the 1D density of states of the chain and say where it diverges and why. That is Sheet 6.2; Model 3 does the same count live.

1.6.5Phonons

Where you meet this. Diamond has no free electrons, yet it conducts heat about five times better than copper (roughly 2000 against 400 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹), which is why diamond heat spreaders sit under some high-power laser diodes. Every joule of that heat is carried by phonons, the quanta of this section.

What this covers. Quantised mode energies, the phonon as one quantum of a mode, its thermal occupation, and crystal momentum ħk defined only up to ħG. Lecture notes p33.

Why it matters. The Planck occupation is where the heat capacity on topic 06 starts. Conservation of crystal momentum modulo ħG is the Umklapp rule of transport (topic 08) and the selection rule of neutron scattering (1.6.6).

1.6.5 · Quanta and crystal momentum

Phonons

1.6.1 · What k means, 1.6.2 · Solution

Each normal mode $(\mathbf k,s)$ is an independent harmonic oscillator, so quantum mechanics allows only the energies

Energy of one mode
$$E_{\mathbf k,s}=\hbar\omega_s(\mathbf k)\Big(n_{\mathbf k,s}+\frac12\Big)$$ (13)
Energy of the crystal
$$\begin{aligned}E&=\sum_{\mathbf k,s}\hbar\omega_s(\mathbf k)\Big(n_{\mathbf k,s}+\frac12\Big)\\ n&=0,1,2,\dots\end{aligned}$$ (14)

A phonon is one quantum of a mode; $n_{\mathbf k,s}$ is the number of phonons in it, and $\frac12\hbar\omega$ is the zero-point energy. In equilibrium the mean occupation is the Planck (Bose–Einstein) value

Thermal occupation
$$\langle n\rangle=\frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT}-1}$$ (15)

Example: the top mode of the Na chain in Model 4 has $\hbar\omega=14.9$ meV; at 300 K ($k_BT=25.9$ meV) $\langle n\rangle=1.28$, at 10 K only $3\times10^{-8}$. At low $T$ only the soft acoustic modes near $k=0$ are excited, which gives the $T^3$ law. Used in 1.7.1 → partition function and (★).

Crystal momentum. In collisions a phonon acts as if it had momentum $\hbar\mathbf k$, but it is a pattern of relative displacements and carries no real momentum (except the uniform $\mathbf k=0$ translation). Since $\mathbf k$ and $\mathbf k+\mathbf G$ label the same mode, $\hbar\mathbf k$ is only defined up to $\hbar\mathbf G$, and in a scattering process it is conserved only modulo $\hbar\mathbf G$: the lattice as a whole takes up $\hbar\mathbf G$.

Tutor board wording: the board calls $n_{\mathbf k,l}$ the number of "available" phonons. It is the number of phonons present in the mode (the occupation number). The board's branch index $l$ is the notes' $s$.

Calling ħk the momentum of a phonon. It is crystal momentum: defined only up to ħG and conserved in collisions only modulo ħG.

A mode has ħω = 14.9 meV. How many phonons does it hold on average at 300 K?

$x=14.9/25.9=0.576$, so $\langle n\rangle=1/(e^{0.576}-1)=1/0.78=1.28$.

Each mode is a quantum oscillator with energy ħω(n + ½); n counts its phonons, Planck-distributed in equilibrium. ħk is crystal momentum, defined modulo ħG.

You can now write the energy of the vibrating crystal in phonons, give the thermal occupation of a mode, and say why ħk is not a true momentum. Topic 06 starts from exactly this.

1.6.6Measurement of phonons

Where you meet this. The phonon dispersion curves in textbooks, including the sodium curves behind Model 4 (Kittel ch. 4 Fig. 11), were measured by firing neutrons from research reactors at single crystals. Bertram Brockhouse shared the 1994 Nobel Prize in Physics for developing this neutron spectroscopy.

What this covers. Inelastic neutron scattering with its two conservation laws, why thermal neutrons match phonons in both momentum and energy, and why light (Raman, infrared) reaches only k ≈ 0. Lecture notes p33.

Why it matters. It is where every measured ω(k) comes from, and it explains why infrared spectra see only optical phonons (1.6.2). It uses crystal momentum modulo ħG from 1.6.5.

1.6.6 · Neutrons and light

Mapping ω(k)

1.6.5 · Phonons

Inelastic neutron scattering maps the whole $\omega(\mathbf k)$. A neutron enters with $\mathbf k$, leaves with $\mathbf k'$, and creates (+) or absorbs (−) a phonon $\mathbf K$ (Kittel eqs. 33–34):

Crystal momentum and energy balance
$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf k+\mathbf G&=\mathbf k'\pm\mathbf K\\ \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2M_n}&=\frac{\hbar^2k'^2}{2M_n}\pm\hbar\omega(\mathbf K)\end{aligned}$$ (16)

Measuring the energy lost and the direction gives one point $(\mathbf K,\omega)$ on the dispersion, with $\mathbf G$ chosen so that $\mathbf K$ lies in the first zone. Neutrons suit the job because a thermal neutron (25 meV) has $k=3.5\times10^{10}$ m⁻¹ ($\lambda=0.18$ nm), comparable with the zone size $\pi/a\sim10^{10}$ m⁻¹, and an energy comparable with phonon energies (10–60 meV).

Light (Raman scattering, infrared absorption) sees only $\mathbf k\approx0$. A photon of the same 25 meV has $k=1.3\times10^5$ m⁻¹, and even visible light at 2 eV has $k=1.0\times10^7$ m⁻¹, about $10^{-3}$ of the zone. At $k\approx0$ acoustic modes have $\omega\approx0$, so light probes the optical modes. Example: NaCl absorbs infrared at its transverse optical frequency $\omega_T=3.1\times10^{13}$ s⁻¹ ($\hbar\omega_T=20$ meV, $\lambda=61\ \mu$m).

Forgetting the G in the momentum balance. The phonon created has the first-zone K = q − G, which can be far from the momentum transfer q itself.

Why can a 2 eV photon not create a zone-boundary phonon?

Its wave vector, $1.0\times10^7$ m⁻¹, is about $10^{-3}$ of $\pi/a$. Crystal momentum must balance, so it can only make phonons with $K\approx0$.

Neutrons match phonons in both k and energy, so they map the whole zone. Photons carry almost no momentum, so light sees only k ≈ 0, which means the optical modes.

You can now write the two conservation laws of neutron scattering and explain why neutrons map the whole zone while light sees only k ≈ 0. That is part (d) of the NaCl exercise and the last bullet of the exam angle; Model 4 shows which phonons a neutron can create.

1.6.6 · Neutron scattering (uses 1.6.5 crystal momentum)

Which phonons can a neutron create?

A neutron hits a chain of sodium atoms along [001] (spacing d = 0.211 nm, ν_max = 3.6 THz from Kittel ch. 4 Fig. 11) and scatters forward or back. Energy and crystal momentum must both balance: the neutron curve (blue) has to meet the phonon dispersion (copper). The dispersion repeats in every zone, so the neutron can hand $\hbar G$ to the lattice and create a phonon whose first-zone $K$ is far from its momentum transfer $q$.

Try
  • The opening state is a thermal neutron at 25 meV. Its curve meets the dispersion twice: inside the first zone, and again two zones out at q ≈ 4.3π/d. That second phonon has K = 0.31π/d; the lattice took up G = 2·2π/d. The readout does this reduction, K = q − G, for every event.
  • Lower E_i to a few meV: the neutron curve gets small and only crosses near q = 0. Slow neutrons reach only long-wavelength phonons.
  • Compare the photon line (dotted, at q ≈ 0): a photon of the same energy has 10⁵ times less momentum and can only reach K ≈ 0. That is why Raman and infrared see only k ≈ 0 modes.
  • Tick absorption: the parabola below the axis meets the mirrored dispersion. Such events need a phonon already present, so they get rare at low T.

Sheet 6 · Problem 1 · 4 pts

l-th nearest-neighbor interaction

A simple linear chain has springs $f_l$ between $l$-th nearest neighbours, so the total potential is $U=\sum_n\sum_{l>0}0.5\,f_l\,[u(na)-u((n+l)a)]^2$. Show that the dispersion relation becomes $\omega(k)=2\cdot\big[\sum_{l>0}f_l\sin^2(lka/2)/m\big]^{1/2}$.

Method

Exactly the lecture's route for the nearest-neighbour chain (1.6.1): force $=-\partial U/\partial u_n$, then the plane-wave ansatz. The only new step is finding every term of the double sum that contains $u_n$: for each $l$ there are two, the spring to the right neighbour $n+l$ and the spring from the left neighbour $n-l$.

Show worked solution

Steps

  1. Write $u_n=u(na)$. For a fixed $l$, $u_n$ appears in the term with index $n$ (pair $n,n+l$) and in the term with index $n-l$ (pair $n-l,n$):
    $$\begin{aligned}&\tfrac12f_l(u_n-u_{n+l})^2\\&\quad+\tfrac12f_l(u_{n-l}-u_n)^2\end{aligned}$$
  2. Differentiate (all sums over $l>0$):
    $$\begin{aligned}F_n&=-\frac{\partial U}{\partial u_n}\\&=-\sum_{l}f_l\big[(u_n-u_{n+l})\\&\qquad+(u_n-u_{n-l})\big]\\&=\sum_{l}f_l\,(u_{n+l}+u_{n-l}\\&\qquad-2u_n)\end{aligned}$$
  3. Equation of motion $m\ddot u_n=F_n$ with $u_n=u_0\,e^{i(kna-\omega t)}$, so $u_{n\pm l}=u_n\,e^{\pm ilka}$ and $\ddot u_n=-\omega^2u_n$:
    $$\begin{aligned}-m\omega^2&=\sum_{l}f_l\,\big(e^{ilka}\\&\qquad+e^{-ilka}-2\big)\end{aligned}$$
  4. Use $e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}-2=2\cos\theta-2=-4\sin^2(\theta/2)$ with $\theta=lka$: $\;m\omega^2=4\sum_{l>0}f_l\sin^2(lka/2)$.
  5. Take the positive root.
Result: $\omega(k)=2\Big[\dfrac1m\sum_{l>0}f_l\sin^2\dfrac{lka}{2}\Big]^{1/2}$, identical to Kittel's eq. (16a), $\omega^2=\frac2M\sum_{p>0}C_p(1-\cos pKa)$.
Sanity check: only $f_1$: $\omega=\sqrt{4f_1/m}\,|\sin(ka/2)|$, the lecture result. Long waves: $\omega^2\approx\frac{k^2a^2}{m}\sum_l l^2f_l$, still linear in $k$, with $v_s^2=\frac{a^2}{m}\sum_l l^2f_l$. The result is still periodic in $2\pi/a$ (each $\sin^2(lka/2)$ is), so the first zone is unchanged. Numerically (node), diagonalising a 12-atom ring with $f_1=1$, $f_2=0.4$, $f_3=-0.1$ reproduces all 12 values of $\omega^2$ to $10^{-14}$. Model 3 with $f_2\neq0$ plots this formula.
Sheet 6 · Problem 2 · 4 pts

Density of states

For a (simple) linear chain with period $a$, atoms of mass $m$ and nearest-neighbour force constant $f$, calculate the 1D density of states $g(\omega)$. Hints: (1) in 1D the 1.BZ integration over $\delta(\omega-\omega(k))$ reduces to individual points; (2) use the lecture's dispersion $\omega(k)=\sqrt{4f/m}\cdot|\sin(\tfrac12ka)|$.

Method

Write $g$ as the zone integral of $\delta(\omega-\omega(k))$ with weight $dk/2\pi$ (one allowed $k$ per $2\pi/L$, periodic boundary conditions). Use $\delta(h(k))=\sum_i\delta(k-k_i)/|h'(k_i)|$ over the roots $k_i$, then express $d\omega/dk$ through $\omega$ itself.

Assumption: $g(\omega)$ is per unit length (the lecture's per-volume convention in 1D) and for one polarisation, counting $\omega\ge0$. Multiply by $L=Na$ for Kittel's whole-crystal $D(\omega)$, or by $a$ for modes per atom.
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. $g(\omega)=\displaystyle\int_{-\pi/a}^{\pi/a}\frac{dk}{2\pi}\,\delta\big(\omega-\omega(k)\big)$, with $\omega(k)=\omega_m|\sin(ka/2)|$ and $\omega_m=\sqrt{4f/m}$.
  2. For $0\le\omega\lt\omega_m$ the first zone holds two roots, $k=\pm k_0$ with $k_0=\frac2a\arcsin(\omega/\omega_m)$. For $\omega\gt\omega_m$ there is none, so $g=0$.
  3. Slope at the roots: $|d\omega/dk|=\frac{a\omega_m}{2}\cos\frac{k_0a}{2}=\frac a2\sqrt{\omega_m^2-\omega^2}$, using $\cos(\arcsin x)=\sqrt{1-x^2}$.
  4. Each root contributes $\dfrac1{2\pi}\dfrac{1}{|d\omega/dk|}=\dfrac{1}{\pi a\sqrt{\omega_m^2-\omega^2}}$, and there are two.
Result: $g(\omega)=\dfrac{2}{\pi a}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\omega_m^2-\omega^2}}=\dfrac{2}{\pi a}\Big(\dfrac{4f}{m}-\omega^2\Big)^{-1/2}$ for $0\le\omega\lt\sqrt{4f/m}$, and 0 above. For the whole chain, $D(\omega)=Lg=\dfrac{2N}{\pi}(\omega_m^2-\omega^2)^{-1/2}$. It diverges (integrably) at $\omega_m$, where $v_g=0$.
Sanity check: normalisation $\int_0^{\omega_m}g\,d\omega=\frac{2}{\pi a}\cdot\frac\pi2=\frac1a$: one mode per atom. Low frequency: $g(0)=\frac{2}{\pi a\omega_m}=\frac1{\pi v_s}$ with $v_s=a\omega_m/2$, the DOS of a 1D continuum with sound speed $v_s$ (two directions, each $\frac{1}{2\pi v_s}$). $D(\omega)$ matches Kittel ch. 5 problem 1. A node histogram of $\omega(k)$ over $2\times10^5$ k-points agrees with the bin-averaged formula to 4 digits. Model 3 does the same count live.

Exam angle

Not asked in 2022. The recovered paper (reconstructed from memory, possibly incomplete) has no question on lattice vibrations. The topic list includes 1.6 in full, though, and sheet 6 sets the level: short derivations on a 1D chain. In 5 minutes without notes you should be able to:

  • derive the monatomic dispersion from the chain potential and state its sound speed and zone-boundary behaviour;
  • explain in one sentence why $k$ is restricted to the first zone (the atoms only sample the wave at $x=ja$, so $k$ and $k+2\pi/a$ are the same motion);
  • set up the two-atom chain, get $\omega_\pm$ at $k=0$ and $\pi/a$, sketch both branches with the gap, and describe the acoustic and optical eigenvectors;
  • derive the 1D DOS (sheet 6.2) and say where it diverges;
  • state why neutrons map the whole zone and light only $k\approx0$.
Quick theory checks (with answers)
  1. Why restrict k to [−π/a, π/a]? The displacements at the atoms for $k$ and $k+2\pi/a$ are identical, so a larger range double-counts modes. A chain of $N$ atoms has exactly $N$ modes per branch.
  2. Group velocity at the zone edge, and its meaning? Zero. The mode is a standing wave, $u_j\propto(-1)^j$: Bragg reflection of the lattice wave.
  3. Is ħk the momentum of a phonon? No. It is crystal momentum, conserved in collisions only modulo $\hbar G$.
  4. How many branches for a crystal with 2 atoms per primitive cell? 6: 3 acoustic (LA, 2 TA) and 3 optical (LO, 2 TO).
Exam-style exercise · 2022 pattern

A diatomic chain for NaCl

Model NaCl along [111] as a 1D chain of alternating Cl ($m_1=35.45$ u) and Na ($m_2=22.99$ u) with equal nearest-neighbour springs $f$ and repeat distance $a=0.325$ nm. The optical frequency at $k=0$ is $\omega_T=3.1\times10^{13}$ s⁻¹. (a) Find $f$. (b) Find both frequencies at $k=\pi/a$ and the width of the gap. (c) Find the sound velocity of the acoustic branch. (d) Give the amplitude ratio of Cl to Na in the optical mode at $k=0$, and say why this mode absorbs infrared light. (1 u $=1.6605\times10^{-27}$ kg.)

Show worked solution
  1. (a) $\omega_+(0)=\sqrt{2f/\mu}$ with $\mu=m_1m_2/(m_1+m_2)=13.95$ u $=2.316\times10^{-26}$ kg. So $f=\mu\omega_T^2/2=11.1$ N/m.
  2. (b) At $k=\pi/a$: acoustic $\sqrt{2f/m_1}=1.94\times10^{13}$ s⁻¹ (3.10 THz), optical $\sqrt{2f/m_2}=2.41\times10^{13}$ s⁻¹ (3.84 THz). The gap is $4.7\times10^{12}$ s⁻¹ wide.
  3. (c) $v_s=a\sqrt{f/2(m_1+m_2)}$ with $m_1+m_2=9.704\times10^{-26}$ kg: $v_s=2.46$ km/s.
  4. (d) $u/v=-m_2/m_1=-0.649$. Cl moves 0.65 times as far as Na, in the opposite direction, so the centre of mass is at rest. The opposite charges of Cl⁻ and Na⁺ then form an oscillating dipole, which the electric field of light at $\omega_T$ can drive. A photon at that frequency has $k\approx10^5$ m⁻¹, so it couples only to the $k\approx0$ mode.
Result: $f\approx11$ N/m; $\omega(\pi/a)=1.94$ and $2.41\times10^{13}$ s⁻¹ (gap $4.7\times10^{12}$ s⁻¹); $v_s\approx2.5$ km/s; $u_{Cl}/u_{Na}=-0.65$.
Assumption: a 1D nearest-neighbour toy, calibrated to one number. $\omega_T$ is from Kittel ch. 14 Table 3 and $a=5.63\,\text{Å}/\sqrt3$ is the spacing of identical (111) planes, with Kittel's cubic lattice constant 5.63 Å. Real NaCl has long-range Coulomb forces, so the other numbers are practice values, not predictions.
Sanity check: the zone-boundary ratio $2.414/1.944=1.242=\sqrt{m_1/m_2}$; $v_s$ from the numerical slope of $\omega_-(k)$ near $k=0$ agrees (2461 m/s, node). Model 2 opens in exactly this state, so the numbers can be read off its readout.
