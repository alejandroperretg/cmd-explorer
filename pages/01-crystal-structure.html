Crystal structure and symmetry

A crystal is a lattice of points with the same group of atoms (the basis) attached to every point. Everything later in the course is built on this: the reciprocal lattice, Brillouin zones, phonons and Bloch electrons all start from the primitive vectors.

Overview

This topic answers three questions. Where are the atoms? Answer with a lattice, its primitive vectors and a basis (1.1.1). Which operations leave the lattice unchanged? Rotations, reflections and inversion, and only 1-, 2-, 3-, 4- and 6-fold rotation axes are possible. That restriction gives exactly 14 Bravais lattices (1.1.2). And how densely can atoms be packed? You need the coordination number and the packing fraction (1.1.5).

Key results to leave with: the fcc and bcc primitive vectors and their cell volume ($a^3/4$ and $a^3/2$), the coordination numbers (sc 6, bcc 8, fcc and hcp 12, diamond 4), and the packing fractions (0.524, 0.680, 0.740, 0.740, 0.340). The 2022 exam asked for all of it for fcc (Q1).

Read alongside Kittel, Introduction to Solid State Physics 8th ed., ch. 1 (Crystal structure: lattice and basis, primitive cell, lattice types, index system, simple crystal structures; pp. 3–19), and the lecture notes pp. 2–7 and 16.

Direct imaging (1.1.3) is taught on the reciprocal lattice page: 1.1.3 Direct imaging →. The notes treat it with diffraction ("1.2"), and sheet 2.3 has the numbers: TEM needs an electron wavelength $\lambda=h/p$ below the atomic spacing, and the STM current falls as $I\propto e^{-2\kappa d}$, so a fraction of an ångström in height changes $I$ strongly.

Concepts

1.1.1Primitive lattice

Where you meet this. A 300 mm silicon wafer is one single crystal. Every one of its 5.0×10²² atoms per cm³ sits on an fcc lattice point or at one fixed offset from it, which is why a wafer cleaves along flat planes and why its orientation is printed as a (100) or (111) index.

What this covers. Lattice versus basis, primitive vectors, the primitive, conventional and Wigner–Seitz cells (notes pp. 2–3; Kittel ch. 1).

Why it matters. Sheet 1.1 is a Bravais-lattice test, and exam Q1b asks for the fcc primitive vectors. The reciprocal lattice (topic 02), Brillouin zones and Bloch electrons all start from the primitive vectors.

1.1.1 · Lattice and basis

Lattice, basis, crystal structure

A lattice is an infinite set of mathematical points that looks the same from every point. Each point is an integer combination of three linearly independent primitive translation vectors:

Lattice vectorSheet 1.1
$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf R&=n_1\mathbf a_1+n_2\mathbf a_2+n_3\mathbf a_3,\\ n_i&\in\mathbb Z.\end{aligned}$$ (1)

A basis is the group of atoms attached identically to every lattice point. Atom $j$ sits at $\mathbf r_j = x_j\mathbf a_1+y_j\mathbf a_2+z_j\mathbf a_3$ with $0\le x_j,y_j,z_j\lt 1$. Lattice + basis = crystal structure. Think of wallpaper: the lattice says where the motif goes and the basis says what it is.

This is why a crystal's flat faces always meet at the same angles: every face is a lattice plane, so the angle between two faces is an angle between lattice planes, fixed by the lattice and not by how the sample grew. Cleavage along planes and direction-dependent properties (anisotropy) follow from the same fact.

Notation: the lecture notes write the lattice vector as $\mathbf R$, Kittel as $\mathbf T$. This page uses $\mathbf R$ and keeps $\mathbf T$ for the translation part of a symmetry operation, as the notes do.

Equating lattice points with atoms. A lattice point is a position, not an atom. Diamond has 8 atoms per cube but only 4 lattice points: the lattice is fcc and each point carries a 2-atom basis.

NaCl has 4 Na⁺ and 4 Cl⁻ per cube. What are its lattice and basis?

fcc (4 points per cube) with a 2-ion basis: Cl⁻ at 000 and Na⁺ at ½½½. 4 points × 2 ions = 8 ions per cube.

Used in 1.3.2 Reciprocal lattice → (Fourier series over the lattice).

Crystal structure = lattice (1), integer combinations of primitive vectors, plus a basis of atoms attached to every point.

1.1.1 · Unit cells

Primitive cell and Wigner–Seitz cell

1.1.1 Lattice and basis

A primitive cell fills all space without gaps or overlaps when repeated by the lattice translations, and contains exactly one lattice point. The parallelepiped spanned by $\mathbf a_1,\mathbf a_2,\mathbf a_3$ is one choice, with volume

Primitive cell volumeExam Q1bSheet 1.1
$$V_c=|\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)|.$$ (2)

The choice of primitive vectors is not unique, but every primitive cell has the same volume. A conventional cell (e.g. the cube of fcc) is larger and shows the symmetry more clearly. To count points in it, a corner point counts ⅛, an edge point ¼, a face point ½ and an interior point 1.

The Wigner–Seitz cell is the set of points of space closer to one lattice point than to any other. Construct it by drawing the perpendicular bisector planes to the lines joining that point to its neighbours and keeping the smallest enclosed volume. It is primitive and has the full symmetry of the lattice.

Counting every corner of the cube as a whole point. A corner is shared by 8 cubes, so it counts ⅛. Otherwise the fcc cube seems to hold 14 points instead of 4.

The fcc cube (edge a) holds how many lattice points, and what is V_c?

$8\cdot\tfrac18+6\cdot\tfrac12=4$ points, so $V_c=a^3/4$. The triple product (2) of the fcc vectors gives the same.

Used in 1.3.2 Reciprocal lattice → ($V_{BZ}=(2\pi)^3/V_c$) and 1.3.3 Brillouin zones → (the Wigner–Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice).

A primitive cell holds one lattice point and has volume (2), whatever vectors you pick. The conventional cell holds several and shows the symmetry.

You can now test whether a set of points is a Bravais lattice, and choose primitive vectors and check them with the triple product. That is Sheet 1.1 and the cell-volume part of Exam Q1b.

1.1.2Lattice classification by symmetry

Where you meet this. Snowflakes are six-sided because ice is a hexagonal crystal. In 1982 Dan Shechtman saw a sharp ten-fold diffraction pattern from an Al–Mn alloy, a symmetry that the argument below forbids for any lattice. The resolution was a new kind of ordered but non-periodic solid, the quasicrystal, and the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

What this covers. Rotations, reflections and inversion as 3×3 matrices, why only 1-, 2-, 3-, 4- and 6-fold axes fit a lattice, the 14 Bravais lattices in 7 crystal systems, the sc, bcc and fcc primitive vectors, and the Miller-index notation for planes and directions (notes pp. 4–5, 9; Kittel ch. 1).

Why it matters. Sheet 1.3 asks for six symmetry matrices, sheet 1.1a needs the Bravais list, and exam Q1a–b asks for the symmetry and primitive vectors of fcc.

1.1.2 · Symmetry operations

Symmetry operations as matrices

1.1.1 Lattice and basis

A symmetry operation maps the lattice onto itself:

Symmetry operation (T a lattice translation)
$$\mathbf R'=M\mathbf R+\mathbf T.$$ (3)

$M$ is a 3×3 matrix that must keep lengths, so it is orthogonal ($M^TM=\mathbb 1$, $\det M=\pm1$):

  • rotation $C_n$ by $2\pi/n$ ($\det M=+1$);
  • inversion $M=-\mathbb 1$, $\mathbf r\to-\mathbf r$;
  • reflection in a plane with unit normal $\hat{\mathbf n}$, given by (4). This equals inversion after a 180° rotation about $\hat{\mathbf n}$, as stated in the notes (p. 4).
Reflection in the plane with unit normal n̂Sheet 1.3d, f
$$M=\mathbb 1-2\hat{\mathbf n}\hat{\mathbf n}^T.$$ (4)

Practical rule: the columns of $M$ are the images of $\hat{\mathbf x},\hat{\mathbf y},\hat{\mathbf z}$. Rotation about a unit axis $\hat{\mathbf n}$ by $\varphi$ (Rodrigues):

Rotation by φ about the unit axis n̂ (Rodrigues)Sheet 1.3a, c, e
$$\begin{aligned}M&=\cos\varphi\,\mathbb 1+\sin\varphi\,[\hat{\mathbf n}]_\times\\&\quad+(1-\cos\varphi)\,\hat{\mathbf n}\hat{\mathbf n}^T.\end{aligned}$$ (5)

Here $[\hat{\mathbf n}]_\times\mathbf v = \hat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf v$. Try it in Model 2.

Mixing active and passive rotations. Rotating the point by φ (active) and rotating the axes by φ (passive) give transposed matrices. State which one you use; sheet 1.3 says "coordinate transformation".

Write M for a 90° rotation about ẑ, column by column.

$\hat{\mathbf x}\to\hat{\mathbf y}$, $\hat{\mathbf y}\to-\hat{\mathbf x}$, $\hat{\mathbf z}\to\hat{\mathbf z}$, so the columns are $(0,1,0)$, $(-1,0,0)$, $(0,0,1)$: $M=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$, with $\det M=+1$.

Read off any symmetry matrix by columns (images of x̂, ŷ, ẑ). Rotations have det +1; inversion and reflections (4) have det −1; oblique axes need (5).

1.1.2 · Crystallographic restriction

Why only 1-, 2-, 3-, 4- and 6-fold axes

1.1.2 Symmetry operations

In the basis $\mathbf a_1,\mathbf a_2,\mathbf a_3$ a symmetry maps lattice vectors to lattice vectors, so its matrix has integer entries and an integer trace. The trace does not depend on the basis. In a Cartesian frame with $z$ along the axis, a rotation by $\varphi$ has trace $1+2\cos\varphi$. Hence

Allowed rotation angles of a lattice
$$\begin{aligned}2\cos\varphi&\in\mathbb Z\\ \Rightarrow\ \varphi&\in\{0^\circ,60^\circ,90^\circ,\\&\qquad 120^\circ,180^\circ\}\end{aligned}$$ (6)

and their negatives. That is $n = 1, 6, 4, 3, 2$. A 5-fold axis ($2\cos72^\circ=0.618$) is impossible: pentagons cannot tile a plane.

C₂180°−2
C₃120°−1
C₄90°0

Notes slip: the notes' table of basic rotations (p. 4) labels the 6-fold rotation "C₅"; it is $C_6$ (60°). The notes also list 240°, 270°, 300°, which are the same operations as −120°, −90°, −60°.

Reading (6) as "every allowed angle is a symmetry of every lattice". It only lists what is possible. A cube has no 60° axis: 60° about [111] (sheet 1.3e) is a valid matrix but not a symmetry of the cubic lattice.

Can a lattice have an 8-fold axis?

No: $2\cos45^\circ=\sqrt2=1.414$ is not an integer.

Integer trace forces $2\cos\varphi\in\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}$: only 1-, 2-, 3-, 4- and 6-fold axes fit a lattice.

1.1.2 · Bravais lattices

The 14 Bravais lattices

1.1.2 Allowed axes

Sheet 1.1a

In 2D there are 5 lattice types: oblique, square, hexagonal, rectangular, centred rectangular. In 3D, combining the allowed point symmetries with centring gives 14, grouped into 7 crystal systems by the shape of the conventional cell (Kittel Table 1). P = primitive, I = body-centred, F = face-centred, C = base-centred, R = rhombohedral.

1+2+4+2+3+1+1 = 14. Missing combinations reduce to listed ones. Example: "base-centred cubic" is just simple tetragonal (sheet 1.1a, Model 1).

Why is there no "base-centred cubic" among the 14?

Centring two opposite faces breaks the 3-fold axes, and the result is a lattice already listed: simple tetragonal with $a'=a/\sqrt2$, $c=a$ (sheet 1.1a).

7 crystal systems × allowed centrings = 14 Bravais lattices; any other centring is one of them in disguise.

Show that the fcc vectors (9) are 60° apart.

$\mathbf a_1\cdot\mathbf a_2=\tfrac{a^2}4$ and $|\mathbf a_i|^2=\tfrac{a^2}2$, so $\cos\theta=\tfrac12$ and $\theta=60^\circ$. For bcc, $\mathbf a_1\cdot\mathbf a_2=-\tfrac{a^2}4$ and $|\mathbf a_i|^2=\tfrac{3a^2}4$ give $\cos\theta=-\tfrac13$, $\theta=109.47^\circ$.

1.1.2 · Index system

Planes and directions: (hkl), {hkl}, [uvw], ⟨uvw⟩

1.1.1 Lattice and basis; 1.1.2 Cubic lattices

The notes close 1.1.2 with this notation ("for sc; others are more complex", p. 5) and return to it on p. 9. This answers the open "what does this mean exactly?" there.

  • (hkl): a plane, or the set of parallel planes. Take the intercepts with the axes in units of $\mathbf a_1,\mathbf a_2,\mathbf a_3$, take reciprocals, and scale to the smallest integers. Equivalently, the plane passes through $\mathbf a_1/h$, $\mathbf a_2/k$, $\mathbf a_3/l$. An intercept at ∞ gives 0. A negative index is written with a bar: $(\bar 1 00)$.
  • Example: intercepts 1, 2, ∞ → reciprocals 1, ½, 0 → (210).
  • {hkl}: all planes equivalent by symmetry. {100} = the six cube faces.
  • [uvw]: a direction, the smallest integers along $u\mathbf a_1+v\mathbf a_2+w\mathbf a_3$. ⟨uvw⟩: the family of equivalent directions, e.g. ⟨111⟩ = the body diagonals.
  • In cubic crystals only, [hkl] ⟂ (hkl).

Using the intercepts themselves as indices. The indices are the reciprocals of the intercepts, cleared of fractions. Intercepts 1, 2, ∞ give (210), not (12∞).

A plane cuts the axes at 2a₁, 3a₂ and runs parallel to a₃. Its Miller indices?

Reciprocals ½, ⅓, 0; multiply by 6: (320).

Used in 1.3.1 Bragg's law → (plane spacing d) and 1.5.5 Young's modulus → (directions such as [110]).

(hkl) are reciprocal intercepts, [uvw] are vector components; braces and angle brackets mean the whole symmetry family.

You can now write any rotation or reflection matrix, say why 5-fold axes are impossible, place a lattice in its crystal system, give the cubic primitive vectors, and name planes and directions. That is Sheet 1.3, Sheet 1.1a and Exam Q1a–b.

1.1.5Simple crystal structures

Where you meet this. Cryostats and LNG piping use austenitic stainless steel, which is fcc, because fcc metals stay ductile down to liquid-helium temperature: their close-packed planes slip instead of cracking. Ordinary bcc steel turns brittle in the cold. The coordination numbers and packing fractions below are the first step in telling the two apart.

What this covers. The common structures as lattice + basis (NaCl, CsCl, hcp, diamond, zinc blende), coordination numbers, and how to compute a packing fraction (notes pp. 6–7, 16; Kittel ch. 1).

Why it matters. Sheet 1.2 asks for CN and packing fraction of fcc, hcp, sc and diamond, and exam Q1c–d asked the same for fcc. Diamond and zinc blende return as the semiconductor structures, and the structure factor (topic 02) needs the basis.

The coordination number (CN) is the number of nearest neighbours. Put touching spheres of radius $r$ = half the nearest-neighbour distance on every atom; the packing fraction is the share of space they fill:

Packing fraction (N_cell atoms in a cell of volume V_cell)Exam Q1dSheet 1.2Exam-relevant derivation
$$f=\frac{N_{\rm cell}\,\tfrac43\pi r^3}{V_{\rm cell}}.$$ (10)

Any cell works, as long as $N_{\rm cell}$ and $V_{\rm cell}$ belong to the same one. fcc and hcp reach 0.740, the densest packing of equal spheres; sc 0.524; diamond only 0.340.

Taking $r=a/2$ in fcc. The spheres touch along the face diagonal, not the cube edge: $4r=\sqrt2a$. With $r=a/2$ the "packing fraction" comes out above 1.

bcc: along which direction do spheres touch, and what is f?

Along the body diagonal: $4r=\sqrt3a$. Two atoms per cube: $f=2\cdot\tfrac43\pi(\sqrt3a/4)^3/a^3=\pi\sqrt3/8=0.680$.

Used in 1.3.5 Structure factor → (the basis decides which reflections vanish), 1.4.2 Madelung energy → (NaCl, CsCl) and 1.4.3 Covalent crystals → (diamond).

Name a structure as lattice + basis; find where spheres touch to get $r(a)$; then (10). fcc = hcp 0.740 > bcc 0.680 > sc 0.524 > diamond 0.340.

You can now describe NaCl, CsCl, hcp, diamond and zinc blende as lattice + basis and compute any coordination number and packing fraction. That is Sheet 1.2 and Exam Q1c–d.

Homework guides

Sheet 1 · Problem 1 · 4 pts

Bravais lattices

Is it a primitive (Bravais) lattice? If yes, give three primitive translation vectors; if not, describe it as a Bravais lattice with as small a basis as possible. (a) Base-centred cubic: sc plus points in the centres of the horizontal faces. (b) Side-centred cubic: sc plus points in the centres of the vertical faces. (c) Edge-centred cubic: sc plus points at the midpoints of the lines joining nearest neighbours.

Method

First count points per cube (corner ⅛, face ½, edge ¼). Then test the defining property of a lattice: every point must see the same surroundings. Take the vector $\mathbf t$ from a corner to one of the extra points. If adding $\mathbf t$ to every point lands on a point, $\mathbf t$ is a lattice translation. If it fails for even one point, the set is not a lattice, and only the sc translations remain, with the extra points as basis atoms.

Assumption: "horizontal" faces are the two faces normal to z (top and bottom); "vertical" faces are the four normal to x and y. The cube edge is a.
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. (a) Points per cube: $8\cdot\tfrac18 + 2\cdot\tfrac12 = 2$, at $0$ and $\tfrac a2(1,1,0)$. Adding $\mathbf t=\tfrac a2(1,1,0)$ sends corners to top/bottom face centres and face centres to corners, e.g. $\tfrac a2(1,1,0)+\mathbf t = a(1,1,0)$. Every point lands on a point, so it is a Bravais lattice.
  2. Primitive vectors: $\mathbf a_1=\tfrac a2(1,1,0)$, $\mathbf a_2=\tfrac a2(-1,1,0)$, $\mathbf a_3=a(0,0,1)$. Check: $V_c=|\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)| = a^3/2$, i.e. one point per primitive cell for two per cube.
  3. $|\mathbf a_1|=|\mathbf a_2|=a/\sqrt2$, and all three vectors are mutually perpendicular, so this is simple tetragonal with $a'=a/\sqrt2$, $c=a$. That is why "base-centred cubic" is not among the 14.
  4. (b) Points per cube: $8\cdot\tfrac18 + 4\cdot\tfrac12 = 3$, at $0$, $\tfrac a2(1,0,1)$, $\tfrac a2(0,1,1)$. Try $\mathbf t=\tfrac a2(1,0,1)$: the point $\tfrac a2(0,1,1)$ goes to $\tfrac a2(1,1,2)$, the centre of a horizontal face, which is empty. The other candidates $\tfrac a2(0,1,1)$ and $\tfrac a2(1,-1,0)$ fail the same way. Not a Bravais lattice.
  5. Only the sc translations map the structure onto itself, so it is sc ($a\hat{\mathbf x}, a\hat{\mathbf y}, a\hat{\mathbf z}$) with a 3-point basis: $0$, $\tfrac a2(1,0,1)$, $\tfrac a2(0,1,1)$.
  6. (c) Points per cube: $8\cdot\tfrac18 + 12\cdot\tfrac14 = 4$, at $0$, $\tfrac a2\hat{\mathbf x}$, $\tfrac a2\hat{\mathbf y}$, $\tfrac a2\hat{\mathbf z}$. Try $\mathbf t=\tfrac a2\hat{\mathbf x}$: $\tfrac a2\hat{\mathbf y}$ goes to $\tfrac a2(1,1,0)$, a face centre, which is empty. The other differences fail the same way. Not a Bravais lattice: sc with a 4-point basis $0$, $\tfrac a2\hat{\mathbf x}$, $\tfrac a2\hat{\mathbf y}$, $\tfrac a2\hat{\mathbf z}$.
Result: (a) Bravais, simple tetragonal: $\tfrac a2(1,1,0)$, $\tfrac a2(-1,1,0)$, $a(0,0,1)$. (b) sc + 3-atom basis. (c) sc + 4-atom basis.
Sanity check: basis size × (points per primitive cell of the true lattice) must equal points per cube: (a) $a^3/(a^3/2)=2$ ✓, (b) 3 × 1 = 3 ✓, (c) 4 × 1 = 4 ✓. Model 1 "Base-centred" shows the 90° angles and $V_c=a^3/2$.
Sheet 1 · Problem 2 · 4 pts

Packing fraction

Identical solid spheres sit on the points of each structure, with neighbours just touching. Give the coordination number (number of nearest neighbours) and the packing fraction (sphere volume / total volume) for (a) fcc, (b) hcp, (c) sc, (d) diamond.

Method

Find the nearest-neighbour distance $d$; touching means $r=d/2$. Then divide the volume of the atoms in one cell by the cell volume: $f = N_{\rm cell}\,\tfrac43\pi r^3 / V_{\rm cell}$. Any cell works (conventional or primitive) as long as $N_{\rm cell}$ and $V_{\rm cell}$ belong to the same cell.

Assumption: hcp has the ideal ratio $c/a=\sqrt{8/3}$, so spheres touch both within a layer and between layers (the sheet asks for a close-packed arrangement). Real hcp metals deviate (Mg 1.623, Zn 1.861; Kittel ch. 1).
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. (a) fcc. Nearest neighbours are at the face centres, $d=a/\sqrt2$; there are 12 (4 in each of the three cube planes through the origin). Spheres touch along the face diagonal: $4r=\sqrt2 a$, $r=a/(2\sqrt2)$. With 4 atoms per cube: $f = 4\cdot\tfrac43\pi\,\dfrac{a^3}{16\sqrt2}\Big/a^3 = \dfrac{\pi}{3\sqrt2}$.
  2. (b) hcp. 6 neighbours in the layer, 3 above, 3 below: CN = 12. Primitive cell: rhombic prism with base $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}a^2$, height $c$, 2 atoms, $r=a/2$. Touching between layers: the distance from an A atom to a B atom is $\sqrt{a^2/3+c^2/4}=a$, so $c=\sqrt{8/3}\,a$. Then $f=\dfrac{2\cdot\tfrac43\pi(a/2)^3}{\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}a^2\cdot\sqrt{8/3}\,a}=\dfrac{\pi}{3\sqrt2}$.
  3. (c) sc. 6 neighbours at $d=a$, $r=a/2$, 1 atom per cube: $f=\tfrac43\pi\tfrac{a^3}{8}/a^3=\pi/6$.
  4. (d) diamond. Each atom bonds to 4 atoms of the other fcc sublattice at ¼ of the body diagonal: $d=\sqrt3a/4$, $r=\sqrt3a/8$. Atoms per cube: $8\cdot\tfrac18+6\cdot\tfrac12+4=8$. $f = 8\cdot\tfrac43\pi\,\dfrac{3\sqrt3a^3}{512}\Big/a^3 = \dfrac{\sqrt3\,\pi}{16}$.
Result: (a) fcc: CN 12, $f=\pi/(3\sqrt2)=0.740$. (b) hcp: CN 12, $f=0.740$. (c) sc: CN 6, $f=\pi/6=0.524$. (d) diamond: CN 4, $f=\sqrt3\pi/16=0.340$.
Sanity check: the values match Kittel Table 2 (sc, fcc), ch. 1 p. 15 (hcp 0.74) and p. 17 (diamond 0.34, "46 percent" of fcc: 0.340/0.740 = 0.459 ✓). The order fcc = hcp > sc > diamond agrees with the sheet's statement. fcc via the primitive cell: $\tfrac43\pi r^3/(a^3/4)$ gives the same 0.740. Sheet 1 · Problem 3 · 6 pts

Symmetry operations

Give the 3×3 matrices for the x, y, z coordinate transformation of: (a) 180° rotation around the x-axis; (b) inversion; "(b)" 120° rotation around the z-axis; (d) reflection at the xz-plane; (e) 60° rotation around [111]; (f) reflection at the (110) plane (through the origin).

Method

Write the images of $\hat{\mathbf x},\hat{\mathbf y},\hat{\mathbf z}$ as the columns of $M$. For a rotation about an oblique axis use the Rodrigues formula (Concepts). For a reflection with unit normal $\hat{\mathbf n}$ use $M=\mathbb 1-2\hat{\mathbf n}\hat{\mathbf n}^T$. Each preset in Model 2 shows one answer.

Assumptions: (1) The sheet labels two parts "b)" and has no c); the 120° rotation is called (c) here. (2) Rotations are active and right-handed: the point $\mathbf r$ moves to $\mathbf r'=M\mathbf r$, turning anticlockwise when viewed from the tip of the axis. If "coordinate transformation" is read as rotating the axes instead (passive), each rotation matrix is replaced by its transpose. (a), (b), (d) and (f) are their own transposes, so they do not change.
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. (a) $\hat{\mathbf x}\to\hat{\mathbf x}$, $\hat{\mathbf y}\to-\hat{\mathbf y}$, $\hat{\mathbf z}\to-\hat{\mathbf z}$: $M=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&-1&0\\0&0&-1\end{pmatrix}$.
  2. (b) $\mathbf r\to-\mathbf r$: $M=-\mathbb 1=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\0&-1&0\\0&0&-1\end{pmatrix}$.
  3. (c) $\cos120^\circ=-\tfrac12$, $\sin120^\circ=\tfrac{\sqrt3}2$: $M=\begin{pmatrix}-\tfrac12&-\tfrac{\sqrt3}2&0\\\tfrac{\sqrt3}2&-\tfrac12&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$.
  4. (d) The xz-plane has normal $\hat{\mathbf y}$, so only $y$ flips: $M=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&-1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$. This also equals $-C_2(\hat{\mathbf y})$, the notes' "rotation then inversion".
  5. (e) $\hat{\mathbf n}=(1,1,1)/\sqrt3$, $\varphi=60^\circ$: $\cos\varphi\,\mathbb 1$ gives ½ on the diagonal, $(1-\cos\varphi)\hat{\mathbf n}\hat{\mathbf n}^T$ adds $\tfrac16$ to every entry, and $\sin\varphi[\hat{\mathbf n}]_\times$ adds $\pm\tfrac12$ off the diagonal: $$M=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&2\\2&2&-1\\-1&2&2\end{pmatrix}.$$
  6. (f) The (110) plane has normal $\hat{\mathbf n}=(1,1,0)/\sqrt2$, so $\hat{\mathbf n}\hat{\mathbf n}^T$ has ½ in the four top-left entries: $M=\mathbb 1-2\hat{\mathbf n}\hat{\mathbf n}^T=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\-1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$, i.e. $(x,y,z)\to(-y,-x,z)$.
Result: the six matrices above. Determinants: +1 for the rotations (a), (c), (e); −1 for inversion and the reflections (b), (d), (f).
Sanity check: every $M$ is orthogonal (columns are unit length and mutually perpendicular). For (e), $M^2$ is the cyclic permutation $x\to y\to z\to x$ (the 120° rotation about [111]), and $M^3 = 2\hat{\mathbf n}\hat{\mathbf n}^T-\mathbb 1$ (the 180° rotation); both were checked numerically in node. (e) leaves $(1,1,1)$ fixed and has trace $2=1+2\cos60^\circ$ ✓. (f) equals $-C_2([110])$ ✓. Note: (e) is not a symmetry of a cubic lattice, because [111] is only a 3-fold axis there. It is a matrix exercise; Model 2 shows the cube failing to map onto itself.

FCC lattice

(a) General properties of the fcc lattice. (b) Sketch and calculate the primitive basis vectors. (c) Coordination number. (d) Packing fraction.

This is sheet 1.2a plus the fcc primitive vectors (1.1.2, 1.1.5).

Show model answer
  1. (a) A cubic Bravais lattice: points at the cube corners and face centres, 4 per conventional cube ($8\cdot\tfrac18+6\cdot\tfrac12$). It has full cubic symmetry: three 4-fold axes ⟨100⟩, four 3-fold ⟨111⟩, six 2-fold ⟨110⟩, mirror planes and inversion. It is close-packed: the (111) planes are triangular layers stacked ABCABC…, and neighbours touch along ⟨110⟩. Its Wigner–Seitz cell is a rhombic dodecahedron; its reciprocal lattice is bcc. Examples: Cu, Al, Ag, Au, Ni.
  2. (b) Sketch the cube with the origin at a corner and draw three arrows to the three adjacent face centres: $\mathbf a_1=\tfrac a2(1,1,0)$, $\mathbf a_2=\tfrac a2(0,1,1)$, $\mathbf a_3=\tfrac a2(1,0,1)$, each of length $a/\sqrt2$ and 60° apart. They span a rhombohedron with $V_c=\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)=a^3/4$, so one point per primitive cell (Model 1, fcc).
  3. (c) 12 nearest neighbours at $a/\sqrt2$, the maximum for equal spheres (6 second neighbours at $a$).
  4. Exam-relevant derivation (d) Touching along the face diagonal: $4r=\sqrt2 a$; $f=4\cdot\tfrac43\pi r^3/a^3=\pi/(3\sqrt2)\approx0.74$.

Copper

Copper is fcc with $a = 3.61$ Å (Kittel Table 3). Find (i) the primitive cell volume, (ii) the nearest-neighbour distance and coordination number, (iii) the radius of touching spheres and the packing fraction, (iv) the number of atoms per m³.

Show worked solution
  1. $V_c=a^3/4 = (3.61\times10^{-10}\,\text{m})^3/4 = 1.18\times10^{-29}$ m³.
  2. $d=a/\sqrt2 = 2.55$ Å; CN = 12.
  3. $r=d/2=1.28$ Å; $f=0.740$, independent of $a$.
  4. $n = 4/a^3 = 1/V_c = 8.50\times10^{28}$ m⁻³.
