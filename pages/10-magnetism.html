Overview

An electron of charge $-e$ in a field $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$ has the Hamiltonian $H_e=(\mathbf p+e\mathbf A)^2/2m-\mathbf m_{\text{spin}}\cdot\mathbf B$. For a uniform field, $\mathbf A=-\tfrac12\,\mathbf r\times\mathbf B$, and squaring out (carefully, since $\mathbf p$ and $\mathbf A$ are operators) splits the field-dependent part into three terms: $\mu_B\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf L/\hbar$ and $\mu_Bg_e\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf S/\hbar$, linear in $B$ and paramagnetic, and $(e^2/8m)B^2r_\perp^2$, quadratic and diamagnetic. The energy levels $E_n(B)$ then give the moment of each state, $M_n=-\frac1V\partial E_n/\partial B$, and the thermal average follows from the free energy: $\langle M\rangle=-\frac1V\partial F/\partial B$ and $\chi_m=-\frac{\mu_0}{V}\partial^2F/\partial B^2$.

Key results to leave with: the boxed Hamiltonian, the three-term $\Delta H_e$ with its signs, why the linear terms dominate whenever they are not zero (about $10^6$ times larger for an atom at 1 T), and the chain $E_n(B)\to F\to M,\,S,\,\chi_m$. The lecture stopped there; Curie, Langevin, Pauli and Landau come next and are previewed at the end. This page uses the free-electron Hamiltonian from topic 07 and the crystal quantum numbers from topic 09.

2.8 · Magnetic response

Magnetization and susceptibility

A field $\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf H$ induces a magnetization $\mathbf M$, the magnetic moment per volume (A/m). For weak fields the response is linear:

Magnetic susceptibility
$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf M&=\chi_m\mathbf H\\ \chi_m&=\frac{\partial M}{\partial H}\Big|_{H=0}\end{aligned}$$ (1)

In SI, $\chi_m$ is dimensionless. $\chi_m>0$ is paramagnetism (M along B, the sample is pulled into a strong field); $\chi_m<0$ is diamagnetism (M against B, pushed out). Both are tiny for most materials, so $B\approx\mu_0H$ inside the sample too.

Magnetism needs quantum mechanics. In classical statistical mechanics a system in thermal equilibrium has no magnetization at all (Bohr–van Leeuwen theorem): the field enters only through $\mathbf p+e\mathbf A$, and shifting the momentum integration variable in the classical partition function removes $\mathbf A$, so $F$ does not depend on $B$ and $M=0$.

The lecture names three effects in an atom:

  • the orbital angular momentum $\mathbf L$ carries a moment: paramagnetic;
  • the spin $\mathbf S$ carries a moment: paramagnetic;
  • the field induces orbital currents that oppose it (Lenz): diamagnetic, present in every material.

All three drop out of one Hamiltonian in 2.8.1.

Quoting a molar or CGS susceptibility as if it were the SI volume $\chi_m$. Tables often give $\chi_{\text{mol}}$ in cm³/mol (CGS); the SI molar value is $4\pi\times10^{-6}$ times it in m³/mol, and the volume $\chi_m$ is $\chi_{\text{mol}}$ times moles per m³.

A sample is pushed out of the strong-field region of a magnet. What is the sign of χ_m, and which of the three effects must dominate?

$\chi_m<0$, diamagnetic. The induced-current effect dominates, which happens when the atoms carry no net $\mathbf L$ or $\mathbf S$ (closed shells, e.g. He, Cu⁺ cores, water).

$\mathbf M=\chi_m\mathbf H$: positive χ from the moments of L and S, negative χ from induced currents, and none of it exists classically.

You can now say what χ_m measures and what its sign means, and name the three sources. To compute them, you need the Hamiltonian, 2.8.1 →.

2.8.1 · Minimal coupling

The Hamiltonian in a field

2.8 · Magnetic response; ← from 2.1.2 Plane waves

Recap from 2.1.2 Plane waves. A free electron has $H_{e,0}=\mathbf p^2/2m$ with $\mathbf p=-i\hbar\nabla$.

Write the field through a vector potential, $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$. For a particle of charge $q$ the momentum operator becomes $\mathbf p-q\mathbf A$; for the electron $q=-e$, so $\mathbf p+e\mathbf A$. The spin moment couples to $\mathbf B$ directly, as a dipole does:

Hamilton operator for an electron in a B-field
$$\begin{aligned}H_e=\;&\frac{\big(\mathbf p+e\mathbf A(\mathbf r)\big)^2}{2m}\\&-\mathbf m_{\text{spin}}\cdot\mathbf B(\mathbf r)\end{aligned}$$ (2)

A periodic crystal potential or the nuclear Coulomb potential just adds to (2); it does not change the field terms. For a uniform field the lecture uses

Vector potential of a uniform field
$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf A&=-\tfrac12\,\mathbf r\times\mathbf B\\ \nabla\times\mathbf A&=\mathbf B,\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0\end{aligned}$$ (3)

For $\mathbf B=B\hat z$ this is $\mathbf A=\tfrac B2(-y,\,x,\,0)$: circles around the z axis. $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$ (Coulomb gauge) is what makes the expansion in the next card simple.

Writing $\mathbf p-e\mathbf A$. That is the rule for charge $+e$; with the lecture's electron charge $-e$ it is $\mathbf p+e\mathbf A$. The sign decides the sign of the orbital Zeeman term.

Check ∇×A = B for A = (B/2)(−y, x, 0).

$(\nabla\times\mathbf A)_z=\partial_xA_y-\partial_yA_x=\tfrac B2+\tfrac B2=B$, and the x and y components vanish because nothing depends on z. $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=\partial_x(-\tfrac B2y)+\partial_y(\tfrac B2x)=0$.

The field enters twice: through $\mathbf p+e\mathbf A$ (orbital motion) and through $-\mathbf m_{\text{spin}}\cdot\mathbf B$ (spin).

2.8.1 · Careful squaring

Expanding (p + eA)² with operators

2.8.1 · Minimal coupling

Exam-relevant derivation

$\mathbf p$ and $\mathbf A(\mathbf r)$ are operators that need not commute, so the square has two cross terms in a fixed order:

Square, keeping the order
$$\begin{aligned}&(\mathbf p+e\mathbf A)^2=\mathbf p^2\\&\quad+e(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf A+\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf p)\\&\quad+e^2\mathbf A^2\end{aligned}$$ (4)

Step 1: the order does not matter here. Acting on a wavefunction, $\mathbf p\cdot(\mathbf A\psi)=-i\hbar(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A)\psi+\mathbf A\cdot(\mathbf p\psi)$. In the Coulomb gauge $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$, so $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf A=\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf p$.

Step 2: insert A = −½ r × B. This gives the lecture's line

After inserting the uniform-field A
$$\begin{aligned}H_e=\;&\frac{\mathbf p^2}{2m}\\&-\frac{1}{2m}\frac e2\big[\mathbf p\cdot(\mathbf r\times\mathbf B)\\&\qquad+(\mathbf r\times\mathbf B)\cdot\mathbf p\big]\\&+\frac{1}{2m}\frac{e^2}{4}(\mathbf r\times\mathbf B)^2\\&-\mathbf m_{\text{spin}}\cdot\mathbf B\end{aligned}$$ (5)

Step 3: the cross term is the angular momentum. Cycling the triple product with $\mathbf B$ (a constant) moved out, $(\mathbf r\times\mathbf B)\cdot\mathbf p=-\mathbf B\cdot(\mathbf r\times\mathbf p)=-\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf L$; $\mathbf r$ stays to the left of $\mathbf p$ throughout, so no ordering is changed. With step 1 both orderings are equal:

Cross term and quadratic term
$$\begin{aligned}&\mathbf p\cdot(\mathbf r\times\mathbf B)+(\mathbf r\times\mathbf B)\cdot\mathbf p\\&\quad=-2\,\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf L\\ &(\mathbf r\times\mathbf B)^2=B^2r_\perp^2\end{aligned}$$ (6)

Here $\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times\mathbf p$ and $r_\perp$ is the distance from the axis through the origin along $\mathbf B$ ($|\mathbf r\times\mathbf B|=rB\sin\theta$). So the linear term is $+\frac{e}{2m}\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf L=\mu_B\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf L/\hbar$.

Step 4: the spin moment. $\mathbf m_{\text{spin}}=-\mu_Bg_e\mathbf S/\hbar$, antiparallel to the spin because the charge is negative, with

Bohr magneton and electron g-factor
$$\begin{aligned}\mu_B&=\frac{e\hbar}{2m}\\&=9.274\times10^{-24}\ \text{J/T}\\&=57.88\ \mu\text{eV/T}\\ g_e&\approx2\ (2.0023)\end{aligned}$$ (7)

Expanding $(\mathbf p+e\mathbf A)^2$ as $\mathbf p^2+2e\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf p+e^2A^2$ without saying why. It is only right because $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$; in another gauge an extra $-i\hbar e\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf A$ appears. Say it, because "careful squaring" is what the lecture stressed.

Why is μ_B·B·L/ħ positive for L along B, even though a current loop's energy is −m·B?

The electron's charge is negative, so its orbital moment is $\mathbf m_L=-\mu_B\mathbf L/\hbar$, opposite to $\mathbf L$. Then $-\mathbf m_L\cdot\mathbf B=+\mu_B\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf L/\hbar$: consistent with $U=-\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf B$.

Coulomb gauge makes $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf A=\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf p$; the cross term becomes $\mu_B\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf L/\hbar$ and $\mathbf A^2$ becomes $\tfrac14B^2r_\perp^2$.

2.8.1 · Three terms

Paramagnetic and diamagnetic terms

2.8.1 · Careful squaring

Practice 1

Collecting (5)–(7): $H_e=H_{e,0}+\Delta H_e$, with the whole field dependence in

B-dependent part of the Hamiltonian
$$\begin{aligned}\Delta H_e=\;&\mu_B\frac{\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf L}{\hbar}+\frac{e^2}{8m}B^2r_\perp^2\\&+\mu_Bg_e\frac{\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf S}{\hbar}\end{aligned}$$ (8)

For $\mathbf B=B\hat z$:

Field along zPractice 1
$$\begin{aligned}\Delta H_e=\;&\mu_BB\,\frac{L_z+g_eS_z}{\hbar}\\&+\frac{e^2}{8m}B^2(x^2+y^2)\end{aligned}$$ (9)
  • First term, linear in B: paramagnetism. In a state with $L_z=m_l\hbar$, $S_z=m_s\hbar$ it shifts the level by $\mu_BB(m_l+g_em_s)$. States with $m_l+g_em_s<0$ go down, so in equilibrium they fill first and the moment lines up with B.
  • Second term, quadratic in B: diamagnetism. Always positive, $\frac{e^2B^2}{8m}\langle x^2+y^2\rangle$; the energy rises with B, so the induced moment points against it. Every electron has it.

Sizes at 1 T. $\mu_BB=57.9$ μeV. For a hydrogen 1s electron, $\langle x^2+y^2\rangle=\tfrac23\langle r^2\rangle=2a_0^2$, and the diamagnetic term is $1.2\times10^{-10}$ eV, about $2\times10^{-6}$ of the linear one. The two would be equal only near $B^*=4\hbar|m_l+g_em_s|/(e\langle r_\perp^2\rangle)\approx5\times10^5$ T. So the diamagnetic term matters only when the linear term averages to zero: closed shells ($\sum m_l=\sum m_s=0$), as in He, Ne or ion cores. Model 1 shows the two terms side by side.

Concluding that diamagnetism is negligible. It is small, but in closed-shell atoms and ions it is the only response there is, so it decides the sign of χ for most solids that are not magnetic.

For m_l = −1, m_s = +½, what is the linear shift with g_e = 2? With g_e = 2.0023?

$m_l+g_em_s=-1+1=0$ with $g_e=2$: the linear term cancels and only the diamagnetic shift remains. With 2.0023 it is $0.00116\,\mu_BB$, about 67 neV at 1 T. Try it in Model 1.

$\Delta H_e$: $\mu_BB(L_z+g_eS_z)/\hbar$ (paramagnetic, ∝ B) plus $\frac{e^2}{8m}B^2r_\perp^2$ (diamagnetic, ∝ B², always raises the energy).

2.8.1 · Thermodynamics of magnetization

From energy levels to the moment of a state

2.8.1 · Three terms; ← from 2.6.1 Bloch theorem

Solving the Schrödinger equation with (8) tells you how each level moves with the field: $E_n(B)$. The label $n$ is the full set of quantum numbers:

  • atoms: $n=(n,l,m_l,m_s)$;
  • crystals: $n=(\mathbf k,n,m_s)$, the Bloch wavevector and band index of topic 09 plus spin.

A magnetic dipole in a field has energy $U=-\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf B$ (a current loop has $\mathbf m=I\mathbf A$, with $\mathbf A=\hat{\mathbf n}A$ the area vector). Turned around: if the level moves by $dE_n$ when the field changes by $d\mathbf B$, the state carries the moment $-\partial E_n/\partial\mathbf B$. Per volume, $dE_n=-V\mathbf M_n\cdot d\mathbf B$, and with $\mathbf M\parallel\mathbf B$:

Magnetization of state nPractice 3
$$\mathbf M_n=-\frac1V\frac{\partial E_n}{\partial B}\,\hat{\mathbf B}$$ (10)

Applied to (9): the linear term gives the $B$-independent moment $-\mu_B(m_l+g_em_s)/V$ (a permanent moment), the quadratic term an induced moment $-\frac{e^2B}{4mV}\langle r_\perp^2\rangle$, proportional to B and opposite to it.

What moment does the m_s = −½, m_l = 0 state carry (g_e = 2)?

$E=-\mu_BB$ from the spin term, so $-\partial E/\partial B=+\mu_B$: one Bohr magneton along $\mathbf B$, as expected for a spin pointing against B with a negative charge.

A state's moment is the slope of its level: $M_n=-\frac1V\partial E_n/\partial B$.

2.8.1 · Free energy

Thermal average: M, S and χ from F

2.8.1 · From energy levels to the moment

Exam-relevant derivationPractice 2

At temperature $T$ each state is occupied with Boltzmann weight $e^{-\beta E_n}/Z$, with $\beta=1/k_BT$ and partition function $Z=\sum_ne^{-\beta E_n}$. Average (10):

Thermal average of the magnetization
$$\begin{aligned}\langle M\rangle&=\frac1Z\sum_nM_ne^{-\beta E_n}\\&=-\frac{1}{VZ}\sum_n\frac{\partial E_n}{\partial B}e^{-\beta E_n}\\&=-\frac1V\frac{\partial}{\partial B}\Big[-\frac1\beta\ln Z\Big]\end{aligned}$$ (11)

The bracket is the Helmholtz free energy, $F=-k_BT\ln Z$. (Differentiate $\ln Z$ to check: the chain rule brings down $-\beta\,\partial E_n/\partial B$.)

Magnetization
$$\langle\mathbf M\rangle=-\frac1V\frac{\partial F}{\partial B}\,\hat{\mathbf B}$$ (12)

The same result from thermodynamics: the work done on the sample is $-V\mathbf M\cdot d\mathbf B$, so $dU=T\,dS-V\mathbf M\cdot d\mathbf B$, and with $F=U-TS$, $dF=-S\,dT-V\mathbf M\cdot d\mathbf B$. Read off the partial derivatives, and use $M=\chi_mH=\chi_mB/\mu_0$ for the last one:

Entropy, magnetization, susceptibilityPractice 2
$$\begin{aligned}S&=-\Big(\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}\Big)_B\\ M&=-\frac1V\Big(\frac{\partial F}{\partial B}\Big)_T\\ \chi_m&=-\frac{\mu_0}{V}\Big(\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial B^2}\Big)_{T,\,B\to0}\end{aligned}$$ (13)

Example: one spin ½ per site, no orbital moment, $N$ spins in $V$, density $n=N/V$. With $g_e=2$ the levels are $E_\pm=\pm\mu_BB$, so $Z=(2\cosh\beta\mu_BB)^N$ and $F=-Nk_BT\ln(2\cosh\beta\mu_BB)$. Then (13) gives $M=n\mu_B\tanh(\mu_BB/k_BT)$ and $\chi_m=\mu_0n\mu_B^2/k_BT$. Model 2 does exactly these derivatives numerically.

Differentiating $Z$ instead of $F$, or forgetting the factor $1/V$. $M$ is a moment per volume; $-\partial F/\partial B$ alone is the total moment of the sample, in J/T.

At T → 0, what does (12) reduce to?

Only the ground state survives in $Z$, so $F\to E_0(B)$ and $\langle M\rangle\to M_0=-\frac1V\partial E_0/\partial B$: the moment of the ground state, eq. (10).

Used in 2.8 · Going further → (Curie and Langevin are this formula applied to two cases)

Levels $E_n(B)$ → $Z$ → $F=-k_BT\ln Z$; then $S=-\partial F/\partial T$, $M=-\frac1V\partial F/\partial B$, $\chi_m=-\frac{\mu_0}V\partial^2F/\partial B^2$.

You can now write down the Hamiltonian of an electron in a field, expand it with the operator ordering justified, name each term's effect and sign, and get M, S and χ from a given set of levels: practice P1–P3 and the exam-style exercise.

2.8 · Beyond the lecture

Curie, Langevin, Pauli, Landau

2.8.1 · Free energy; ← from 2.1.3 Density of states

Not yet lectured

Curie law (free spins). The spin-½ example of 2.8.1 already is it: $\chi_m=\mu_0n\mu_B^2/k_BT$. The general form for moments with angular momentum $J$ is $\chi_m=\mu_0np^2\mu_B^2/3k_BT$ with $p^2=g^2J(J+1)$; spin ½ with $g=2$ has $p^2=3$ and gives the same result.

Curie law
$$\chi_m=\frac{C}{T},\qquad C=\frac{\mu_0n\,p^2\mu_B^2}{3k_B}$$ (14)

Langevin diamagnetism (closed shells). Only the quadratic term of (9) survives. At $T\to0$, $F=E_0(B)$ and first-order perturbation theory gives $E_0(B)=E_0+\frac{e^2B^2}{8m}\sum_i\langle x_i^2+y_i^2\rangle$. For a spherical atom $\langle x^2+y^2\rangle=\tfrac23\langle r^2\rangle$; then (13) gives, for $n$ atoms per volume with $Z$ electrons each,

Langevin diamagnetism
$$\chi_m=-\frac{\mu_0nZe^2\langle r^2\rangle}{6m}$$ (15)

Pauli paramagnetism (conduction electrons). Fermi–Dirac statistics stops most electrons from flipping their spin: only those within about $\mu_BB$ of $\varepsilon_F$ can. The result depends on the density of states at the Fermi level, so it is small and almost independent of T.

Pauli and Landau
$$\begin{aligned}\chi_P&=\frac{\mu_0\mu_B^2D(\varepsilon_F)}{V}=\frac{3n\mu_0\mu_B^2}{2\varepsilon_F}\\ \chi_L&=-\tfrac13\chi_P\end{aligned}$$ (16)

$\chi_L$ is Landau diamagnetism: the orbital motion of free electrons, quantized into Landau levels spaced by $\hbar\omega_c=\hbar eB/m$ (0.116 meV at 1 T). For Na ($n=2.65\times10^{28}$ m⁻³, $\varepsilon_F=3.24$ eV) the free-electron values are $\chi_P=8.3\times10^{-6}$ and $\chi_L=-2.8\times10^{-6}$.

Why is the Pauli susceptibility of a metal far below the Curie value n μ_B²μ₀/k_BT at room temperature?

Pauli blocking. Curie assumes every spin is free to flip; in a degenerate gas only a fraction of order $k_BT/\varepsilon_F$ near the Fermi level can, so χ is smaller by about $T/T_F$, roughly 1/100 at 300 K. The same shell argument gave the small electronic heat capacity on topic 07.

Free spins: χ ∝ 1/T. Closed shells: χ < 0, set by ⟨r²⟩. Conduction electrons: small, T-independent Pauli χ minus a third for Landau.

You can now estimate the sign and order of magnitude of χ for an insulator with closed shells, a salt with free spins, and a simple metal, ahead of the lecture.

Practice 1 · not from a sheet

Paramagnetic vs diamagnetic shift of an atom at 1 T

A hydrogen atom in its 1s state sits in a field $B=1$ T along z. (a) Using eq. (9), compute the linear (spin) shift for $m_s=\pm\tfrac12$ and the diamagnetic shift, with $\langle r^2\rangle=3a_0^2$ for 1s. (b) What is their ratio? (c) At what field would they be equal? (d) Compare both with $k_BT$ at 300 K.

Method

Evaluate the two terms of (9) in the state: $L_z=0$, $S_z=m_s\hbar$, and $\langle x^2+y^2\rangle=\tfrac23\langle r^2\rangle$ for a spherical state.

Assumption: $\langle r^2\rangle_{1s}=3a_0^2$ is the textbook hydrogen value; first-order perturbation theory (expectation value of ΔH in the unperturbed state).
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. Linear: $\Delta E=g_e\mu_BBm_s=\pm\mu_BB=\pm9.27\times10^{-24}$ J $=\pm57.9$ μeV (with $g_e=2$).
  2. Diamagnetic: $\langle x^2+y^2\rangle=2a_0^2=5.60\times10^{-21}$ m². $\Delta E=\dfrac{e^2B^2}{8m}2a_0^2=\dfrac{(1.602\times10^{-19})^2\cdot5.60\times10^{-21}}{8\cdot9.109\times10^{-31}}=1.97\times10^{-29}$ J $=1.23\times10^{-10}$ eV.
  3. Ratio: $1.23\times10^{-10}/5.79\times10^{-5}=2.1\times10^{-6}$.
  4. Equal when $\mu_BB=\frac{e^2B^2}{8m}2a_0^2$, i.e. $B^*=\dfrac{4\hbar}{e\cdot2a_0^2}=4.7\times10^5$ T.
  5. $k_BT(300\text{ K})=25.9$ meV: the spin splitting $2\mu_BB$ is 0.45 % of it, the diamagnetic shift $5\times10^{-9}$ of it.
Result: ±57.9 μeV (spin) against +1.2×10⁻¹⁰ eV (diamagnetic), ratio 2×10⁻⁶; equal only at about 5×10⁵ T. Both far below $k_BT$ at room temperature.
Sanity check: units of $\hbar/(ea_0^2)$: J s/(C m²) = V s/m² = T. Recomputed in node with CODATA constants; Model 1 at m_l = 0, ⟨r⊥²⟩ = 2 a₀² reads the same B*.
Practice 2 · not from a sheet

Susceptibility of a two-level spin system from F

$N$ independent spins ½ (no orbital moment, $g_e=2$) in volume $V$ sit in a field $B$ along z at temperature $T$. (a) Give the levels and $Z$. (b) Find $F$, then $M$ from eq. (13). (c) Find $\chi_m$ and $S$. (d) Evaluate $M$ and $\chi_m$ for $n=5.51\times10^{27}$ m⁻³ at 1 T and 1 K, and $\chi_m$ at 300 K.

Method

Follow the lecture's chain: levels → $Z$ → $F=-k_BT\ln Z$ → derivatives. The spins are independent, so $Z=z^N$ with a single-spin $z$.

Assumption: $n$ is the Cu²⁺ density of CuSO₄·5H₂O from its density 2.286 g/cm³ and molar mass 249.7 g/mol (standard handbook values), treated as pure spin ½.
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. Levels: $E_\pm=\mu_Bg_eBm_s=\pm\mu_BB$. One spin: $z=e^{\beta\mu_BB}+e^{-\beta\mu_BB}=2\cosh x$, $x=\mu_BB/k_BT$. $Z=z^N$.
  2. $F=-Nk_BT\ln(2\cosh x)$. $M=-\frac1V\frac{\partial F}{\partial B}=\frac NV k_BT\tanh x\cdot\frac{\mu_B}{k_BT}=n\mu_B\tanh x$.
  3. $\chi_m=\mu_0\frac{\partial M}{\partial B}\Big|_{B\to0}=\mu_0n\mu_B\cdot\frac{\mu_B}{k_BT}=\dfrac{\mu_0n\mu_B^2}{k_BT}$, the Curie law. $S=-\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}=Nk_B[\ln(2\cosh x)-x\tanh x]$, which is $Nk_B\ln2$ at $B=0$.
  4. At 1 T, 1 K: $x=0.672$, $\tanh x=0.587$, $M=0.587\cdot n\mu_B=3.0\times10^4$ A/m. $\chi_m(1\text{ K})=0.043$; $\chi_m(300\text{ K})=1.4\times10^{-4}$.
Result: $M=n\mu_B\tanh(\mu_BB/k_BT)$, $\chi_m=\mu_0n\mu_B^2/k_BT$. For the given n: $M=3.0\times10^4$ A/m at 1 T and 1 K; $\chi_m=0.043$ at 1 K and $1.4\times10^{-4}$ at 300 K.
Sanity check: limits: $x\to\infty$ gives saturation $n\mu_B$, $x\to0$ the linear law. The general Curie constant $\mu_0np^2\mu_B^2/3k_B$ with $p^2=g^2S(S+1)=3$ gives the same χ. Model 2 computes the derivatives of F numerically and agrees with these closed forms (node, CODATA constants).
Practice 3 · not from a sheet

Diamagnetism of helium from the ground-state energy

Helium has a closed 1s² shell ($L=S=0$). (a) Using eq. (9), write the ground-state energy $E_0(B)$ to order $B^2$. (b) Use $F\to E_0$ at $T\to0$ and eq. (13) to get $\chi_m$ for $n$ atoms per volume. (c) The measured molar susceptibility is $-1.9\times10^{-6}$ cm³/mol (CGS). Convert to SI and find $\langle r^2\rangle$ per electron, in units of $a_0^2$. (d) Compare with a hydrogen-like 1s orbital with effective nuclear charge $Z_{\text{eff}}=1.69$, for which $\langle r^2\rangle=3a_0^2/Z_{\text{eff}}^2$.

Method

The linear terms vanish ($\sum m_l=\sum m_s=0$), so only the diamagnetic term shifts the level. Then $M_0=-\frac1V\partial E_0/\partial B$ per atom and $\chi_m=-\frac{\mu_0}{V}\partial^2F/\partial B^2$.

Data and assumptions: measured value from Kittel ch. 11 ("Langevin Diamagnetism Equation"); SI molar χ = 4π×10⁻⁶ × CGS value; $Z_{\text{eff}}=27/16\approx1.69$ is the standard variational value for He. Spherical atom, so $\langle x^2+y^2\rangle=\tfrac23\langle r^2\rangle$.
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. $E_0(B)=E_0(0)+\dfrac{e^2B^2}{8m}\cdot2\cdot\tfrac23\langle r^2\rangle=E_0(0)+\dfrac{e^2B^2}{6m}\langle r^2\rangle$ (two electrons).
  2. Per volume, $F=nV\,E_0(B)$, so $\chi_m=-\mu_0n\dfrac{\partial^2E_0}{\partial B^2}=-\dfrac{\mu_0n\cdot2e^2\langle r^2\rangle}{6m}$: Langevin, eq. (15), with $Z=2$.
  3. SI molar: $\chi_{\text{mol}}=4\pi\times10^{-6}\cdot(-1.9\times10^{-6})=-2.39\times10^{-11}$ m³/mol $=-\mu_0N_A\,2e^2\langle r^2\rangle/6m$.
  4. $\mu_0N_Ae^2/6m=3.55\times10^{9}$ m/mol, so $2\langle r^2\rangle=2.39\times10^{-11}/3.55\times10^{9}=6.72\times10^{-21}$ m² $=2.40\,a_0^2$, i.e. $\langle r^2\rangle=1.20\,a_0^2$ per electron.
  5. Hydrogen-like with $Z_{\text{eff}}=1.69$: $3/1.69^2=1.05\,a_0^2$, about 12 % below. Electron repulsion makes the real orbital slightly larger.
Result: $\chi_m=-\mu_0n\,Ze^2\langle r^2\rangle/6m$; the measured He value implies $\langle r^2\rangle\approx1.2\,a_0^2$ per electron, close to the screened-hydrogen estimate 1.05 $a_0^2$. For He gas at 0 °C and 1 atm ($n=2.69\times10^{25}$ m⁻³) this is $\chi_m\approx-1.1\times10^{-9}$.
Sanity check: sign negative, as for any closed shell. Units of $\mu_0N_Ae^2/m$: (T m/A)(1/mol)(C²)/kg = m/mol, times m² gives m³/mol. Numbers recomputed in node; the inferred $\langle r^2\rangle$ is about 40 % of hydrogen's 3 a₀², as expected for a nuclear charge near 2.

Exam-style exercise · 2022 pattern

Electron in a uniform magnetic field

a) Write the Hamiltonian of an electron (charge −e, mass m, spin S) in a magnetic field $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$. b) Show that $\mathbf A=-\frac12\mathbf r\times\mathbf B$ describes a uniform field, and expand the Hamiltonian for this $\mathbf A$. State where the operator ordering matters. c) Name the effect of each field-dependent term and its sign. d) A system has levels $E_n(B)$. Derive the thermal magnetization in terms of the free energy, and give $\chi_m$.

Show model answer
  1. a) $H_e=\dfrac{(\mathbf p+e\mathbf A)^2}{2m}-\mathbf m_{\text{spin}}\cdot\mathbf B$, with $\mathbf m_{\text{spin}}=-g_e\mu_B\mathbf S/\hbar$, $\mu_B=e\hbar/2m$, $g_e\approx2$.
  2. b) For $\mathbf B=B\hat z$, $\mathbf A=\frac B2(-y,x,0)$: $\nabla\times\mathbf A=B\hat z$ and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$. Expand $(\mathbf p+e\mathbf A)^2=\mathbf p^2+e(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf A+\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf p)+e^2A^2$; the order matters in general, but $\mathbf p\cdot(\mathbf A\psi)=\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf p\psi-i\hbar(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A)\psi$ and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$, so both cross terms are equal. $(\mathbf r\times\mathbf B)\cdot\mathbf p=-\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf L$ gives the cross term $\frac{e}{2m}\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf L$; $A^2=\frac14B^2(x^2+y^2)$. Result: $H_e=\frac{\mathbf p^2}{2m}+\mu_B\frac{\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf L}{\hbar}$ $+\frac{e^2}{8m}B^2(x^2+y^2)$ $+g_e\mu_B\frac{\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf S}{\hbar}$.
  3. c) $\mu_B\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf L/\hbar$ and $g_e\mu_B\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf S/\hbar$: linear in B, permanent orbital and spin moments, paramagnetism ($\chi>0$; the levels with $m_l+g_em_s<0$ go down and fill first). $\frac{e^2}{8m}B^2r_\perp^2$: quadratic, always raises the energy, induced moment against B, diamagnetism ($\chi<0$), present in every atom and dominant only for closed shells.
  4. d) State $n$ carries $M_n=-\frac1V\partial E_n/\partial B$. Boltzmann average: $\langle M\rangle=\sum_nM_ne^{-\beta E_n}/Z=\frac1V\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial B}=-\frac1V\frac{\partial F}{\partial B}$, with $F=-k_BT\ln Z$. Since $M=\chi_mB/\mu_0$, $\chi_m=-\frac{\mu_0}{V}\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial B^2}\big|_{B\to0}$.
Points lost in practice: the sign of eA for a negative charge, expanding the square as if p and A were numbers without mentioning ∇·A = 0, a missing 1/V, and swapping which term is para- and which is diamagnetic.
