Overview

Electrons in a periodic potential: why allowed energies form bands separated by gaps, and how the filling of the highest band decides whether a solid is a metal, semiconductor or insulator. Bloch's theorem (2.6.1) says every eigenstate is a plane wave times a lattice-periodic function, labelled by a wave vector k in the first Brillouin zone and a band index n. Two limits make the bands computable. In the nearly-free-electron model (2.6.2), free parabolas are folded into the zone and a weak potential opens gaps of 2|UG| at the zone faces. In tight binding (2.6.3), atomic orbitals overlap and spread into cosine bands.

Key results to leave with: the two forms of Bloch's theorem; the empty-lattice energies $\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}|\mathbf k-\mathbf G|^2$ along [100] with their degeneracies; the central equation and the gap $E_g=2|U_{\mathbf G}|$; and why the cos and sin standing waves differ in energy. Together these answer exam 2022 Q4. Lectures have reached 2.6.2. Tight binding has not been lectured yet, so it is taught here from Kittel.

Read alongside Kittel, Introduction to Solid State Physics 8th ed., ch. 7 (Energy Bands) and ch. 9 (Fermi Surfaces and Metals), and the lecture notes pp. 50–54.

Concepts

2.6.1Bloch theorem

What this covers. Why a periodic potential forces every eigenstate into the form plane wave × cell-periodic function, the two equivalent statements, a proof sketch, and what follows for k, ψ and bands. Lecture notes p50–53.

Why it matters. Exam Q4a asks for the theorem and its consequences directly, and Practice A drills it. Its labels, k in the first Brillouin zone and the band index n, are used by 2.6.2, 2.6.3, Fermi surfaces and semiconductors (3.1).

2.6.1 · Periodic potential

Electrons in a periodic potential

The ions sit on a lattice, so the potential energy they create repeats: $U(\mathbf r+\mathbf R)=U(\mathbf r)$ for every lattice vector $\mathbf R$. One electron in this potential obeys

One-electron Schrödinger equation
$$\Big[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2+U(\mathbf r)\Big]\psi(\mathbf r)=\varepsilon\,\psi(\mathbf r).$$ (1)

Electrons that obey this equation are called Bloch electrons ($m$ = electron mass, $\varepsilon$ in J or eV). Two opposite limits make it solvable. In one, $U$ is weak (nearly free electrons, 2.6.2). In the other, deep atomic wells overlap only slightly (tight binding, 2.6.3). Bloch's theorem (2.6.1) holds in both.

Which two limits make (1) solvable, and what is small in each?

Nearly free electrons: $U$ is small compared with the kinetic energy. Tight binding: the overlap between orbitals on neighbouring atoms is small.

The whole chapter is equation (1) with a periodic $U$. The rest is what periodicity forces on its solutions, plus two limits where it can be solved.

2.6.1 · Statement and proof

Bloch's theorem

2.6.1 · Periodic potential

Every eigenstate can be chosen in the form

Bloch's theoremExam Q4a
$$\psi_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)=u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)\,e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r},\qquad u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r+\mathbf R)=u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r),$$ (2)

or, equivalently (the form boxed in the notes),

Equivalent formExam Q4aPractice A
$$\psi(\mathbf r+\mathbf R)=e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}\,\psi(\mathbf r).$$ (3)

$\mathbf k$ is a wave vector (m⁻¹), $n$ is the band index, and $u$ repeats from cell to cell.

Proof sketch. Q4a asks for the statement and its consequences, not this proof. Know the idea; you need not reproduce it.

  1. Define translation operators, $\hat T_{\mathbf R}\psi(\mathbf r)=\psi(\mathbf r+\mathbf R)$. Because $U$ is periodic, every $\hat T_{\mathbf R}$ commutes with $H$, and the translations commute with each other. Eigenstates of $H$ can therefore be chosen as eigenstates of all of them at once: $\hat T_{\mathbf R}\psi=c(\mathbf R)\,\psi$.
  2. Two translations in a row add up, so $c(\mathbf R+\mathbf R')=c(\mathbf R)\,c(\mathbf R')$. Periodic (Born–von Karman) boundary conditions over $N_i$ cells along $\mathbf a_i$ give $c(\mathbf a_i)^{N_i}=1$, so $c(\mathbf a_i)=e^{2\pi i m_i/N_i}$ with integer $m_i$.
  3. Hence $c(\mathbf R)=e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}$ with $\mathbf k=\sum_i (m_i/N_i)\,\mathbf b_i$, using $\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf b_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}$.
  4. Then $u(\mathbf r)\equiv e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}\psi(\mathbf r)$ gives $u(\mathbf r+\mathbf R)=e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}\,e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}\psi(\mathbf r)=u(\mathbf r)$, so $u$ is periodic.

Kittel proves the same thing on a ring of N atoms (ch. 7, "Bloch Functions"), and again from the central equation below. There $u_{\mathbf k}=\sum_{\mathbf G}C_{\mathbf k-\mathbf G}\,e^{-i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf r}$ is a Fourier series over reciprocal lattice vectors, so it is periodic automatically. The second proof also covers degenerate states.

Saying ψ is lattice-periodic. Only $u$ and $|\psi|^2$ are. $\psi(\mathbf r+\mathbf R)=\psi(\mathbf r)$ holds only when $e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}=1$, e.g. at $\mathbf k=0$.

1D, lattice constant a: a state has ψ(x+a) = −ψ(x). Which k in the first zone?

By (3), $e^{ika}=-1$, so $k=\pi/a$ (the same state as $-\pi/a$). It is the zone-boundary state: the sign alternates from cell to cell.

A Bloch state is a plane wave times a cell-periodic function. Equivalently, a shift by one lattice vector only multiplies ψ by the phase $e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}$.

2.6.1 · Consequences

What Bloch's theorem implies

2.6.1 · Statement and proof

Exam Q4aPractice A
  • k is only defined up to a G. Since $e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf R}=1$, the labels $\mathbf k$ and $\mathbf k+\mathbf G$ give the same phase factor. The first Brillouin zone therefore holds every distinct state: N values of k for N cells, so 2N states per band once spin is counted.
  • k is the phase step from cell to cell, not a momentum. $\hat{\mathbf p}\psi_{n\mathbf k}=\hbar\mathbf k\,\psi_{n\mathbf k}+e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}\,(-i\hbar\nabla u_{n\mathbf k})$. The second term is the crystal's contribution, so $\psi$ is not a momentum eigenstate. $\hbar\mathbf k$ is the crystal momentum, conserved in collisions only up to $\hbar\mathbf G$ (the Umklapp rule of 2.4).
  • ψ is not periodic, but |ψ|² is. At $k=0$ every cell has the same sign. At the zone edge $k=\pi/a$ the sign alternates from cell to cell (Model 2).
  • Bands. Inserting $\psi=u\,e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}$ gives an equation for $u$ on a single cell:
Equation for the periodic part u, on one cell
$$\Big[\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\Big(\frac1i\nabla+\mathbf k\Big)^2+U(\mathbf r)\Big]u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)=\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}\,u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r).$$ (4)

A problem confined to one cell has a discrete ladder of solutions $n=1,2,\dots$ for each $\mathbf k$, and each rung $\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}$ varies smoothly with $\mathbf k$. Each rung is a band.

Notes slip (p52): the handwritten version puts $U(\mathbf r)$ inside the bracket multiplied by $\hbar^2/2m$. $U$ must stand alone, as written here (Kittel ch. 7). Check: for $U=0$, a constant $u$ must give the free electron, $\varepsilon=\hbar^2k^2/2m$. With the extra prefactor the units would not even match.

Calling ħk the electron's momentum. It is crystal momentum: ψ is not a momentum eigenstate, and ħk is conserved only up to ħG.

Why does one band hold exactly 2N electrons in a crystal of N cells?

Born–von Karman boundary conditions allow N distinct k in the first zone, one per cell. The band has one state per k, and spin doubles that to 2N.

k is a phase step per cell, defined only up to a G, so the first Brillouin zone holds every state. For each k there is a discrete ladder of bands $\varepsilon_n(\mathbf k)$.

2.6.2Nearly free electron model

What this covers. Start from free electrons with U → 0 (the empty lattice), see why a weak periodic potential splits the two standing waves at a zone face, write the problem as the central equation, and solve it near the face to get the gap Eg = 2|UG|. The notes stop at the unsolved central equation (p54); the rest is taught here from Kittel ch. 7.

Why it matters. Exam Q4c–f, most of Q4: the [100] sketch with degeneracies, the energies at k₀ and k₁, naming the approximation, and how gaps open. Practice B uses the gap and standing waves. Band gaps are what separate metals from insulators and semiconductors (3.1).

Empty-lattice approximation

2.6.1 Bloch theorem and the reciprocal lattice of fcc (1.3, worked in Q4b)

Let $U\to0$ but keep the lattice's bookkeeping. The states are still plane waves, and Bloch's theorem only relabels them. A free wave is written as $e^{i(\mathbf k-\mathbf G)\cdot\mathbf r}$ with $\mathbf k$ in the first zone:

Empty-lattice bandsExam Q4c
$$\varepsilon_{\mathbf G}(\mathbf k)=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\lvert\mathbf k-\mathbf G\rvert^2,\qquad \mathbf k\in\text{1st BZ}.$$ (5)

Each $\mathbf G$ gives one free parabola, shifted by $\mathbf G$ and folded back into the zone: this is the reduced-zone scheme. Repeating that picture at every $\mathbf G$ gives the periodic zone scheme that the exam asks for. Along [100], $\mathbf k=(k,0,0)$ and

Along [100]Exam Q4c–dExam-relevant derivation
$$\varepsilon=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\big[(k-G_x)^2+G_y^2+G_z^2\big].$$ (6)

Every $\mathbf G$ with the same $G_x$ and the same $G_y^2+G_z^2$ gives the same curve. The number of such $\mathbf G$ is the degeneracy to write on a sketch. Energies below are in units of $E_0=\hbar^2\pi^2/2ma^2$, where $a$ is the cubic lattice constant.

Two ways to lose points in Q4c: leaving degeneracies unlabelled, and drawing bands that "end" at the zone edge. In the periodic zone scheme the picture repeats every G.

sc lattice, k = 0: the second-lowest empty-lattice energy and its degeneracy, in units of E₀?

$4E_0$, sixfold: the six $\mathbf G=\frac{2\pi}a(\pm1,0,0),(0,\pm1,0),(0,0,\pm1)$ all have $\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}|\mathbf G|^2=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{4\pi^2}{a^2}=4E_0$. Compare the table.

With U → 0 the bands are free parabolas (5), one per G, folded into the zone. G's with equal $G_x$ and equal $G_y^2+G_z^2$ give one curve, and their count is its degeneracy.

2.6.2 · Standing waves

Why the cos and sin standing waves split

2.6.2 · Empty lattice

Not yet lectured

In 1D at $k=\pi/a$, the waves $e^{\pm i\pi x/a}$ have the same energy and Bragg-reflect into each other. The eigenstates are the two standing waves

Standing waves at the zone faceExam Q4fPractice B
$$\psi_+\propto\cos\frac{\pi x}{a},\qquad \psi_-\propto\sin\frac{\pi x}{a}.$$ (7)

They have the same kinetic energy but put the electron in different places. $\lvert\psi_+\rvert^2\propto\cos^2(\pi x/a)$ piles charge on the ions at $x=0,a,2a,\dots$, and $\lvert\psi_-\rvert^2\propto\sin^2(\pi x/a)$ piles it between them. With $U(x)=2U_G\cos(2\pi x/a)$ and both states normalised over one cell,

Potential energy of each standing wave
$$\langle\psi_+|U|\psi_+\rangle=+U_G,\qquad \langle\psi_-|U|\psi_-\rangle=-U_G,$$ (8)

which differ by $2\lvert U_G\rvert$: a gap opens between them. The ions attract electrons, so $U$ is most negative at the ions and $U_G<0$. Then $\psi_+$, with its charge on the ions, is the top of band 1, and $\psi_-$ is the bottom of band 2. Reversing the sign of $U_G$ swaps them; try it in Model 8.

The sign of ψ at an atom. A single sign means nothing, because multiplying ψ by −1 (or any phase) changes no measurement. What matters is where |ψ|² piles up, and the relative sign between neighbouring cells, which k sets.

Sanity check: both expectation values were integrated numerically in node and give ±U_G to four digits. Kittel writes the potential as $U\cos(2\pi x/a)$ and finds $E_g=U$. That is the same result, because its Fourier component is $U_G=U/2$.

Assuming the cos state is always lower. It is lower only for attractive ions ($U_G<0$). The sign of $U_G$ decides which standing wave lies below the gap.

If UG > 0, which standing wave is the top of band 1?

$\psi_-\propto\sin(\pi x/a)$. By (8) it now has the lower potential energy, $-U_G$, so it sits below the gap, and $\cos$ becomes the bottom of band 2.

At the zone face the two travelling waves mix into standing waves. The one whose charge sits where U is lowest (on attractive ions) lies 2|UG| below the other.

2.6.2 · Central equation

The central equation

2.6.1 Bloch theorem, 2.6.2 · Empty lattice

Expand both the potential and the Bloch function in plane waves:

Plane-wave expansions
$$U(\mathbf r)=\sum_{\mathbf G}U_{\mathbf G}\,e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf r},\qquad \psi_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)=\sum_{\mathbf G}C_{\mathbf k-\mathbf G}\,e^{i(\mathbf k-\mathbf G)\cdot\mathbf r}.$$ (9)

Insert both into the Schrödinger equation and collect the coefficient of $e^{i(\mathbf k-\mathbf G)\cdot\mathbf r}$. In the notes' notation, $\varepsilon^0_{\mathbf q}=\hbar^2q^2/2m$ is the free kinetic energy:

Central equation (nearly free electrons)Exam Q4e
$$\big(\varepsilon^0_{\mathbf k-\mathbf G}-\varepsilon\big)\,C_{\mathbf k-\mathbf G}+\sum_{\mathbf G'}U_{\mathbf G'-\mathbf G}\,C_{\mathbf k-\mathbf G'}=0 .$$ (10)

There is one equation per $\mathbf G$. Together they form an eigenvalue problem for the matrix $H_{\mathbf G\mathbf G'}=\varepsilon^0_{\mathbf k-\mathbf G}\,\delta_{\mathbf G\mathbf G'}+U_{\mathbf G'-\mathbf G}$, whose eigenvalues at each $\mathbf k$ are the band energies $\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}$. $U_{\mathbf 0}$ is set to 0, since the average potential only shifts every level. Kittel's eq. (27), $(\lambda_k-\varepsilon)C(\mathbf k)+\sum_{\mathbf G}U_{\mathbf G}C(\mathbf k-\mathbf G)=0$, is the same equation after $\mathbf k\to\mathbf k-\mathbf G$. Model 8 solves it numerically with 27–33 plane waves.

Where the notes stop (p54): they reach this equation and do not solve it. Two slips there: the kinetic term is written $-\frac{\hbar}{2m}\nabla$ where it should be $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2$, and the coefficient inside the sum reads like $C_{\mathbf k-\mathbf G}$. It must be $C_{\mathbf k-\mathbf G'}$, otherwise the sum would not couple different waves. The solution below follows Kittel ch. 7.
In (10), which two plane waves does a given Fourier component couple directly?

$U_{\mathbf G'-\mathbf G}$ couples $e^{i(\mathbf k-\mathbf G)\cdot\mathbf r}$ to $e^{i(\mathbf k-\mathbf G')\cdot\mathbf r}$, i.e. waves whose wave vectors differ by $\mathbf G'-\mathbf G$. If that component is zero, the two waves do not mix directly.

In a plane-wave basis the Schrödinger equation becomes a matrix eigenproblem at each k: free energies on the diagonal, Fourier components of U off it. The bands are its eigenvalues.

2.6.2 · Gap

The gap at the zone boundary: 2|UG|

2.6.2 · Standing waves, 2.6.2 · Central equation

Not yet lectured

If $U$ is weak, a plane wave $e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}$ is changed noticeably only where another wave $e^{i(\mathbf k-\mathbf G)\cdot\mathbf r}$ has the same kinetic energy, $\lvert\mathbf k-\mathbf G\rvert=\lvert\mathbf k\rvert$. That is the Bragg condition, and the k's that satisfy it are exactly the Brillouin-zone faces. Keep just those two waves:

Two-wave approximation near a zone faceExam-relevant derivationExam Q4f
$$\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon^0_{\mathbf k}&U_{\mathbf G}\\ U_{\mathbf G}^{*}&\varepsilon^0_{\mathbf k-\mathbf G}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}C_{\mathbf k}\\ C_{\mathbf k-\mathbf G}\end{pmatrix}=\varepsilon\begin{pmatrix}C_{\mathbf k}\\ C_{\mathbf k-\mathbf G}\end{pmatrix}$$ (11)
Its two rootsPractice B
$$\varepsilon_\pm=\tfrac12\big(\varepsilon^0_{\mathbf k}+\varepsilon^0_{\mathbf k-\mathbf G}\big)\pm\sqrt{\tfrac14\big(\varepsilon^0_{\mathbf k}-\varepsilon^0_{\mathbf k-\mathbf G}\big)^2+\lvert U_{\mathbf G}\rvert^2}$$ (12)

On the zone face the two diagonal entries are equal, so $\varepsilon_\pm=\varepsilon^0\pm\lvert U_{\mathbf G}\rvert$. That is a gap of

Band gap, nearly free electronsExam Q4fPractice B
$$E_g=2\lvert U_{\mathbf G}\rvert .$$ (13)

Away from the face, the two waves differ in energy by much more than $U$, and the shift is only second order, $\approx\lvert U_{\mathbf G}\rvert^2/(\varepsilon^0_{\mathbf k}-\varepsilon^0_{\mathbf k-\mathbf G})$, so the free parabola survives. Near the face the bands flatten. Velocity and mass are read off the band's slope and curvature:

Group velocity and effective mass of a bandExam Q4fPractice BPractice C
$$v=\frac1\hbar\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial k},\qquad \frac1{m^*}=\frac1{\hbar^2}\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon}{\partial k^2}.$$ (14)

At the zone face the bands are flat, so $v=0$, and the curvature, hence $m^*$, has opposite signs on the two sides of the gap.

This is an avoided crossing, the same level repulsion as in a 2×2 GOE matrix: a coupling $U$ between two degenerate levels pushes them $2\lvert U\rvert$ apart. Where two free curves cross but $U_{\mathbf G-\mathbf G'}=0$ between them, the crossing stays.

Drawing a gap at every crossing in Q4f. A crossing opens only if the potential couples the two waves ($U_{\mathbf G-\mathbf G'}\neq0$). Uncoupled crossings stay crossings.

1D, U(x) = 2U₁cos(2πx/a) with U₁ = −0.3 eV: gap at k = π/a, and the group velocity at the band edges there?

By (13), $E_g=2|U_1|=0.6$ eV. Both band edges meet the zone face flat, so $v=\hbar^{-1}\partial\varepsilon/\partial k=0$ there.

Where two free waves are degenerate (on a Bragg plane), $U_{\mathbf G}$ mixes them and opens a gap $E_g=2|U_{\mathbf G}|$. The bands meet the zone face flat, with v = 0.

The opposite limit: start from an atomic orbital $\phi(\mathbf r)$ (an s state) on each atom and let it overlap weakly with its neighbours. The combination

Tight-binding Bloch sumPractice C
$$\psi_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)=N^{-1/2}\sum_j e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r_j}\,\phi(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_j)$$ (15)

Nearest-neighbour band energyPractice C
$$\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}=-\alpha-\gamma\sum_{\boldsymbol\rho}e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\rho}.$$ (16)

For sc, with 6 neighbours at distance $a$:

sc s band and its band-bottom massPractice C
$$\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}=-\alpha-2\gamma(\cos k_xa+\cos k_ya+\cos k_za),\qquad m^*=\frac{\hbar^2}{2\gamma a^2}.$$ (17)

  • fcc, 12 neighbours: $\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}=-\alpha-4\gamma\big(\cos\tfrac{k_ya}2\cos\tfrac{k_za}2+\cos\tfrac{k_za}2\cos\tfrac{k_xa}2+\cos\tfrac{k_xa}2\cos\tfrac{k_ya}2\big)$, running from $-\alpha-12\gamma$ at Γ to $-\alpha+4\gamma$ at X, a width of $16\gamma$.

Forgetting spin when filling the band. One electron per atom half-fills an s band (2N states), so the solid is a metal, not an insulator.

Bandwidth $12\gamma=3.0$ eV. From (17), $m^*/m=(\hbar^2/2m)/(\gamma a^2)=3.81/(0.25\times9.0)=1.7$.

Overlapping atomic orbitals spread each level into a cosine band whose width scales with the overlap γ (12γ for sc). Weak overlap means a narrow band and a heavy electron.

2.6Connecting ideas

What this covers. Four questions that tie 2.6.1–2.6.3 together: what k means, how one electron is a wave packet, what the sign of ψ tells you, and why only a partly full band conducts.

Why it matters. These are the conceptual questions behind the models, and the bridge from bands to transport (2.2–2.5) and to semiconductors (3.1).

2.6 · Connecting ideas

Four questions about k, ψ and conduction

2.6.1 · Consequences, 2.6.2 · Gap, 2.6.3 Tight binding

  • What does k mean? The phase turned per cell, $e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}$, multiplying a periodic $u$. Model 2 shows it in real space.
  • One electron or one k? A single-k state fills the whole crystal. An electron that is "here" is a wave packet of k's in a window Δk, and it moves at $v=\hbar^{-1}\partial\varepsilon/\partial k$ (Model 2, packet width).
  • What does the sign of ψ at an atom mean? Only |ψ|² and relative signs matter. In tight binding, the same sign everywhere is the band bottom and alternating signs are the band top. In the nearly-free picture, whether the charge sits on or between the ions decides which side of the gap a state is on (Model 8, right plot).
  • How does a metal conduct? Bloch states are extended even below the barrier tops, because tunnelling repeats coherently in every cell. A perfect lattice does not scatter them, and a current needs a partly filled band (Models 3 and 4).
Why does a completely full band carry no current, even with a field applied?

Every k is occupied, so the field cannot change which states are filled. For each state at k with velocity v there is a filled state at −k with −v, so the total stays zero (Model 3 with 24 of 24).

k is a phase step, a localised electron is a packet moving at $\hbar^{-1}\partial\varepsilon/\partial k$, and only a partly filled band can carry a current.

2.6.3 · Model 1 · finite chain

Atoms → bands

N atoms in a row, each with two orbitals (at −7 eV and −3.5 eV below the vacuum level). Bringing atoms closer lets electrons hop between neighbours, and each orbital splits into N levels. Every level holds two electrons, spin up and spin down.

Try
  • Raise N from 1 to 64 and watch the levels fuse into a band.
  • Shrink the spacing slowly. The upper levels turn from separate dashes into one unbroken line once they rise above the hills between atoms.
  • With 2 electrons per atom, shrink the spacing until the gap closes. The semiconductor becomes a metal.
  • Set 1 electron per atom. The lower band is half full, so it conducts at any spacing.
Why only two bands? Every orbital of the atom (1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, ...) turns into its own band, so a real solid has many bands. The deep ones come from inner orbitals and are always completely full, so they never take part in conduction. Only two bands matter: the highest one holding electrons and the next one above it. That is why the model keeps just these two, here called Band A and Band B. The names on the plot depend on how full each band is. A full band with an empty one above it is the valence band, and the empty band above it is the conduction band. A partly full band is also called a conduction band, because electrons can move in it.
Does an electron in a band need 3 eV to escape? No. The 0 eV line is the energy needed to leave the solid completely, into vacuum. Inside a solid, the neighbouring nuclei pull the hills between atoms down. Once a band sits above those hills (an unbroken line on the left plot), each of its states is spread over the whole row, and the electron already belongs to every atom at once. Electrons in bands below the hills still move between atoms by tunnelling, just more slowly. That tunnelling is why those levels spread into a narrower band too. So "can the electron leave its atom?" is not the real question. The real question is whether the band has empty states left. Models 3 and 4 show why.
2.6.1 · Model 2 · one band, 40 atoms in a ring

What k looks like

Pick a k and see the electron's wave in real space. Every atom carries a copy of its orbital, multiplied by a phase that turns by k·a from one atom to the next. The copper bumps are those copies, pointing up or down with the phase. The blue area is |ψ|², the chance of finding the electron at each atom.

With a packet width of 0 the electron has one exact k and is spread over the whole ring. Widening the packet mixes in nearby k values, which makes a wave packet: a localised electron that travels at the slope of the band. The box under the plots explains how.

Try
  • k = 0: every bump has the same sign. This is the video's bonding state, at the bottom of the band. k = ±π/a: the signs alternate + − + −. That's the antibonding state at the top.
  • Packet width 0.1π/a: at k = 0.5π/a the packet moves fastest, because the band is steepest there. At k = 0 or π/a it doesn't move at all.
  • Packet width 0: the bumps ripple but |ψ|² stays flat. An exact k means no information at all about position.
  • Move k past 0.5π/a and watch the effective mass turn negative: near the top of a band an electron behaves like a hole.
What is a wave packet, and why do we need one? A wave with one exact k is a perfectly regular ripple that runs around the whole crystal, the same everywhere. Its wavelength and momentum are exact, but it has no position: the electron is equally likely to be at every atom. Set the packet width to 0 to see this. But a real electron in a wire can be "roughly here", so it can't be a single k. To get an electron that is roughly here, add several waves with slightly different k. Where their crests line up they add up to a bump, and everywhere else they cancel. That bump is the wave packet, the blue area. Tick "Show the separate waves" to see the ingredients behind it. It works like a group of ocean waves: one lump of wave that travels as a unit. Δk says how many different k values are mixed in; these are the copper dots on the band plot. A small Δk means few ingredients and a long packet spread over many atoms. A large Δk means a short, sharp packet, but its k is less definite. You can't have both at once: packet length × Δk ≈ 1. That's the uncertainty principle. The packet moves at the slope of the band (the group velocity), not at the speed of the ripples inside it. Watch the copper ripples slide through the blue lump at a different speed. The lump is where the electron actually is, so its speed is the electron's speed. The moving electrons in Model 4 are packets like this.
2.6.3 · Model 3 · one band, 24 atoms in a ring

Why a partly full band conducts

Each state in a band is a wave spread over the whole crystal. The states differ in wavelength, labelled by the crystal momentum k = 2π / wavelength. At k = 0 the wave stands still (the bottom of the band), and the sign of k says which way it travels. The atoms form a ring so the row has no ends.

Each state's velocity is the slope of the band at that k. With no voltage the filled states sit symmetrically around k = 0, so every right-mover is cancelled by a left-mover and no current flows. A voltage pushes every electron by the same small step in k. That works only if there are empty states to step into.

Try
  • Half-full band (12 of 24): set the push to 0. Right- and left-movers balance. Raise it and the filled block slides right: current.
  • Full band (24 of 24): every state is taken, so the push changes nothing and the net current stays 0. A full band only conducts if electrons jump the gap to the next band.
  • Look at "Step to nearest empty state". In a real piece of metal it is about a millionth of an eV, so any voltage is enough.
2.6.3 · Model 4 · a wire of 12 × 3 atoms

Current in a wire

Top: the inside of the wire. The grey + circles are the ions of the lattice. Each one is wrapped in its tightly bound Band A electrons (the haze). Copper dots are the electrons that are free to move.

Bottom: the same electrons on an energy diagram like Model 1's. The battery tilts every energy downhill toward the + end.

Free electrons fly around fast in random directions and keep bumping into the vibrating ions (the small flashes). Between bumps the voltage speeds them up a little toward +, so on average they drift that way. That drift is the current.

Try
  • Metal: raise the voltage and watch the current bar grow. Then heat the wire. The ions shake harder, the electrons bump more often, and the current drops. That's why a metal's resistance rises with temperature.
  • Metal at 0 V: the electrons still race around, but the current averages to zero. Random motion alone carries no current.
  • Semiconductor at 300 K: this small piece has no free electrons. Heat it: electrons jump the gap, each one leaving a hole, and the current climbs fast, the opposite of the metal.
  • Insulator: the gap is too large. Even at 900 K nothing moves, whatever the voltage.
How does an electron get past the hills between atoms? It doesn't climb over them. A metal's Band B lies above the hills, and bands below them are still linked by tunnelling, so their states already stretch along the whole wire. The voltage doesn't lift electrons over anything. It only tilts the energies very slightly (in a real wire about 10⁻⁸ eV per atom), which nudges each electron toward + between collisions. A partly full band has empty states right next to the filled ones (Model 3), so even that tiny nudge is enough. The bound electrons in Band A are not drawn as moving dots, because a full band carries no current: every electron moving right is matched by one moving left. It only conducts once heat empties a few of its states, and those empty states move as holes.

2.6.3 · Model 6 · square lattice

Fermi surface in 2D

E(k) = −2t(cos kxa + cos kya) − 4t′ cos kxa cos kya, with t = 1 eV. States with E < EF are filled. Their boundary, here a line, is the Fermi surface. The dashed line is a cut, and the Fermi points kF are where the band crosses EF along it.

Try
  • Low EF: a near-circle, like free electrons.
  • EF = 0 with t′ = 0: a perfect square at half filling, and a spike in the density of states (van Hove singularity).
  • High EF: the filled region wraps around, leaving hole pockets at the corners.
2.6.3 · Model 7 · simple cubic lattice

Fermi surface in 3D

E(k) = −2t(cos kxa + cos kya + (tz/t) cos kza), with t = 1 eV. The copper surface is every k where E(k) = EF, drawn inside the cubic Brillouin zone. The green plane is the cut shown on the right.

Try
  • Slide EF up from −6: sphere, then it touches the zone faces at −2, then an open network, then hole pockets at the corners.
  • Lower tz/t toward 0: the surface becomes a corrugated cylinder, as in layered materials.
  • Move the cut through the necks. Extremal cross-section areas like these are what quantum-oscillation experiments measure.

Empty lattice and nearly free electrons

Free-electron parabolas $\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}|\mathbf k-\mathbf G|^2$, folded into the first Brillouin zone along Γ–X. With UG = 0 this is the empty lattice of exam Q4c–d. A nonzero UG switches on a weak periodic potential. The page then solves the central equation numerically (27 plane waves for fcc, 33 for sc) at every k, and gaps open at the zone boundary.

Simplification: the same UG is used for the two shortest shells of G, and every other UG is 0. Negative UG means the ions attract electrons.

Try
  • Set UG = 0 to get the empty lattice. The ×n labels are the degeneracies, and the readout at Γ and at k = 0.50π/a gives the answer to Q4d.
  • Switch between fcc and sc. The zone edge X moves from 2π/a (bcc reciprocal lattice) to π/a, so k = π/2a goes from a quarter of Γ–X to halfway.

Model notes

Models 1–7 are nearest-neighbour tight-binding models: each electron sits in one orbital per atom and hops to neighbours with amplitude t. Real silicon has many orbitals and an indirect gap, and real copper's Fermi surface is a sphere with necks touching the zone faces of an FCC lattice. The shapes here are the simplest ones that show the same ideas. Wave vectors are in units of 1/a, where a is the lattice constant. Spin is counted as a factor of 2 in every electron number. Model 8 is the opposite limit: free plane waves plus a weak potential. Its lattice constant is aluminium's (4.05 Å, which follows from Kittel's n = 18.06·10²² cm⁻³ with 3 e⁻ per atom and 4 atoms per cube). Its sc option is a hypothetical simple cubic crystal with the same a.

Practice A · theory · about 4 pts

Bloch function or not?

1D lattice with constant a. Which of these are Bloch functions, and with which k in the first zone? (i) $e^{i5\pi x/2a}$; (ii) $\cos(\pi x/a)$; (iii) $\cos(\pi x/a)\,e^{i2\pi x/a}$; (iv) $e^{i\pi x/2a}+e^{i\pi x/a}$. For (ii), what is $\psi(x+a)/\psi(x)$? For (iv), is |ψ|² lattice-periodic?

Method

A function is a Bloch function with label k exactly when all of its plane-wave components differ from k by reciprocal lattice vectors, $G=2\pi n/a$. Reduce k into $(-\pi/a,\pi/a]$ by subtracting a G.

Show worked solution

Steps

  1. (i) One plane wave, $5\pi/2a$. Subtracting $G=2\pi/a$ gives $k=\pi/2a$. Check: $\psi(x+a)=e^{i5\pi/2}\psi(x)=i\,\psi(x)=e^{i\pi/2}\psi(x)$. ✓
  2. (ii) $\cos(\pi x/a)=\tfrac12(e^{i\pi x/a}+e^{-i\pi x/a})$. The two components differ by $2\pi/a=G$, so this is a Bloch function with $k=\pi/a$ (the same as $-\pi/a$). $\psi(x+a)=\cos(\pi x/a+\pi)=-\psi(x)$, i.e. $e^{i\pi}$. It is the zone-boundary standing wave.
  3. (iii) $\tfrac12(e^{i3\pi x/a}+e^{i\pi x/a})$. The components differ by $2\pi/a$, so it is a Bloch function, with $k=\pi/a$ (since $3\pi/a-2\pi/a=\pi/a$).
  4. (iv) The components differ by $\pi/2a$, which is not a reciprocal lattice vector, so this is not a Bloch function. It mixes two different k, like the ingredients of a wave packet. $|\psi|^2=2+2\cos(\pi x/2a)$ has period 4a, not a.
Result: (i) k = π/2a; (ii) k = π/a, ψ(x+a)/ψ(x) = −1; (iii) k = π/a; (iv) not a Bloch function, and |ψ|² is not periodic with a.
Sanity check: for every Bloch case, ψ(x+a)/ψ(x) is a pure phase e^{ika} independent of x. Evaluating it at two different x by hand gives the same value.
Practice B · nearly free electrons · about 8 pts

Gaps and effective masses in a 1D weak potential

1D crystal, a = 3.0 Å, $U(x)=2U_1\cos(2\pi x/a)+2U_2\cos(4\pi x/a)$ with $U_1=-0.40$ eV and $U_2=-0.15$ eV. (a) Free-electron energy at k = π/a. (b) Band edges and gap at π/a, and which standing wave lies below the gap. (c) Where is the second gap, and how large is it? (d) Effective masses just below and just above the first gap.

Method

The Fourier components are $U_{\pm2\pi/a}=U_1$ and $U_{\pm4\pi/a}=U_2$. Each gap opens where two free waves that differ by one of these G's are degenerate. Use the two-wave solution, which is valid because |U| is much smaller than the kinetic energy there.

Show worked solution

Steps

  1. (a) $\lambda=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\big(\frac{\pi}{a}\big)^2=3.810\ \text{eV Å}^2\times(\pi/3.0\ \text{Å})^2=4.18$ eV.
  2. (b) At k = π/a the waves $e^{\pm i\pi x/a}$ differ by $2\pi/a$ and are coupled by $U_1$. The roots are $\varepsilon_\pm=\lambda\pm|U_1|$, i.e. 3.78 eV and 4.58 eV, so the gap is 0.80 eV. Since $U_1<0$, the cos state, with its charge on the ions, lies below the gap.
  3. (c) The next degenerate pair is $e^{\pm i2\pi x/a}$ at k = 0 (the same as k = 2π/a), with kinetic energy $\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(2\pi/a)^2=16.7$ eV. They differ by $4\pi/a$, so $U_2$ couples them, and the gap between bands 2 and 3 is $2|U_2|=0.30$ eV, at the zone centre.
  4. (d) Write $k=\pi/a+\tilde K$. Expanding the two-wave root for small $\tilde K$ gives $\varepsilon\approx\lambda\pm|U|+\frac{\hbar^2\tilde K^2}{2m}\big(1\pm\frac{2\lambda}{|U|}\big)$ (Kittel ch. 7, eq. 51–52). Hence $m^*/m=1/(1\pm2\lambda/|U_1|)$, with $2\lambda/|U_1|=20.9$.
Result: (a) 4.18 eV; (b) band edges 3.78 and 4.58 eV, gap 0.80 eV, cos state below; (c) at k = 0, about 16.7 eV, gap 0.30 eV; (d) m* = −0.050 m just below the gap (top of band 1, behaves like a hole) and m* = +0.046 m just above it.
Sanity check: a finite-difference second derivative of the exact two-wave formula in node gives the same masses. A full 15-plane-wave diagonalisation of the central equation gives gaps of 0.81 and 0.28 eV; the small differences are second-order mixing with other waves.
Practice C · tight binding (from Kittel ch. 9) · about 8 pts

An s band on a simple cubic lattice

sc lattice, a = 3.0 Å, one s orbital per atom, nearest-neighbour overlap γ = 0.50 eV. (a) Show that $\psi_{\mathbf k}=N^{-1/2}\sum_j e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r_j}\phi(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_j)$ is a Bloch function. (b) Give ε(k) and the bandwidth. (c) Effective mass at the band bottom. (d) Electron velocity at k = (π/2a, 0, 0). (e) With one valence electron per atom: metal or insulator?

Method

Use Kittel's first-order energy $\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}=-\alpha-\gamma\sum_{\boldsymbol\rho}e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\rho}$ over the 6 nearest neighbours. The velocity is $\hbar^{-1}\partial\varepsilon/\partial k$, and the effective mass comes from the curvature at k = 0.

Show worked solution

Steps

  1. (a) $\psi_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf r+\mathbf T)=N^{-1/2}\sum_j e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r_j}\phi(\mathbf r-(\mathbf r_j-\mathbf T))$. Relabel $\mathbf r_j-\mathbf T\to\mathbf r_j$ (still a lattice point); this pulls out $e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf T}$, which is Bloch's condition.
  2. (b) $\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}=-\alpha-2\gamma(\cos k_xa+\cos k_ya+\cos k_za)$, from $-\alpha-6\gamma$ to $-\alpha+6\gamma$. The bandwidth is $12\gamma=6.0$ eV.
  3. (c) $\varepsilon\approx-\alpha-6\gamma+\gamma a^2k^2$, so $m^*=\hbar^2/2\gamma a^2$ and $m^*/m=(\hbar^2/2m)/(\gamma a^2)=3.810/(0.50\times9.0)=0.85$.
  4. (d) $v_x=\frac1\hbar\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial k_x}=\frac{2\gamma a}{\hbar}\sin k_xa=\frac{2(0.50\ \text{eV})(3.0\times10^{-10}\ \text{m})}{6.582\times10^{-16}\ \text{eV s}}\times1=4.6\times10^{5}$ m/s. This is the band's steepest point.
  5. (e) The band holds 2N states and gets N electrons, so it is half full: a metal. The band is symmetric about $-\alpha$, so εF = −α.
Result: (b) bandwidth 6.0 eV; (c) m* = 0.85 m; (d) v = 4.6·10⁵ m/s along +x; (e) half-filled band, metal, εF = −α.
Sanity check: Model 7 (t = γ = 1 eV) shows a band range of ±6 eV, i.e. 12γ. Model 2 quotes 760 km/s at the steepest point for a = 2.5 Å and t = 1 eV; scaling by γa gives 760 × (0.5 × 3.0)/(1 × 2.5) = 456 km/s, which matches (d).

Electrons in condensed matter

Electrons in a periodic potential: (a) Bloch theorem; (b) reciprocal lattice vectors; (c) free-electron band structure along [100]; (d) the first two energies at k₀ = (0,0,0) and k₁ = (π/2a,0,0); then, with a small periodic potential: (e) which approximation; (f) sketch En(k) and compare.

If the intended lattice was simple cubic, the sc values are given alongside. "Compare it to b)" in (f) presumably means (c), the empty-lattice sketch.
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. (a) Eigenstates in a periodic potential have the form $\psi_{n\mathbf k}=u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}$ with $u$ lattice-periodic; equivalently $\psi(\mathbf r+\mathbf R)=e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}\psi(\mathbf r)$. Consequences: k is defined modulo G, so the first BZ suffices; for each k there is a discrete ladder of bands $\varepsilon_n(\mathbf k)$; ħk is crystal momentum, not momentum; |ψ|² is periodic but ψ is not; and $v=\hbar^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf k}\varepsilon_n$.
  2. (b) fcc primitive vectors: $\mathbf a_1=\frac a2(0,1,1)$, $\mathbf a_2=\frac a2(1,0,1)$, $\mathbf a_3=\frac a2(1,1,0)$. From $\mathbf b_1=2\pi\,\frac{\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3}{\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)}$ and cyclic permutations,
