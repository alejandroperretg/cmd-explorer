Overview

Electrons in a periodic potential: why allowed energies form bands separated by gaps, and how the filling of the highest band decides whether a solid is a metal, semiconductor or insulator. The models below use the tight-binding picture (syllabus 2.6.3). Bloch's theorem (2.6.1) and the nearly-free-electron / empty-lattice picture (2.6.2) are still to be added.

Read alongside Kittel, Introduction to Solid State Physics 8th ed., ch. 7 (Energy Bands) and ch. 9 (Fermi Surfaces and Metals), and the lecture notes pp. 50–54.

Atoms → bands

N atoms in a row, each with two orbitals (at −7 eV and −3.5 eV below the vacuum level). Bringing atoms closer lets electrons hop between neighbours, and each orbital splits into N levels. Every level holds two electrons, spin up and spin down.

Try
  • Raise N from 1 to 64 and watch the levels fuse into a band.
  • Shrink the spacing slowly. The upper levels turn from separate dashes into one unbroken line once they rise above the hills between atoms.
  • With 2 electrons per atom, shrink the spacing until the gap closes. The semiconductor becomes a metal.
  • Set 1 electron per atom. The lower band is half full, so it conducts at any spacing.
Why only two bands? Every orbital of the atom (1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, ...) turns into its own band, so a real solid has many bands. The deep ones come from inner orbitals and are always completely full, so they never take part in conduction. Only two bands matter: the highest one holding electrons and the next one above it. That is why the model keeps just these two, here called Band A and Band B. The names on the plot depend on how full each band is. A full band with an empty one above it is the valence band, and the empty band above it is the conduction band. A partly full band is also called a conduction band, because electrons can move in it.
Does an electron in a band need 3 eV to escape? No. The 0 eV line is the energy needed to leave the solid completely, into vacuum. Inside a solid, the neighbouring nuclei pull the hills between atoms down. Once a band sits above those hills (an unbroken line on the left plot), each of its states is spread over the whole row, and the electron already belongs to every atom at once. Electrons in bands below the hills still move between atoms by tunnelling, just more slowly. That tunnelling is why those levels spread into a narrower band too. So "can the electron leave its atom?" is not the real question. The real question is whether the band has empty states left. Models 3 and 4 show why.
Model 2 · one band, 40 atoms in a ring

What k looks like

Pick a k and see the electron's wave in real space. Every atom carries a copy of its orbital, multiplied by a phase that turns by k·a from one atom to the next. The copper bumps are those copies, pointing up or down with the phase. The blue area is |ψ|², the chance of finding the electron at each atom.

With a packet width of 0 the electron has one exact k and is spread over the whole ring. Widening the packet mixes in nearby k values, which makes a wave packet: a localised electron that travels at the slope of the band. The box under the plots explains how.

Try
  • k = 0: every bump has the same sign. This is the video's bonding state, at the bottom of the band. k = ±π/a: the signs alternate + − + −. That's the antibonding state at the top.
  • Packet width 0.1π/a: at k = 0.5π/a the packet moves fastest, because the band is steepest there. At k = 0 or π/a it doesn't move at all.
  • Packet width 0: the bumps ripple but |ψ|² stays flat. An exact k means no information at all about position.
  • Move k past 0.5π/a and watch the effective mass turn negative: near the top of a band an electron behaves like a hole.
What is a wave packet, and why do we need one? A wave with one exact k is a perfectly regular ripple that runs around the whole crystal, the same everywhere. Its wavelength and momentum are exact, but it has no position: the electron is equally likely to be at every atom. Set the packet width to 0 to see this. But a real electron in a wire can be "roughly here", so it can't be a single k. To get an electron that is roughly here, add several waves with slightly different k. Where their crests line up they add up to a bump, and everywhere else they cancel. That bump is the wave packet, the blue area. Tick "Show the separate waves" to see the ingredients behind it. It works like a group of ocean waves: one lump of wave that travels as a unit. Δk says how many different k values are mixed in; these are the copper dots on the band plot. A small Δk means few ingredients and a long packet spread over many atoms. A large Δk means a short, sharp packet, but its k is less definite. You can't have both at once: packet length × Δk ≈ 1. That's the uncertainty principle. The packet moves at the slope of the band (the group velocity), not at the speed of the ripples inside it. Watch the copper ripples slide through the blue lump at a different speed. The lump is where the electron actually is, so its speed is the electron's speed. The moving electrons in Model 4 are packets like this.
Model 3 · one band, 24 atoms in a ring

Why a partly full band conducts

Each state in a band is a wave spread over the whole crystal. The states differ in wavelength, labelled by the crystal momentum k = 2π / wavelength. At k = 0 the wave stands still (the bottom of the band), and the sign of k says which way it travels. The atoms form a ring so the row has no ends.

Each state's velocity is the slope of the band at that k. With no voltage the filled states sit symmetrically around k = 0, so every right-mover is cancelled by a left-mover and no current flows. A voltage pushes every electron by the same small step in k. That works only if there are empty states to step into.

Try
  • Half-full band (12 of 24): set the push to 0. Right- and left-movers balance. Raise it and the filled block slides right: current.
  • Full band (24 of 24): every state is taken, so the push changes nothing and the net current stays 0. A full band only conducts if electrons jump the gap to the next band.
  • Look at "Step to nearest empty state". In a real piece of metal it is about a millionth of an eV, so any voltage is enough.
Model 4 · a wire of 12 × 3 atoms

Current in a wire

Top: the inside of the wire. The grey + circles are the ions of the lattice. Each one is wrapped in its tightly bound Band A electrons (the haze). Copper dots are the electrons that are free to move.

Bottom: the same electrons on an energy diagram like Model 1's. The battery tilts every energy downhill toward the + end.

Free electrons fly around fast in random directions and keep bumping into the vibrating ions (the small flashes). Between bumps the voltage speeds them up a little toward +, so on average they drift that way. That drift is the current.

Try
  • Metal: raise the voltage and watch the current bar grow. Then heat the wire. The ions shake harder, the electrons bump more often, and the current drops. That's why a metal's resistance rises with temperature.
  • Metal at 0 V: the electrons still race around, but the current averages to zero. Random motion alone carries no current.
  • Semiconductor at 300 K: this small piece has no free electrons. Heat it: electrons jump the gap, each one leaving a hole, and the current climbs fast, the opposite of the metal.
  • Insulator: the gap is too large. Even at 900 K nothing moves, whatever the voltage.
How does an electron get past the hills between atoms? It doesn't climb over them. A metal's Band B lies above the hills, and bands below them are still linked by tunnelling, so their states already stretch along the whole wire. The voltage doesn't lift electrons over anything. It only tilts the energies very slightly (in a real wire about 10⁻⁸ eV per atom), which nudges each electron toward + between collisions. A partly full band has empty states right next to the filled ones (Model 3), so even that tiny nudge is enough. The bound electrons in Band A are not drawn as moving dots, because a full band carries no current: every electron moving right is matched by one moving left. It only conducts once heat empties a few of its states, and those empty states move as holes.
Model 5 · periodic chain, two atoms per cell

Band structure E(k) and Fermi–Dirac filling

The same kind of E(k) plot as Model 3, now with two bands. This chain alternates two atom types whose energies differ by Δ, which opens a gap Eg = Δ at the zone edge. Dots show each state's occupation f(E) = 1 / (e(E−μ)/kT + 1).

Try
  • At 300 K and Eg = 1.1 eV (silicon-like), note the excited electrons, then add 100 K.
  • Set Eg to 0. The bands touch and it is a metal.
  • Set electrons per cell to 2.02. The extra electrons sit in the conduction band at any temperature, which is what n-type doping does.
Model 6 · square lattice

Fermi surface in 2D

E(k) = −2t(cos kxa + cos kya) − 4t′ cos kxa cos kya, with t = 1 eV. States with E < EF are filled. Their boundary, here a line, is the Fermi surface. The dashed line is a cut, and the Fermi points kF are where the band crosses EF along it.

Try
  • Low EF: a near-circle, like free electrons.
  • EF = 0 with t′ = 0: a perfect square at half filling, and a spike in the density of states (van Hove singularity).
  • High EF: the filled region wraps around, leaving hole pockets at the corners.
Model 7 · simple cubic lattice

Fermi surface in 3D

E(k) = −2t(cos kxa + cos kya + (tz/t) cos kza), with t = 1 eV. The copper surface is every k where E(k) = EF, drawn inside the cubic Brillouin zone. The green plane is the cut shown on the right.

Try
  • Slide EF up from −6: sphere, then it touches the zone faces at −2, then an open network, then hole pockets at the corners.
  • Lower tz/t toward 0: the surface becomes a corrugated cylinder, as in layered materials.
  • Move the cut through the necks. Extremal cross-section areas like these are what quantum-oscillation experiments measure.

Model notes

All seven are nearest-neighbour tight-binding models: each electron sits in one orbital per atom and hops to neighbours with amplitude t. Real silicon has many orbitals and an indirect gap, and real copper's Fermi surface is a sphere with necks touching the zone faces of an FCC lattice. The shapes here are the simplest ones that show the same ideas. Wave vectors are in units of 1/a, where a is the lattice constant. Spin is counted as a factor of 2 in every electron number.
