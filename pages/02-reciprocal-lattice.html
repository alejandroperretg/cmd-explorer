Reciprocal lattice, diffraction and imaging

A crystal is a periodic arrangement, and a wave that hits it only scatters strongly in directions set by the Fourier transform of that arrangement: the reciprocal lattice. The same lattice of wave vectors defines the Brillouin zone, which later organises phonons and electron bands.

Overview

Two ways to see atoms. Direct imaging (1.1.3) uses electrons: a TEM needs a de Broglie wavelength far below the atomic spacing, and an STM uses the exponential distance dependence of a tunnelling current. Diffraction (1.3) gives up the picture and measures its Fourier transform instead. Each scattered beam is labelled by a reciprocal lattice vector $\mathbf G$, and its strength by the structure factor of the basis.

Key results to leave with:

  • Bragg's law $2d\sin\theta = n\lambda$, and its vector form, the Laue condition $\Delta\mathbf k=\mathbf G$.
  • $\mathbf b_i\cdot\mathbf a_j = 2\pi\delta_{ij}$ and $V_{BZ}=(2\pi)^3/V_c$.
  • The reciprocal of fcc is bcc, and the reciprocal of bcc is fcc.
  • Brillouin-zone faces are exactly the wave vectors that Bragg-reflect.
  • $S_{\mathbf G}=\sum_j f_j e^{-i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf r_j}$ decides which reflections vanish.
  • The form factor $f$ is the Fourier transform of one atom's electron cloud.

Lattices and primitive vectors are on topic 01; this page starts from them. The reciprocal lattice and the Brillouin zone are used again for phonons (topic 05), free electrons (topic 07) and bands (topic 09). Read alongside Kittel, Introduction to Solid State Physics 8th ed., ch. 2 (Bragg law, Fourier analysis, reciprocal lattice, diffraction conditions, Laue equations, Brillouin zones, Fourier analysis of the basis), ch. 1 "Direct Imaging of Atomic Structure", and the lecture notes pp. 7–15.

Concepts

1.1.3Direct imaging of atomic structure

Where you meet this. In 1989 Don Eigler's group at IBM pushed 35 xenon atoms around a nickel surface with an STM tip and spelled "IBM". The STM's inventors, Binnig and Rohrer, had received the 1986 Nobel Prize in Physics. The exponential current law below is why one atom at the tip apex does all the work.

What this covers. How a TEM and an STM reach atomic resolution: the electron's de Broglie wavelength, and the tunnelling current's exponential dependence on the gap (notes pp. 7–9, where it is called "1.2").

Why it matters. Sheet 2.3 asks for both working principles and two numbers. It also motivates diffraction: imaging needs wavelengths below the atomic spacing, and so does Bragg's law.

1.1.3 · TEM and STM

TEM and STM

← from 1.1.1 Lattice and basis

TEM. Electrons accelerated through a voltage $U$ have the de Broglie wavelength

Electron wavelength (non-relativistic)Sheet 2.3
$$\begin{aligned}\lambda&=\frac hp,\\ p&=\sqrt{2m_e eU}.\end{aligned}$$ (1)
Relativistic momentum (TEM voltages)Sheet 2.3c
$$p=\sqrt{2m_eeU\Big(1+\frac{eU}{2m_ec^2}\Big)}.$$ (2)

At 250 kV this gives about 2 pm, a hundred times smaller than the spacing of atoms. Magnetic lenses image the electrons that pass through a thin sample. In practice lens aberrations, not λ, limit the resolution, but it still reaches atomic columns.

STM. A sharp metal tip is held a few atomic diameters above a conducting surface. Electrons tunnel through the vacuum gap $d$, and the current falls off as

STM tunnelling currentSheet 2.3b
$$\begin{aligned}I&\propto e^{-2\kappa d},\\ \kappa&=\frac{\sqrt{2m_e\phi}}{\hbar},\end{aligned}$$ (3)

where $\phi$ is the work function (the barrier height, in J) and $m_e$ the electron mass. With $\phi\approx 3$ eV the current changes by a factor of about 6 per 0.1 nm, so the foremost atom of the tip carries almost all of it. A feedback loop moves the tip to keep $I$ constant, and the tip height traced over the surface is the image.

Course sources differ: the notes write $I\propto e^{-\alpha x}$ and the sheet $e^{-2\kappa d}$, so $\alpha=2\kappa$. The notes list "TEM, SEM" as the two imaging options, but an SEM does not reach atomic resolution; sheet 2.3 and Kittel pair TEM with STM.

Forgetting the relativistic correction at TEM voltages. At 250 kV, $eU/2m_ec^2=0.245$, so (1) overestimates λ by about 10 %.

At 250 kV, what are λ from (1) and from (2)?

(1): 2.45 pm. (2): $p$ is larger by $\sqrt{1.245}=1.116$, so λ = 2.20 pm (node).

With φ = 3 eV, by what factor does I change when the gap shrinks by 0.1 nm?

$\kappa=8.87\times10^{9}$ m⁻¹, so $e^{2\kappa\cdot0.1\,\text{nm}}=e^{1.77}=5.9$.

TEM: λ = h/p must be far below the atomic spacing, so use (2) at high voltage. STM: $I\propto e^{-2\kappa d}$ changes about 6× per 0.1 nm, so the tip apex atom images the surface.

You can now explain how TEM and STM reach atomic resolution and compute the electron wavelength and the current ratio. That is Sheet 2.3.

1.3.1Bragg's law

Where you meet this. In 1913 W. H. and W. L. Bragg used this law on rock salt and found that NaCl contains no molecules: every Na is surrounded by six Cl, and every Cl by six Na. Every powder diffractometer in a materials lab still runs on it, usually with copper Kα X-rays at 154 pm.

What this covers. Reflection from stacks of lattice planes, the glancing angle, and why the wavelength must be at most twice the plane spacing (notes p. 10).

Why it matters. It says where reflections can appear. The reciprocal lattice (1.3.2) and the Laue condition (1.3.3) restate it, and the Brillouin-zone faces are its planes.

1.3.1 · Bragg's law

Bragg's law

Recap from 1.1.2. A lattice plane family (hkl) is a stack of equally spaced, parallel planes that contains every lattice point.

Diffraction is a wave effect, so the wavelength has to be comparable to the spacing of atoms. Treat the crystal as a stack of parallel atomic planes with spacing $d$. Each plane reflects a little of the beam like a weak mirror. Reflections from neighbouring planes add up in phase only when their path difference $2d\sin\theta$ is a whole number of wavelengths:

Bragg's law (n = 1, 2, 3, …)Exercise (d)
$$2d\sin\theta=n\lambda.$$ (4)

Here θ is the glancing angle between the beam and the planes. The beam is deflected by 2θ, and a θ–2θ diffractometer plots intensity against 2θ. A solution needs $\lambda\le 2d$, which is why X-rays (photons, $\lambda=hc/E$) of about 0.1 nm, or electrons and neutrons of similar wavelength, are used. Visible light cannot diffract from a crystal lattice.

Bragg's law says where reflections can appear. It says nothing about their strength, which comes from the basis (1.3.5).

Using the deflection angle as θ. θ is measured from the planes, not from the incoming beam's normal, and the detector sits at 2θ.

Cu Kα (154.06 pm) on the (100) planes of polonium (d = 334 pm): first-order Bragg angle?

$\sin\theta=154.06/668=0.231$, so θ = 13.33° and the deflection is 26.67°. That is the angle Model 2 opens on.

Reflection needs $2d\sin\theta=n\lambda$ (4) with θ the glancing angle, so only waves with $\lambda\le2d$ diffract.

You can now find where a plane family reflects for a given wavelength, and say why X-rays, electrons and neutrons work but light does not. That is part (d) of the copper exercise.

1.3.2Reciprocal lattice

Where you meet this. Switch a TEM from image mode to diffraction mode and the screen shows a regular array of spots: a plane of the reciprocal lattice, magnified. Materials scientists read crystal orientation and lattice constants straight off it.

What this covers. Fourier series of lattice-periodic functions, the vectors $\mathbf G$ that qualify, the reciprocal primitive vectors $\mathbf b_i$, the reciprocal cell volume, and the reciprocal lattices of sc, bcc and fcc (notes p. 11).

Why it matters. Sheets 2.1 and 2.2 compute $\mathbf b_i$ and volumes, and exam Q4b asks for the fcc reciprocal vectors. Phonons, free electrons and Bloch bands all label their states by $\mathbf k$ inside the reciprocal lattice's cell.

1.3.2 · Reciprocal lattice

Reciprocal lattice

← from 1.1.1 Primitive cell, 1.1.2 Cubic lattices

Recap from 1.1.1. Lattice vectors are $\mathbf R=n_1\mathbf a_1+n_2\mathbf a_2+n_3\mathbf a_3$ with integer $n_i$, and the primitive cell has volume $V_c=|\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)|$. fcc: $V_c=a^3/4$; bcc: $a^3/2$.

Anything periodic in the crystal, such as the electron density, can be written as a Fourier series:

Fourier series of a lattice-periodic function
$$n(\mathbf r)=\sum_{\mathbf G}n_{\mathbf G}\,e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf r}.$$ (5)

Only plane waves $e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf r}$ that take the same value at every lattice point qualify:

Definition of the reciprocal lattice
$$\begin{aligned}e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf R}&=1\ \text{for every }\mathbf R\\ \Leftrightarrow\ \mathbf G\cdot\mathbf R&=2\pi\cdot\text{integer}.\end{aligned}$$ (6)

These $\mathbf G$ form a lattice of their own, $\mathbf G=v_1\mathbf b_1+v_2\mathbf b_2+v_3\mathbf b_3$ with integer $v_i$, spanned by

Reciprocal primitive vectorsExam Q4bSheet 2.1Sheet 2.2b
$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf b_1&=2\pi\,\frac{\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3}{\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)},\\ \mathbf b_2&=2\pi\,\frac{\mathbf a_3\times\mathbf a_1}{\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)},\\ \mathbf b_3&=2\pi\,\frac{\mathbf a_1\times\mathbf a_2}{\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)}.\end{aligned}$$ (7)
What (7) is built to satisfy
$$\mathbf b_i\cdot\mathbf a_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}.$$ (8)

$\mathbf b_i$ has units of 1/m. Each $\mathbf G$ is normal to a family of lattice planes, and the spacing of those planes is $d=2\pi/|\mathbf G|$ for the shortest $\mathbf G$ in that direction. Scaling the crystal by a factor λ scales the reciprocal lattice by 1/λ. The cell volume of the reciprocal lattice is

Reciprocal cell (Brillouin-zone) volumeSheet 2.1Sheet 2.2c
$$V_{BZ}=\frac{(2\pi)^3}{V_c}.$$ (9)
Lattice (cube a)Reciprocal latticeShortest G1st Brillouin zone
scsc, cube 2π/a6 × (2π/a)(1,0,0)cube
bccfcc, cube 4π/a12 × (2π/a)(1,1,0)rhombic dodecahedron
fccbcc, cube 4π/a8 × (2π/a)(1,1,1)truncated octahedron

The notes close 1.3.2 by naming the Wigner–Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice the first Brillouin zone; its physics comes after the scattering argument, in 1.3.3 · Brillouin zones.

$|\mathbf b_i|=2\pi/|\mathbf a_i|$ is wrong in general. Some tutoring boards box this formula. It holds only when $\mathbf a_i$ is perpendicular to the other two primitive vectors (sc, tetragonal, orthorhombic). Take fcc, with $\mathbf a_1=\tfrac a2(0,1,1)$, $\mathbf a_2=\tfrac a2(1,0,1)$, $\mathbf a_3=\tfrac a2(1,1,0)$ (Kittel):
  • $|\mathbf a_1|=a/\sqrt2$, so the shortcut predicts $|\mathbf b_1|=\sqrt2\cdot 2\pi/a=1.414\cdot2\pi/a$.
  • The formula above gives $\mathbf b_1=\tfrac{2\pi}a(-1,1,1)$, so $|\mathbf b_1|=\sqrt3\cdot2\pi/a=1.732\cdot2\pi/a$.
  • The shortcut is 18 % too short (the true value is $\sqrt{3/2}=1.22$ times larger). bcc fails by the same factor: the shortcut gives $1.155\cdot2\pi/a$, the true value is $1.414\cdot2\pi/a$.
The correct general statement is $|\mathbf b_1|=2\pi/d$, where $d$ is the spacing of the planes spanned by $\mathbf a_2$ and $\mathbf a_3$. For fcc these are the (111) planes, $d=a/\sqrt3$. Model 3 shows both numbers side by side.
What is the reciprocal lattice of sc with cube edge a?

sc with edge $2\pi/a$: (7) gives $\mathbf b_i=\tfrac{2\pi}a\hat{\mathbf e}_i$, and $V_{BZ}=(2\pi/a)^3=(2\pi)^3/V_c$ as in (9).

Used in 1.6.1 What k means → (k is only defined up to a G), 2.1.3 Counting → (k-space counting), 2.4.2 Umklapp →, 2.6.1 Bloch theorem → and exam Q4b →.

The reciprocal lattice is the set of $\mathbf G$ with $e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf R}=1$ (6), spanned by (7). fcc ↔ bcc, and $V_{BZ}=(2\pi)^3/V_c$.

You can now construct the reciprocal lattice of any lattice, name it for sc, bcc and fcc, and compute its cell volume. That is Sheet 2.1, Sheet 2.2b and Exam Q4b.

1.3.3X-ray scattering

Where you meet this. Most of the more than 200 000 structures in the Protein Data Bank, from haemoglobin to the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, were solved by X-ray diffraction from crystals. Every one of those analyses starts from the Laue condition below.

What this covers. The scattered amplitude as a Fourier integral of the electron density, the Laue condition $\Delta\mathbf k=\mathbf G$, its equivalence to Bragg's law, the Ewald construction, and the Brillouin zone as the set of Bragg planes (notes pp. 12–15, the last as "1.3.4").

Why it matters. It is the bridge from 1.3.1 to 1.3.5: the structure factor is the cell integral left over here. The Brillouin zone (sheet 2.2, exam Q4c–d) organises phonons and electron bands.

1.3.3 · Laue condition

X-ray scattering and the Laue condition

1.3.2 Reciprocal lattice; 1.3.1 Bragg's law

Each volume element of the crystal scatters the incoming wave $e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}$ with an amplitude proportional to the local electron density. The wave leaves towards the detector with wave vector $\mathbf k'$. The path to and from a point $\mathbf r$ adds the phase factor $e^{-i\Delta\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}$ with $\Delta\mathbf k=\mathbf k'-\mathbf k$. Summing over the crystal (Kittel's sign):

Scattering amplitude
$$\begin{aligned}F&=\int n(\mathbf r)\,e^{-i\Delta\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}\,dV\\&=\sum_{\mathbf G}n_{\mathbf G}\\&\quad\times\int e^{i(\mathbf G-\Delta\mathbf k)\cdot\mathbf r}\,dV.\end{aligned}$$ (10)

For a large crystal the integral is negligible unless

Laue conditionSheet 3.2
$$\Delta\mathbf k=\mathbf G.$$ (11)

Equivalently, writing $\mathbf r=\mathbf R+\boldsymbol\rho$ splits $F$ into a lattice sum $\sum_{\mathbf R}e^{-i\Delta\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}$ times one cell integral, and the lattice sum only adds up when every phase is 1. Measured intensity is $|F|^2$.

Elastic scattering, $|\mathbf k'|=|\mathbf k|$, then gives Kittel's two steps:

Elastic diffraction condition
$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf k'&=\mathbf k+\mathbf G\\ \Rightarrow\ (\mathbf k+\mathbf G)^2&=k^2\\ \Rightarrow\ 2\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf G+G^2&=0\\ \xrightarrow{\ \mathbf G\to-\mathbf G\ }\ 2\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf G&=G^2.\end{aligned}$$ (12)

The last step is allowed because $-\mathbf G$ is also a reciprocal lattice vector. With $|\mathbf G|=2\pi n/d$ and $\mathbf k\cdot\hat{\mathbf G}=k\sin\theta$ this becomes $2d\sin\theta=n\lambda$, which is Bragg's law. The same condition, dotted with each $\mathbf a_i$, gives the Laue equations $\mathbf a_i\cdot\Delta\mathbf k=2\pi v_i$.

Ewald construction. Draw $\mathbf k$ ending on the reciprocal-lattice origin, and a sphere of radius $k$ around its tail. Every reciprocal lattice point on the sphere gives a diffracted beam $\mathbf k'$, pointing from the centre to that point. This sphere is the "where is the detector" picture: the detector sits along $\mathbf k'$ (Model 2). A white beam, with a range of $k$, makes many spheres and therefore many spots at once (Laue method).

Notes vs Kittel: the notes (p. 14) state that $\mathbf K=\mathbf k'-\mathbf k$ must equal $\mathbf G$, but then substitute $\mathbf k'=-\mathbf G+\mathbf k$. The final condition is the same; this page follows Kittel's order above. The notes also flip the sign of the exponent between pp. 13 and 14. That flip changes nothing in $|F|^2$, but structure-factor phases need a fixed sign, and this page uses Kittel's $e^{-i\Delta\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}$ throughout. Symbols: the notes and sheet 3.2 call the scattering vector $\mathbf K$, Kittel calls it $\Delta\mathbf k$, and the tutoring boards call it $\mathbf q$.

Reading (11) as "every G gives a beam". The Laue condition and elastic scattering (12) must hold together, so a fixed crystal in a monochromatic beam usually shows no reflection at all: the Ewald sphere misses every point.

How do powder and Laue methods get reflections anyway?

Powder: grains at every orientation, so for each G some grains satisfy (12). Laue: a white beam has a range of $k$, i.e. many Ewald spheres, so many points are hit at once.

Scattering measures the Fourier transform of the density; it is nonzero only at $\Delta\mathbf k=\mathbf G$ (11). With $|\mathbf k'|=|\mathbf k|$ this is $2\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf G=G^2$ (12), Bragg's law in vector form.

1.3.3 · Brillouin zones (notes "1.3.4")

Brillouin zones

1.3.3 Laue condition; ← from 1.1.1 Wigner–Seitz cell

The first Brillouin zone is the Wigner–Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice. Draw the vectors from the origin to its nearest reciprocal lattice points and cut each one in half with a perpendicular plane; the smallest region enclosed by those planes is the zone. A wave vector $\mathbf k$ lies on the bisector plane of $\mathbf G$ when

Zone face = Bragg planeExam Q4c–dSheet 2.2b
$$\mathbf k\cdot\big(\tfrac12\mathbf G\big)=\big(\tfrac12\mathbf G\big)^2.$$ (13)

This is the elastic diffraction condition (12), divided by 4. So the zone faces are exactly the wave vectors that are Bragg-reflected. A wave with $\mathbf k$ on a face is reflected into $\mathbf k-\mathbf G$ and becomes a standing wave. That standing wave gives zero group velocity for phonons (topic 05) and the band gap for electrons (topic 09). Higher zones are the regions reached by crossing one, two, … more bisector planes; each has the same volume as the first, (9).

Giving the fcc Brillouin zone as a rhombic dodecahedron. That is the Wigner–Seitz cell of the fcc real lattice. The reciprocal lattice of fcc is bcc, so the zone is the bcc Wigner–Seitz cell, a truncated octahedron.

For fcc, where does the zone boundary along [100] sit?

The shortest G along [100] is $\tfrac{2\pi}a(2,0,0)$, so the face is at half of it: the X point, $\tfrac{2\pi}a(1,0,0)$ (exam Q4c–d).

Used in 1.6.1 Dispersion → (dispersion over the first zone, zero group velocity at its edge), 1.7.1.3 Debye model → (cut-off sphere holding one zone of modes) and 2.6.2 Standing waves and gaps →.

First Brillouin zone = Wigner–Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice; its faces (13) are the Bragg planes, where waves become standing waves.

You can now derive Bragg's law from the Laue condition, use the Ewald construction, and draw and size a Brillouin zone. That is Sheet 2.2 and the zone-boundary step of Exam Q4c–d.

1.3.5Analysis with basis

Where you meet this. Silicon powder is a standard calibration sample for X-ray diffractometers. Its pattern shows (111), (220), (311) and (400), but no (200) peak, although (200) planes certainly exist. The structure factor of the diamond basis cancels it.

What this covers. The structure factor of a basis, the atomic form factor, and the extinction rules of bcc, fcc and diamond (Kittel ch. 2, "Fourier analysis of the basis"; not in the notes).

Why it matters. Sheet 3.1 is the diamond structure factor and sheet 3.2 the hydrogen form factor. The extinction rules decide which peaks a diffraction pattern shows.

1.3.5 · Structure and form factors

Structure and form factors

1.3.3 Laue condition; ← from 1.1.5 Simple crystal structures

Recap from 1.1.5. A crystal is a lattice plus a basis; atom $j$ of the basis sits at $\mathbf r_j=x_j\mathbf a_1+y_j\mathbf a_2+z_j\mathbf a_3$. Diamond, for example, is fcc with atoms at 000 and ¼¼¼.

Write the cell's electron density as a sum over its atoms $j$. At $\Delta\mathbf k=\mathbf G$ the amplitude is $F=N\,S_{\mathbf G}$ ($N$ cells), with the structure factor

Structure factor of the basisSheet 3.1
$$\begin{aligned}S_{\mathbf G}&=\sum_j f_j\,e^{-i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf r_j},\\ \mathbf G\cdot\mathbf r_j&=2\pi(v_1x_j+v_2y_j\\&\qquad+v_3z_j),\end{aligned}$$ (14)

and the atomic form factor, the Fourier transform of one atom's electron density $n_j$:

Atomic form factor (second line: spherical atom)Sheet 3.2
$$\begin{aligned}f_j&=\int n_j(\boldsymbol\rho)\,e^{-i\mathbf G\cdot\boldsymbol\rho}\,dV\\&=\int_0^\infty 4\pi\rho^2\,n_j(\rho)\\&\qquad\times\frac{\sin G\rho}{G\rho}\,d\rho.\end{aligned}$$ (15)

$f_j(0)=Z$, the number of electrons, and $f_j$ falls off once $G$ exceeds about one over the atom's radius. A compact atom gives a wide transform.

Extinction rules, with $(hkl)$ indexed on the conventional cube:

  • bcc, basis 000, ½½½: $S=f\,[1+e^{-i\pi(h+k+l)}]$, so the reflection is absent when $h+k+l$ is odd.
  • fcc, basis 000, 0½½, ½0½, ½½0: the reflection is present only when $h,k,l$ are all even or all odd.
  • diamond: the fcc rule, and also absent when all indices are even with $h+k+l\equiv2\pmod 4$ (sheet 3.1).

Concluding from a missing reflection that the G is missing. The reciprocal lattice point is there; the basis makes $S_{\mathbf G}=0$.

bcc metal, conventional-cube indices: is the (100) reflection seen? And (110)?

(100): $h+k+l=1$ is odd, so $S=f(1-1)=0$, absent. (110): $h+k+l=2$, $S=2f$, present.

Where reflections can appear is set by the lattice (G); how strong they are is set by the basis through $S_{\mathbf G}$ (14) and the form factors (15).

You can now compute a structure factor, derive extinction rules, and compute a form factor from an electron density. That is Sheet 3.1, Sheet 3.2 and part (d) of the copper exercise.

1.3.3 · X-ray scattering · Model 4

Adding up the scattered phases

A chain of N cells of length a, each holding two atoms: atom 1 at the cell origin and atom 2 a fraction u further along, scattering f₂/f₁ times as strongly. Each atom contributes a unit phasor $e^{-iqx_j}$ scaled by its f. The intensity is the squared length of their sum. Exact for this 1D crystal; no form-factor fall-off.

Try
  • Drag q to 1.000: the phasors line up into a straight line, which is the Laue condition q = G. At 1.05 they curl up and nearly cancel.
  • Increase N: the peaks get taller (∝ N²) and narrower (first zero at q = G ± 1/N). A big crystal gives sharp spots.
  • Set u = 0.50 and f₂/f₁ = 1: the odd peaks vanish. The chain is now simply periodic with a/2, and the structure factor kills every G that the new lattice lacks.

Reciprocal lattice vectors

Prove that the reciprocal primitive vectors satisfy $\mathbf b_1\cdot(\mathbf b_2\times\mathbf b_3)=(2\pi)^3/\big(\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)\big)$. Hint: write $\mathbf b_1$ (but not $\mathbf b_2$ or $\mathbf b_3$) in terms of the $\mathbf a_i$.

Method

Follow the hint: replace only $\mathbf b_1$ by its definition. That leaves a dot product of two cross products, $(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)\cdot(\mathbf b_2\times\mathbf b_3)$. The identity $(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)\cdot(\mathbf C\times\mathbf D)=(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf C)(\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf D)-(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf D)(\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf C)$ reduces it to products $\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf b_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}$.

Show worked solution

Steps

  1. Let $V=\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)$ (signed cell volume). Then $\mathbf b_1=\dfrac{2\pi}{V}\,\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3$, so
    $$\begin{aligned}&\mathbf b_1\cdot(\mathbf b_2\times\mathbf b_3)\\&=\frac{2\pi}{V}\,(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)\\&\quad\cdot(\mathbf b_2\times\mathbf b_3).\end{aligned}$$
  2. Apply the identity with $\mathbf A=\mathbf a_2$, $\mathbf B=\mathbf a_3$, $\mathbf C=\mathbf b_2$, $\mathbf D=\mathbf b_3$:
    $$\begin{aligned}&(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)\cdot(\mathbf b_2\times\mathbf b_3)\\&=(\mathbf a_2\cdot\mathbf b_2)(\mathbf a_3\cdot\mathbf b_3)\\&\quad-(\mathbf a_2\cdot\mathbf b_3)(\mathbf a_3\cdot\mathbf b_2)\\&=(2\pi)(2\pi)-0\cdot0.\end{aligned}$$
  3. Hence $\mathbf b_1\cdot(\mathbf b_2\times\mathbf b_3)=\dfrac{2\pi}{V}(2\pi)^2=\dfrac{(2\pi)^3}{\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)}$. ∎
  4. Alternative route (Kittel ch. 2 Problem 3; tutoring board): write all three $\mathbf b_i$ in terms of the $\mathbf a_i$ and use $(\mathbf c\times\mathbf a)\times(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)=\big(\mathbf c\cdot(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)\big)\,\mathbf a$. This gives $\mathbf b_2\times\mathbf b_3=\frac{(2\pi)^2}{V^2}(\mathbf a_3\times\mathbf a_1)\times(\mathbf a_1\times\mathbf a_2)=\frac{(2\pi)^2}{V}\mathbf a_1$, and then $\mathbf b_1\cdot\frac{(2\pi)^2}{V}\mathbf a_1=\frac{(2\pi)^3}{V}$.
Result: $V_{BZ}=\mathbf b_1\cdot(\mathbf b_2\times\mathbf b_3)=(2\pi)^3/V_c$. The Brillouin zone volume is $(2\pi)^3$ divided by the primitive cell volume.
Sanity check: sc: $\mathbf b_i=\frac{2\pi}a\hat{\mathbf x}_i$ gives $(2\pi/a)^3=(2\pi)^3/a^3$ ✓. Units: m⁻³ ✓. For bcc and fcc the product $V_c V_{BZ}/(2\pi)^3$ evaluates to 1.000 in node (Model 3 readout).

Brillouin zone

(a) Volume of the Wigner–Seitz cells of Cr (bcc, a = 288.5 pm) and Cu (fcc, a = 361.5 pm). (b) Their reciprocal lattice vectors, and the Brillouin zones. (c) The "Inverse Volumina" of the Brillouin zones (unit 1/m³).

Method

A Wigner–Seitz cell is a primitive cell, so its volume is $V_c$: the cube volume divided by the lattice points per cube (2 for bcc, 4 for fcc). Get $\mathbf b_i$ from the cross-product formula with Kittel's primitive vectors. The BZ is the Wigner–Seitz cell of the result, and its volume follows from sheet 2.1. Do not use $|\mathbf b_i|=2\pi/|\mathbf a_i|$: these axes are not orthogonal.

Assumption: "Inverse Volumina" is read as the BZ volume in k-space, $V_{BZ}=(2\pi)^3/V_c$. The stated unit 1/m³ fits this; $1/V_c$ (number of atoms per m³) would also have that unit and is given as a side value.
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. (a) Cr: $V_c=a^3/2=(288.5\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm m)^3/2=1.201\times10^{-29}$ m³. Cu: $V_c=a^3/4=(361.5\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm m)^3/4=1.181\times10^{-29}$ m³.
  2. (b) Cr, bcc. $\mathbf a_1=\tfrac a2(-1,1,1)$, $\mathbf a_2=\tfrac a2(1,-1,1)$, $\mathbf a_3=\tfrac a2(1,1,-1)$. Then $\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3=\tfrac{a^2}2(0,1,1)$ and $V_c=a^3/2$, so
    $$\begin{aligned}\mathbf b_1&=\tfrac{2\pi}a(0,1,1),\\ \mathbf b_2&=\tfrac{2\pi}a(1,0,1),\\ \mathbf b_3&=\tfrac{2\pi}a(1,1,0),\\ \tfrac{2\pi}a&=2.178\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}.\end{aligned}$$
    These are fcc primitive vectors for a cube of edge 4π/a, so the reciprocal lattice is fcc. $|\mathbf b_i|=\sqrt2\cdot2\pi/a=3.080\times10^{10}$ m⁻¹. The shortcut $2\pi/|\mathbf a_i|=2.515\times10^{10}$ m⁻¹ would be wrong.
  3. The BZ of Cr is bounded by the bisectors of the 12 shortest $\mathbf G=\tfrac{2\pi}a(\pm1,\pm1,0)$ (all permutations), at a distance $|\mathbf G|/2=1.540\times10^{10}$ m⁻¹ from Γ. It is a rhombic dodecahedron, with 12 rhombic faces. Its corners lie at $\tfrac{2\pi}a(1,0,0)$ (point H, $2.178\times10^{10}$ m⁻¹) and at $\tfrac{\pi}a(1,1,1)$ (point P).
  4. (b) Cu, fcc. $\mathbf a_1=\tfrac a2(0,1,1)$, $\mathbf a_2=\tfrac a2(1,0,1)$, $\mathbf a_3=\tfrac a2(1,1,0)$. Then $\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3=\tfrac{a^2}4(-1,1,1)$ and $V_c=a^3/4$, so
    $$\begin{aligned}\mathbf b_1&=\tfrac{2\pi}a(-1,1,1),\\ \mathbf b_2&=\tfrac{2\pi}a(1,-1,1),\\ \mathbf b_3&=\tfrac{2\pi}a(1,1,-1),\\ \tfrac{2\pi}a&=1.738\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}.\end{aligned}$$
    These are bcc primitive vectors for a cube of edge 4π/a, so the reciprocal lattice is bcc. $|\mathbf b_i|=\sqrt3\cdot2\pi/a=3.010\times10^{10}$ m⁻¹; the shortcut $2\pi/|\mathbf a_i|=2.458\times10^{10}$ m⁻¹ is 18 % short.
  5. The BZ of Cu has two kinds of face:
    • 8 hexagons bisect $\mathbf G=\tfrac{2\pi}a(\pm1,\pm1,\pm1)$, at $\sqrt3\pi/a=1.505\times10^{10}$ m⁻¹ (point L).
    • 6 squares bisect $\tfrac{2\pi}a(\pm2,0,0)$, at $2\pi/a=1.738\times10^{10}$ m⁻¹ (point X).
    It is a truncated octahedron with 14 faces. The hexagon planes alone would close into an octahedron, and the squares cut off its corners.
  6. (c) $V_{BZ}=(2\pi)^3/V_c$: Cr $248.05/1.201\times10^{-29}=2.066\times10^{31}$ m⁻³; Cu $248.05/1.181\times10^{-29}=2.100\times10^{31}$ m⁻³.
Result: $V_{WS}$: Cr $1.201\times10^{-29}$ m³, Cu $1.181\times10^{-29}$ m³. Reciprocal lattices: Cr → fcc, $|\mathbf b|=3.080\times10^{10}$ m⁻¹, BZ rhombic dodecahedron; Cu → bcc, $|\mathbf b|=3.010\times10^{10}$ m⁻¹, BZ truncated octahedron. $V_{BZ}$: Cr $2.066\times10^{31}$ m⁻³, Cu $2.100\times10^{31}$ m⁻³.
Sanity check:
  • $1/V_c$ for Cu is $8.47\times10^{28}$ m⁻³, Kittel's atom density of copper ✓.
  • The smaller cell (Cu) has the larger zone ✓.
  • Polyhedron volumes, from the vertex sets used in Model 3, checked in node:
    • truncated octahedron with vertices at the permutations of $\tfrac{2\pi}a(0,\pm\tfrac12,\pm1)$: $4(2\pi/a)^3=(2\pi)^3/(a^3/4)$ ✓;
    • rhombic dodecahedron with vertices $\tfrac{2\pi}a(\pm1,0,0)$ and $\tfrac{\pi}a(\pm1,\pm1,\pm1)$: $2(2\pi/a)^3=(2\pi)^3/(a^3/2)$ ✓.
  • $\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf b_j/2\pi$ evaluates to the identity matrix in node for both sets ✓.
Sheet 2 · Problem 3 · 4 pts

Atomic resolution

(a) Working principles of STM and TEM, and how each reaches atomic resolution. (b) $I\propto\exp(-2\kappa d)$, $\kappa=(2m\phi)^{0.5}/\hbar$, with $d$ the tip–sample distance, $m$ the electron mass and $\phi$ the work function (~3 eV): (i) estimate κ; (ii) by what factor does the current increase if the tip moves 0.2 nm closer? (c) TEM electrons accelerated through 250 kV: (i) their de Broglie wavelength; (ii) compare with the resolution of visible light (λ ~ 500 nm).

Method

(a) is the 1.1.3 card above. (b) Convert φ to joules and evaluate κ. Only the exponential depends on d, so the ratio is $e^{2\kappa\Delta d}$. (c) $\lambda=h/p$ with the kinetic energy $eU$. The notes use the non-relativistic $p=\sqrt{2m_eeU}$. At 250 keV the electron moves at 0.74 c, so give the relativistic value too.

Assumption: φ = 3 eV exactly, free-electron mass $m_e$, and the prefactor of $I$ does not change over 0.2 nm. The electrons start at rest. For the light microscope, the Abbe limit $d_{min}\approx\lambda/(2\,\mathrm{NA})$ with numerical aperture NA ≤ 1 (standard optics, not on the sheet).
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. (a) STM:
    • A bias voltage between a sharp tip and a conducting surface drives a tunnelling current across a vacuum gap a few atoms wide.
    • A piezo scanner moves the tip across the surface, and feedback adjusts its height to hold $I$ constant. The recorded height map is the image.
    • Atomic resolution comes from the exponential: about 6× per 0.1 nm (below), so almost all the current flows through the single foremost atom of the tip. Height changes of a few pm are visible.
    TEM:
    • Electrons are accelerated to hundreds of keV and focused by condenser lenses onto a sample thin enough to transmit them.
    • An objective lens and projector lenses form a magnified image, or the diffraction pattern, on a detector.
    • Atomic resolution needs a wavelength well below the atomic spacing, and the electrons have pm wavelengths (c). Lens aberrations, not λ, set the practical limit.
  2. (b)(i) $\phi=3\times1.602\times10^{-19}$ J $=4.81\times10^{-19}$ J.
    $$\begin{aligned}m_e&=9.109\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg},\\ \phi&=4.81\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J},\\ \hbar&=1.0546\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s},\\ \kappa&=\sqrt{2m_e\phi}\,/\,\hbar\\&=8.87\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\\&=8.87\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}.\end{aligned}$$
  3. (b)(ii) $I(d-\Delta d)/I(d)=e^{2\kappa\Delta d}=e^{2\cdot8.87\cdot0.2}=e^{3.55}=34.8$.
  4. (c)(i) $eU=250$ keV $=4.005\times10^{-14}$ J.
    • Non-relativistic: $\lambda=h/\sqrt{2m_eeU}=2.45$ pm.
    • The correction factor is $eU/2m_ec^2=0.245$, which is not small. Relativistic: $\lambda=h/\sqrt{2m_eeU(1+eU/2m_ec^2)}=2.20$ pm, 10 % shorter.
  5. (c)(ii) 500 nm / 2.20 pm ≈ $2.3\times10^5$. A light microscope resolves at best about λ/2 ≈ 250 nm, roughly a thousand atomic spacings, so atoms are out of reach. Electrons are not limited by wavelength at all; their limit comes from the lenses.
Result: κ ≈ 8.9 nm⁻¹; the current increases about 35-fold. λ = 2.45 pm (non-relativistic) or 2.20 pm (relativistic), about 2 × 10⁵ times shorter than visible light.
Sanity check: $\hbar^2\kappa^2/2m_e$ evaluates back to 3.000 eV (node). The electron rule λ(Å) ≈ 12/√E(eV) (Kittel ch. 2 Fig. 1: 1.2 Å at 100 eV) gives 0.024 Å = 2.4 pm at 250 keV, matching the non-relativistic value. Model 1 shows the same numbers live.

Structure factor of diamond

Described as a simple cubic Bravais lattice, diamond has eight atoms as basis. (i) Find the structure factor S of this basis. (ii) Find the zeros of S and show that the allowed diffraction conditions satisfy $\nu_1+\nu_2+\nu_3=4n$ with $n$ an integer and all $\nu_i$ even, or all $\nu_i$ odd ($\nu_i$ = Bragg plane indices).

Method

Diamond is fcc plus the same fcc shifted by ¼ of the body diagonal. On the sc cube with $\mathbf G=\frac{2\pi}a(\nu_1,\nu_2,\nu_3)$, the 8 positions are the 4 fcc sites and the same 4 plus (¼,¼,¼). The sum over the 8 sites therefore factorises into an fcc bracket times a two-site bracket. Find the zeros of each.

Assumption: all eight atoms are the same element with the same form factor f (carbon), and the $\nu_i$ are indices on the conventional cube (Kittel ch. 2 Problem 5, which this problem reproduces).
Show worked solution

Steps

  1. Basis, in units of a: $(0,0,0),(0,\tfrac12,\tfrac12),(\tfrac12,0,\tfrac12),(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0)$ and the same four plus $(\tfrac14,\tfrac14,\tfrac14)$.
  2. (i) $S=f\sum_j e^{-i2\pi(\nu_1x_j+\nu_2y_j+\nu_3z_j)}$ factorises:
    $$\begin{aligned}S=f\,\big[&1+e^{-i\pi(\nu_2+\nu_3)}\\&+e^{-i\pi(\nu_1+\nu_3)}\\&+e^{-i\pi(\nu_1+\nu_2)}\big]\\ \times\big[&1\\&+e^{-i\pi(\nu_1+\nu_2+\nu_3)/2}\big].\end{aligned}$$
  3. fcc bracket. Each term is ±1.
    • All $\nu_i$ even or all odd: every pair sum is even and the bracket is 4.
    • Mixed parity: exactly two of the three pair sums are odd, and the bracket is $1+1-1-1=0$.
  4. Shift bracket. With $s=\nu_1+\nu_2+\nu_3$, the bracket $1+e^{-i\pi s/2}$ takes these values:
    s mod 4bracketsize
    022
    11 − i√2
    200
    31 + i√2
  5. (ii) Zeros: (1) mixed parity, from the fcc bracket; (2) all even with $s=4n+2$, from the shift bracket. Everything else is allowed:
    • all odd: $|S|=4\sqrt2 f$;
    • all even with $\nu_1+\nu_2+\nu_3=4n$: $|S|=8f$.
    This is the stated condition. All-odd indices always have an odd sum, so the "$=4n$" applies to the all-even case.
Result: $S=4f\,[1+e^{-i\pi(\nu_1+\nu_2+\nu_3)/2}]$ when the indices are unmixed, otherwise 0. Allowed: all odd (e.g. 111, 311), or all even with sum $4n$ (220, 400). Forbidden: mixed parity (100, 110, 210), and all even with sum $4n+2$ (200, 222, 420).
Sanity check: a direct 8-term sum in node for all $h\ge k\ge l$ up to 444 gives $|S|/f=5.66=4\sqrt2$ for 111, 311, 331, 333; 8.00 for 220, 400, 422, 440, 444; and 0 (below $10^{-14}$) for all others. At $\nu=0$ the formula gives $|S|=8f$, one f per atom ✓. The (200) reflection of Si is indeed missing in Model 5.

Form factor of atomic hydrogen

The electron density of hydrogen is $n(r)=|\psi(r)|^2$ with the ground state $\psi(r)=\exp(-r/a_0)/\sqrt{\pi a_0^3}$ and Bohr radius $a_0$. Show that the form factor is $f_K=16/(4+K^2a_0^2)^2$, with $K$ the magnitude of the scattering vector.

Method

$f_K=\int n(r)\,e^{-i\mathbf K\cdot\mathbf r}\,dV$. Use spherical coordinates with $\mathbf K$ along the polar axis, so $\mathbf K\cdot\mathbf r=Kr\cos\theta$. The angular integral gives $4\pi\,\sin(Kr)/(Kr)$, and the radial integral is a standard Laplace transform.

Show worked solution

Steps

  1. Angular part: $\int_0^{2\pi}\!d\varphi\int_0^\pi e^{-iKr\cos\theta}\sin\theta\,d\theta=2\pi\int_{-1}^{1}e^{-iKrx}dx=4\pi\,\dfrac{\sin Kr}{Kr}$.
  2. So, with $\beta=2/a_0$,
    $$\begin{aligned}f_K&=\int_0^\infty 4\pi r^2\,\frac{e^{-2r/a_0}}{\pi a_0^3}\\&\qquad\times\frac{\sin Kr}{Kr}\,dr\\&=\frac{4}{Ka_0^3}\\&\quad\times\int_0^\infty r\,e^{-\beta r}\sin Kr\,dr.\end{aligned}$$
  3. The remaining radial integral:
    $$\begin{aligned}&\int_0^\infty r\,e^{-\beta r}\sin Kr\,dr\\&=\mathrm{Im}\int_0^\infty r\,e^{-(\beta-iK)r}\,dr\\&=\mathrm{Im}\frac{1}{(\beta-iK)^2}\\&=\mathrm{Im}\frac{(\beta+iK)^2}{(\beta^2+K^2)^2}\\&=\frac{2\beta K}{(\beta^2+K^2)^2}.\end{aligned}$$
  4. Insert $\beta=2/a_0$: $f_K=\dfrac{4}{Ka_0^3}\cdot\dfrac{4K/a_0}{(4/a_0^2+K^2)^2}=\dfrac{16/a_0^4}{(4+K^2a_0^2)^2/a_0^4}=\dfrac{16}{(4+K^2a_0^2)^2}$. ∎
Result: $f_K=16/(4+K^2a_0^2)^2$. It equals 1 at $K=0$, falls to ½ at $Ka_0=1.29$, and decays as $16/(Ka_0)^4$ for large $K$.
Sanity check: $f(0)=\int n\,dV=1$, one electron ✓. A numerical integral in node gives 0.64000, 0.25000, 0.040000 and 0.0034602 at $Ka_0$ = 1, 2, 4, 8, equal to the formula to 5 digits. Model 6 repeats this numerical check in the page.

Data not on any sheet: Cu Kα₁ wavelength λ = 154.06 pm, the standard laboratory X-ray line.
Show worked solution
  1. (a) With $\mathbf a_1=\tfrac a2(0,1,1)$ etc.: $\mathbf b_1=\tfrac{2\pi}a(-1,1,1)$, $\mathbf b_2=\tfrac{2\pi}a(1,-1,1)$, $\mathbf b_3=\tfrac{2\pi}a(1,1,-1)$, with $2\pi/a=1.738\times10^{10}$ m⁻¹. The reciprocal lattice is bcc, cube edge 4π/a.
